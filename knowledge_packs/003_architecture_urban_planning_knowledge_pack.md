## 1. Purpose

This pack encodes the structured knowledge base for Architecture and Urban Planning — the disciplines governing the design, construction, and organization of the built environment at scales from individual buildings to cities and regions. It provides deterministic, queryable knowledge for reasoning about architectural history, structural and environmental systems, building performance, urban theory, land use, transportation planning, and sustainable design.

## 4. Core Concepts

**C01 — The Classical Orders**
Definition: The five classical orders of Greco-Roman architecture — Doric (simple, sturdy), Ionic (scrolled capitals, more ornate), Corinthian (acanthus leaf capitals, most ornate), Tuscan (simplified Doric), and Composite (combining Ionic and Corinthian) — define a system of proportional rules for columns, entablatures, and pediments that governed Western architecture from antiquity through the Neoclassical period.
Key relationships: Vitruvius (De Architectura, ~25 BCE), proportional systems, entasis (subtle column taper), Palladianism, Neoclassicism.

**C02 — Architectural Periods and Movements**
Definition: Major Western movements: Ancient (Egypt, Greece, Rome), Byzantine (domes, mosaics), Romanesque (round arches, thick walls, ~1000–1200), Gothic (pointed arches, flying buttresses, large windows, ~1100–1500), Renaissance (classical revival, symmetry, ~1400–1600), Baroque (dynamic forms, grandeur, ~1600–1750), Neoclassicism (~1750–1900), Arts and Crafts (~1850–1920), Art Nouveau (~1890–1910), Modernism (~1900–1970), Postmodernism (~1970–present), Contemporary/Digital (~2000–present).
Key relationships: Cultural and technological drivers of each period; key architects and buildings.

**C03 — Modernism in Architecture**
Definition: The early 20th-century movement rejecting historical ornament and embracing functionalism, industrial materials (steel, glass, reinforced concrete), and social reform. Key figures: Le Corbusier (Five Points of Architecture), Walter Gropius (Bauhaus), Ludwig Mies van der Rohe (universal space), Frank Lloyd Wright (organic architecture).
Key relationships: International Style, CIAM (Congrès Internationaux d'Architecture Moderne), functionalism ("form follows function," Sullivan), Bauhaus school, prefabrication.

**C04 — Structural Loads and Systems**
Definition: Buildings must resist: dead loads (self-weight), live loads (occupancy), wind loads, seismic loads, and snow loads. Structural systems: bearing wall (masonry, timber), post-and-beam (timber, steel, concrete), rigid frame, shear wall, core and outrigger (tall buildings), truss, space frame, shell/vault/dome, tensile structures, suspension.
Key relationships: Load path (how forces travel to foundations), redundancy, moment, shear, axial force, factor of safety, serviceability (deflection limits).

**C05 — Reinforced Concrete**
Definition: Composite structural material combining concrete's compressive strength with steel reinforcement bars (rebar) to provide tensile capacity. Allows complex three-dimensional forms. Post-tensioned and pre-tensioned concrete extends span capacity. Invented in 19th century; became dominant structural material by mid-20th century.
Key relationships: Concrete mix design (w/c ratio, admixtures), cover requirements (corrosion protection), carbonation, rebar sizing and placement, reinforced concrete frames (RC frames).

**C06 — Steel Construction**
Definition: Structural steel offers high strength-to-weight ratio, ductility (seismic performance), and long spans. Wide-flange sections (W-shapes), hollow structural sections (HSS), and plate girders are common. Steel connections: bolted, welded, or pin. Fire protection (spray-on or intumescent coating) is required.
Key relationships: Moment frame vs. braced frame, buckling, composite steel-concrete deck, pre-engineered metal buildings, weathering steel (Cor-Ten), AISC standards.

**C07 — Building Envelope**
Definition: The physical separator between the conditioned (controlled) interior and the exterior environment: foundations, walls, windows/doors, and roof. Controls heat, moisture, air, light, and sound transfer. Critical for building energy performance and occupant comfort.
Key relationships: Thermal bridging, vapor barrier, air barrier, fenestration (window-to-wall ratio), U-value (overall heat transfer coefficient), SHGC (solar heat gain coefficient), continuous insulation.

**C08 — Passive Design Strategies**
Definition: Design strategies using building form, orientation, and material properties to reduce energy demand without mechanical systems: solar orientation (south-facing in NH), natural ventilation, thermal mass, daylighting, shading devices, earth sheltering, wind catchers.
Key relationships: Solar altitude and azimuth, overhanging eaves, thermal lag, cross-ventilation, stack effect, climate-responsive design, vernacular architecture.

**C09 — HVAC Systems in Buildings**
Definition: Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning systems maintain thermal comfort and indoor air quality. Types: central air handling units (AHUs), variable air volume (VAV) systems, heat pumps, chilled beam, fan coil units, radiant heating/cooling, dedicated outdoor air systems (DOAS). Biggest energy consumer in most commercial buildings.
Key relationships: Psychrometrics, enthalpy, ASHRAE 90.1 (energy code), ASHRAE 55 (thermal comfort), COP (coefficient of performance), SEER (seasonal energy efficiency ratio), economizer modes.

**C10 — Acoustics in Architecture**
Definition: Control of sound in buildings includes room acoustics (reverberation time T60, speech intelligibility, music performance), noise isolation (STC — Sound Transmission Class, IIC — Impact Insulation Class), and mechanical noise control. Different spaces require different acoustic environments.
Key relationships: Reverberation, absorption, reflection, diffusion, background noise (NC curves), flanking paths, mass-air-mass principle, acoustic panels.

**C11 — Daylighting Design**
Definition: Strategic use of natural light to reduce artificial lighting energy use and improve occupant wellbeing. Metrics: illuminance (lux), Useful Daylight Illuminance (UDI), Daylight Autonomy (DA), Annual Sunlight Exposure (ASE). Design elements: toplighting (skylights, clerestories), sidelighting (windows), light shelves, atria.
Key relationships: Sun path analysis, glare control, glazing specification (VLT, SHGC), energy savings from daylight-linked controls, circadian lighting.

**C12 — Building Energy Performance Metrics**
Definition: Energy Use Intensity (EUI, kBtu/sf/yr or kWh/m²/yr) is the primary metric for building energy performance. Zero-energy buildings (ZEB): produce as much energy as consumed annually on-site. Nearly Zero Energy Buildings (NZEB): EU standard requiring very high efficiency plus renewable energy.
Key relationships: ASHRAE 90.1, International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), energy modeling (EnergyPlus, eQUEST), ENERGY STAR score, benchmarking, building commissioning.

**C13 — Green Building Certification**
Definition: Third-party certification systems evaluating building sustainability. LEED (Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design, US Green Building Council): points-based system across energy, water, materials, IEQ, location. BREEAM (UK). Passive House (energy performance standard). WELL (health/wellness focus). Living Building Challenge (regenerative standard).
Key relationships: Prerequisites vs. credits, certification levels (Certified, Silver, Gold, Platinum), lifecycle assessment (LCA), embodied carbon vs. operational carbon.

**C14 — Building Information Modeling (BIM)**
Definition: Digital representation of physical and functional characteristics of a building, enabling integrated design, documentation, coordination, and lifecycle management. BIM platforms: Autodesk Revit, ArchiCAD, Bentley AECOsim. BIM maturity levels (0–3D): Level 2 (3D coordinated models) is standard in UK public sector; Level 3 (integrated data environment) is emerging.
Key relationships: IFC (Industry Foundation Classes, open standard), clash detection, 4D BIM (construction scheduling), 5D BIM (cost), 6D BIM (sustainability), digital twin.

**C15 — Urban Form and City Structure**
Definition: Cities exhibit recurring spatial structures. Concentric zone model (Burgess, 1925): CBD → transition zone → working-class housing → residential → commuter zone. Sector model (Hoyt): pie-shaped sectors along transport routes. Multiple nuclei (Harris, Ullman): multiple activity centers. Contemporary: polycentric, network cities.
Key relationships: Central business district (CBD), urban sprawl, density gradient, edge cities, TOD (transit-oriented development).

**C16 — Land Use Planning and Zoning**
Definition: Zoning regulates land use by dividing jurisdictions into zones with specified permitted uses, density, setbacks, height limits, and parking requirements. Euclidean zoning (use separation, US standard) vs. form-based codes (regulating physical form rather than use) vs. performance zoning (outcome-based).
Key relationships: Comprehensive plan (general plan), zoning ordinance, variances and conditional use permits, exclusionary zoning (minimum lot size, single-family requirements), inclusionary zoning, mixed-use zoning.

**C17 — Transit-Oriented Development (TOD)**
Definition: High-density, mixed-use, walkable development centered on transit stations, reducing car dependency. TOD principles: short walk (400–800m catchment), mixed uses, quality public space, parking managed not maximized, affordable housing inclusion.
Key relationships: TOD typology (gateway, urban, neighborhood), station area planning, value capture (transit-induced land value increase), BART TOD (San Francisco Bay Area), Curitiba BRT model.

**C18 — Housing Policy and Affordability**
Definition: Housing affordability is conventionally measured as: affordable if housing costs ≤30% of gross household income. Affordability gap driven by: supply constraints (zoning, regulations, construction costs), demand pressures (population growth, income inequality), and financialization of housing. Policy responses: inclusionary zoning, public housing, vouchers (Section 8/HCV), rent stabilization, housing trust funds, upzoning.
Key relationships: AMI (Area Median Income), cost-burdened (>30% income on housing), severely cost-burdened (>50%), YIMBY/NIMBY dynamics, missing middle housing, social housing.

**C19 — Historic Preservation**
Definition: Protection and management of historic buildings, districts, landscapes, and archaeological sites. National Register of Historic Places (US): listing confers federal tax credits for rehabilitation. Secretary of the Interior's Standards for Rehabilitation: guidelines for compatible alterations. Historic preservation easements, heritage conservation areas.
Key relationships: Adaptive reuse, significance criteria (architectural, historical, cultural, archaeological), Secretary of Interior Standards, HABS/HAER documentation, UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

**C20 — Universal Design and Accessibility**
Definition: Design of environments and products usable by all people, regardless of ability. ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act, 1990) establishes minimum accessibility standards for facilities serving the public: ramp slopes, door widths, restroom clearances, parking stalls.
Key relationships: ADAAG (ADA Accessibility Guidelines), accessible routes, reach ranges, contrast ratios, tactile ground surface indicators, universal design principles (seven: equitable use, flexibility, simple use, perceptible information, tolerance for error, low physical effort, size/space for approach).

**C21 — Urban Transportation Planning**
Definition: Planning multimodal transportation systems — streets, pedestrian networks, cycling, transit, freight — to provide mobility while managing demand, safety, and environmental impact. Four-step travel demand model (trip generation → distribution → mode split → assignment). Newer approaches: activity-based modeling, agent-based simulation.
Key relationships: Level of service (LOS, A–F), Vehicle Miles Traveled (VMT), mode share, Vision Zero (zero traffic fatalities), complete streets, induced demand.

**C22 — Public Space Design**
Definition: Design of streets, plazas, parks, waterfronts, and other publicly accessible spaces to support social interaction, civic life, and urban vitality. William H. Whyte's urban space research: key factors include sun, seating, food vendors, visual connection with street, and triangulation (elements that provoke interaction between strangers).
Key relationships: Jan Gehl (human-scale design), placemaking, Third Place (Ray Oldenburg), tactical urbanism, green infrastructure, biophilic design.

**C23 — Parametric and Computational Design**
Definition: Generative design processes using algorithmic rules (parameters) to explore form, optimize performance, or produce complex geometries. Tools: Grasshopper (Rhino), Dynamo (Revit), Generative Design (Autodesk). Enables performance simulation integration, fabrication-aware design, and exploration of solution spaces.
Key relationships: Algorithmic design, topology optimization, form finding, digital fabrication (CNC, 3D printing), mass customization, performance-driven design.

**C24 — Climate Resilience in Urban Design**
Definition: Design of cities and buildings to withstand and recover from climate hazards: flooding (elevated structures, permeable surfaces, retention basins), extreme heat (cool pavements, shade trees, green roofs), sea-level rise (managed retreat, coastal armoring, living shorelines), wind (aerodynamic form, wind-resistant construction).
Key relationships: Nature-based solutions (NbS), green-blue infrastructure, sponge city concept, coastal flood protection, heat action plans, urban resilience frameworks (100 Resilient Cities, C40).

**C25 — Embodied Carbon in Construction**
Definition: The greenhouse gas emissions associated with the materials and construction processes throughout the building lifecycle: extraction, manufacturing, transport, construction, maintenance, and demolition. Typically 10–20% of total building lifecycle emissions for high-performance buildings, but can be 50%+ for net-zero operational buildings. Key materials: cement (~8% of global CO₂), steel, aluminum.
Key relationships: Whole Life Carbon (operational + embodied), EC3 (Embodied Carbon in Construction Calculator), low-carbon concrete (SCMs: fly ash, GGBS, slag), mass timber (CLT, glulam), carbon stored in materials, circularity.

**C26 — Tall Building Design**
Definition: Buildings > 150m (supertall >300m, megatall >600m) require specialized structural systems to resist lateral loads (wind, seismic). Systems: moment frame, braced frame, tube (framed tube, tube-in-tube, bundled tube — Sears Tower), outrigger with belt trusses, diagrid (Hearst Tower), core with mega-columns.
Key relationships: Damping systems (tuned mass damper, water damper), wind tunnel testing, floor plate efficiency, net-to-gross ratio, base shear, overturning moment.

**C27 — Mass Timber Construction**
Definition: Structural use of engineered wood products at scales previously requiring steel or concrete: Cross-Laminated Timber (CLT), Glued Laminated Timber (glulam), Laminated Veneer Lumber (LVL), Mass Plywood Panels. Stores biogenic carbon, lower embodied carbon than steel/concrete, rapidly renewable.
Key relationships: Charring rate (fire protection in heavy timber), moisture management, acoustics (wood is less dense = more vibration), IBC 2021 (permits mass timber up to 18 stories), forestry certification (FSC, SFI).

**C28 — Housing Typologies**
Definition: The range of housing forms from single-family detached to dense multifamily. Missing middle housing: small-scale multifamily (duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, courtyard buildings, row houses) at densities between single-family and mid-rise apartment — historically common but prohibited by many U.S. zoning codes.
Key relationships: Floor area ratio (FAR), lot coverage, setback requirements, accessory dwelling units (ADUs), density bonuses, form-based code allowances.

**C29 — Vernacular Architecture**
Definition: Traditional building forms developed by local communities without professional architects, using locally available materials and climate-responsive strategies. Reflects accumulated cultural and environmental knowledge: adobe in hot-dry climates, stilt houses in flood-prone areas, thick stone walls in cold climates.
Key relationships: Bioclimatic design, passive cooling (windcatchers, courtyards, thick adobe walls), vernacular revival, Christopher Alexander ("A Pattern Language"), place identity.

**C30 — Urban Heat Island Mitigation**
Definition: Design strategies to reduce UHI intensity: cool roofs (high albedo, SRI ≥ 78 for low-slope roofs under ENERGY STAR), green roofs (evapotranspiration cooling), street trees (shade, evapotranspiration, canopy cover targets), cool pavements (permeable, high-albedo), urban water features.
Key relationships: Surface Energy Balance (SEB), urban forest, green infrastructure credit in LEED, TERN (heat resilience), urban albedo, tree equity.

## 5. Patterns

**P01 — Site Analysis and Climate-Responsive Orientation**
Description: Analyze sun path, prevailing winds, views, topography, drainage, and neighboring context before determining building footprint orientation and form; optimize solar access and natural ventilation potential.
When to use: All new building design; master planning; building performance simulation.
Example: Orient long axis East-West in temperate climates (NH) to maximize south-facing glazing for passive solar gain and minimize east/west heat gain; use overhangs sized for winter sun penetration and summer shading.

**P02 — Post-Occupancy Evaluation (POE)**
Description: Systematic assessment of building performance after occupancy: energy consumption vs. design intent, occupant satisfaction surveys, IEQ measurements, operational issues; feedback to improve design and operations.
When to use: Institutional buildings, repeat building types (schools, hospitals), building commissioning verification.
Example: ASHRAE POE methodology; UK Soft Landings process links design team to building management through first 2 years of operation.

**P03 — Integrated Design Process (IDP)**
Description: Early and continuous collaboration among all design disciplines (architecture, structural, MEP, civil, landscape, cost) and the owner, enabled by shared BIM model; resolve trade-offs holistically rather than sequentially.
When to use: All complex building projects; LEED certification requires integrative process.
Example: Passive House design requires HVAC engineer input during early schematic design to size mechanical systems based on building envelope performance targets.

**P04 — Zoning Variance Analysis**
Description: When a proposed project cannot meet standard zoning requirements, analyze criteria for variance (hardship, unique conditions) or special use permit (compatible with surrounding uses); present case to zoning board with supporting evidence.
When to use: Nonconforming sites; unusual program requirements; adaptive reuse.
Example: Historic building rehabilitation requires setback variance because the existing structure predates current setback requirements; demonstrate no adverse impact on neighbors.

**P05 — Transit Demand Analysis**
Description: Use four-step or activity-based travel demand modeling to forecast ridership, mode shares, and VMT impacts of transportation investments or land use changes; assess level of service (LOS) for all modes.
When to use: Transit feasibility studies, environmental impact assessment, TOD planning.
Example: BART extension ridership forecast uses regional travel demand model (MTC's Travel Model One) calibrated against observed boarding data.

**P06 — Affordable Housing Feasibility Analysis**
Description: Assess project financial feasibility combining: land cost, construction cost, financing terms (LIHTC tax credit equity, soft debt), income from restricted rents (based on AMI levels), and gap funding needed; optimize mix of unit types and AMI levels for viability.
When to use: Affordable housing development; community land trust projects; public-private partnerships.
Example: LIHTC 9% credit provides ~70% of eligible project costs as equity; combined with soft debt from HOME funds and below-market land from municipality, the project closes the gap between rents and costs.

**P07 — Structural System Selection Matrix**
Description: Evaluate structural systems against project criteria (span, height, geometry, cost, schedule, fire rating, seismic zone, architectural expression) using a weighted matrix; select system optimizing performance across criteria.
When to use: Early schematic design; cost planning; unusual geometry or program.
Example: Long-span gymnasium: steel truss vs. glulam arch vs. tension structure matrix comparing cost, lead time, aesthetics, and local contractor expertise.

**P08 — Energy Modeling and Target Setting**
Description: Create calibrated whole-building energy model (EnergyPlus, eQUEST) from design documents; simulate annual energy consumption by end use; test design alternatives (envelope, glazing, HVAC, lighting); set EUI target relative to ASHRAE baseline.
When to use: Required for LEED EA Prerequisite; best practice for all commercial buildings.
Example: Energy model shows 40% energy savings vs. ASHRAE 90.1 baseline by combining high-performance envelope, VRF HVAC, LED lighting with daylight controls, and heat recovery ventilation.

**P09 — Parametric Design Optimization**
Description: Define design objectives (structural efficiency, daylighting, energy, fabrication cost) and constraints; use parametric model with optimization algorithm (genetic algorithm, gradient descent) to explore solution space and identify high-performing design configurations.
When to use: Complex building geometry; performance-driven design; fabrication optimization.
Example: Grasshopper + Galapagos genetic algorithm optimizes window-to-wall ratio and shading device depth to minimize energy consumption while maximizing useful daylight in each facade orientation.

**P10 — Historic Preservation Review**
Description: Assess proposed alterations to historic buildings using Secretary of Interior's Standards for Rehabilitation; document existing conditions; design compatible alterations distinguishable from original fabric; minimize loss of historic material.
When to use: Federal Historic Tax Credit projects; local landmark reviews; adaptive reuse.
Example: Converting historic factory to apartments: retain original brick, windows, and steel structure; add rooftop addition set back from parapet (distinguishable, compatible scale); install modern MEP in existing interiors without damaging historic fabric.

**P11 — Urban Design Framework Planning**
Description: Establish a design framework (form-based code, urban design guidelines, master plan) setting parameters for multiple development projects over time: street grid, block structure, building heights, setbacks, active ground floor uses, open space network, and mobility framework.
When to use: New neighborhood development; infill planning; transit station area planning.
Example: Dallas Uptown form-based code specifies build-to-lines, active ground floor requirements, step-backs above 6 stories, and pedestrian infrastructure standards — enabling diverse development by many architects while maintaining coherent urban form.

**P12 — Lifecycle Cost Analysis (LCCA)**
Description: Compare capital costs, operating costs (energy, maintenance), and replacement costs over building lifecycle (30–50 years) for alternative design choices; select option with lowest total cost of ownership.
When to use: Comparing HVAC systems, envelope options, material specifications; making the business case for higher first-cost energy efficiency investments.
Example: LED lighting + daylight controls: $200k more than basic fluorescent; but saves $40k/yr in energy → 5-year simple payback, 30% IRR over 20-year lifecycle.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP01 — Designing Without Site-Specific Climate Analysis**
Why wrong: Generic design solutions ignore local climate, solar angles, wind patterns, and thermal mass requirements, resulting in buildings that perform poorly, are uncomfortable, and consume excessive energy.
What to do instead: Conduct solar, wind, and microclimate analysis early in design; tailor passive strategies to local conditions; use climate-based daylighting metrics (CBDM).

**AP02 — Treating Zoning as an Immutable Constraint**
Why wrong: Zoning codes are human-made regulations that can be changed; treating them as fixed limits stifles innovation and prevents necessary housing production and urban adaptation.
What to do instead: Engage zoning amendment processes for appropriate rezoning; use form-based codes, planned unit developments (PUDs), or special purpose districts for site-specific flexibility; advocate for code reform where codes are exclusionary or counterproductive.

**AP03 — Designing for Peak Load Rather Than Annual Performance**
Why wrong: Sizing HVAC systems for rare peak conditions (hottest day, maximum occupancy) typically results in oversized equipment that performs poorly at part-load conditions, which represent 80%+ of actual operating hours; high capital cost, poor efficiency, comfort problems.
What to do instead: Size for annual performance; right-size mechanical systems using energy modeling; use variable-speed drives and variable-flow systems for part-load efficiency; match system design to climate and occupancy profiles.

**AP04 — Parking Minimums as Default Planning**
Why wrong: Minimum parking requirements incentivize car use, consume land that could be development, raise housing costs (~$20,000–$80,000 per structured parking space), and create pedestrian-hostile environments; particularly counterproductive near transit.
What to do instead: Conduct parking demand studies; apply parking maximums (not minimums) near transit; use shared parking strategies; implement parking demand management; consider parking unbundling.

**AP05 — Neglecting Embodied Carbon in Net-Zero Strategy**
Why wrong: As buildings become more energy-efficient, operational carbon decreases over time, but embodied carbon from construction materials occurs immediately and is not recovered during the building's life. A "net-zero operational" building may have high upfront carbon from concrete and steel.
What to do instead: Measure and minimize embodied carbon from project inception using EC3 or equivalent tools; prioritize low-carbon materials (mass timber, recycled content, supplementary cementitious materials); design for adaptability and longevity.

**AP06 — Single-Discipline BIM Without Model Coordination**
Why wrong: BIM value is primarily realized through multidisciplinary model coordination; a single-discipline BIM that is not coordinated with structural, MEP, and civil models produces the same coordination conflicts as 2D drawings.
What to do instead: Establish BIM Execution Plan (BEP) at project start; define model authorship, exchange formats, coordination meetings, and clash detection procedures; use federated or centralized model management.

**AP07 — Ignoring Human Scale in Urban Design**
Why wrong: Designs that optimize for automobile movement, aerial views, or monumental scale produce places that are hostile to pedestrians and fail to generate street life. "If it looks good from an airplane, you've made a mistake" (Jan Gehl paraphrase).
What to do instead: Design from the perspective of the pedestrian at eye level; apply Gehl's principles; use active ground floor uses, transparent facades, and human-scale details; evaluate designs by walking the space at full scale.
