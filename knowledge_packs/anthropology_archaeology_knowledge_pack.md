## 1. Purpose

This pack encodes the structured knowledge base for Anthropology and Archaeology — the holistic study of humanity: its biological evolution, cultural diversity, social organization, material culture, and deep-time history. It provides deterministic, queryable knowledge for reasoning about human evolution, cultural systems, kinship and social structure, archaeological methods, and the interpretation of the material and biological record of past human societies.

## 3. Structure

This pack is organized in five tiers: (1) biological foundations of humanity (evolution, primatology, paleoanthropology); (2) cultural anthropology fundamentals (culture, kinship, religion, economy); (3) archaeological method and theory; (4) specific regional and thematic content (prehistory, complex societies, contact zones); (5) applied anthropology (forensic, medical, cultural heritage). All subfields share the commitment to holism — understanding humans as simultaneously biological, cultural, historical, and social beings.

## 4. Core Concepts

**C01 — The Four-Field Approach**
Definition: American anthropology is organized around four subfields: (1) biological/physical anthropology, (2) cultural/social anthropology, (3) linguistic anthropology, and (4) archaeology. The four-field approach reflects the belief that humanity can only be understood holistically — as biological, cultural, linguistic, and historical simultaneously.
Key relationships: Holism, cross-cultural comparison, biocultural synthesis, applied anthropology (fifth field recognized by some).

**C02 — Culture**
Definition: The shared, learned, and transmitted patterns of behavior, values, beliefs, symbols, and knowledge that characterize a human group. Culture is: learned (not biologically inherited), shared (by members of a group), symbolic (language, ritual, art), integrated (elements interconnect), and dynamic (changes over time and through contact).
Key relationships: Enculturation, acculturation, ethnocentrism, cultural relativism, emic vs. etic perspective, culture area concept.

**C03 — Cultural Relativism**
Definition: The methodological principle that cultural practices and beliefs should be understood within their own cultural context, without imposing the evaluative standards of another culture. Methodological relativism (understanding) is distinct from moral relativism (refusing to evaluate any practice as harmful).
Key relationships: Franz Boas (founder of American anthropology), ethnocentrism (opposite of CR), emic/etic distinction, human rights debates, female genital cutting as case study.

**C04 — Ethnography**
Definition: The primary research method of cultural anthropology: immersive, long-term fieldwork in a community, typically involving participant observation, formal and informal interviews, life histories, mapping, and collection of documentary evidence. Results in a written ethnographic account.
Key relationships: Participant observation, key informants, fieldwork, thick description (Geertz), reflexivity, positionality, institutional review board (IRB) ethics, crisis of representation.

**C05 — Kinship**
Definition: The social and cultural systems of relatedness (biological, adoptive, fictive) that structure family, descent, alliance, and inheritance. Kinship systems classify relatives according to culturally specific criteria that vary cross-culturally.
Key relationships: Descent groups (patrilineal, matrilineal, bilateral, ambilineal, double descent), lineage, clan, moiety, kinship terminology systems (Eskimo, Hawaiian, Iroquois, Crow, Omaha, Sudanese), marriage rules (exogamy, endogamy), alliance theory (Lévi-Strauss).

**C06 — Marriage Systems**
Definition: Culturally defined rules governing the formation of conjugal unions. Monogamy (one spouse), polygamy (multiple spouses: polygyny — one man multiple wives [most common form globally], polyandry — one woman multiple husbands), group marriage. All cultures have rules governing who may marry whom (incest taboos are universal; specific prohibitions vary).
Key relationships: Brideprice/bridewealth (goods from groom's family to bride's), dowry (goods from bride's family), exogamy, endogamy, levirate (widow marries deceased husband's brother), sororate.

**C07 — Political Organization**
Definition: The ways human societies organize authority, decision-making, and social control. Elman Service's typology: band (small, egalitarian, forager, personal authority), tribe (larger, segmentary, rank sometimes), chiefdom (hereditary rank, redistribution economy), state (stratified, specialized government, bureaucracy, monopoly on force).
Key relationships: Social stratification, hereditary vs. achieved leadership, redistribution, warfare, state formation theories (integration, conflict, managerial), neoevolutionary critique.

**C08 — Economic Anthropology**
Definition: The study of economic systems, exchange, and value across cultures. Three modes of exchange (Polanyi): reciprocity (gift exchange: generalized, balanced, negative), redistribution (goods flow to center then back out), and market exchange. Gift economy: exchange creates social bonds and obligations (Mauss, "The Gift").
Key relationships: Substantivism vs. formalism debate, moral economy, moral minimalist concept, prestige economy, potlatch (Northwest Coast redistribution ceremony), kula ring (Malinowski, Trobriand islands).

**C09 — Religion and Ritual**
Definition: Universal human practices involving beliefs in supernatural entities or forces, ritual actions, and moral systems. Key functions (Durkheim): social solidarity, marking social transitions. Key elements: myth (sacred narrative), ritual (prescribed action), symbol (sacred/profane distinction), taboo.
Key relationships: Animism (E.B. Tylor), totemism, shamanism, mana, rites of passage (van Gennep: separation → liminality → reincorporation), liminality, communitas, pilgrimage.

**C10 — Human Evolution and Paleoanthropology**
Definition: The fossil and genetic record of hominin evolution from the last common ancestor with chimpanzees (~6–7 Ma) to anatomically modern Homo sapiens (~300 ka). Key genera: Ardipithecus, Australopithecus, Paranthropus, Homo. Key species: H. habilis, H. erectus, H. heidelbergensis, H. neanderthalensis, H. sapiens.
Key relationships: Bipedalism, encephalization (brain size increase), tool use (Oldowan, Acheulean, Mousterian, Upper Paleolithic), out-of-Africa dispersals, multiregional vs. recent African origin models.

**C11 — Australopithecus and Early Hominins**
Definition: Bipedal hominins from 4.4–1.0 Ma in Africa. Key species: A. afarensis (Lucy, ~3.2 Ma, Hadar, Ethiopia; small brain ~400cc, bipedal), A. africanus (Taung child, R. Dart, 1924), A. sediba (~1.9 Ma). Paranthropus (robust australopiths): large megadont molars, sagittal crest.
Key relationships: Laetoli footprints (3.7 Ma bipedal tracks), Hadar site, brain size (~400–500cc), arboreal vs. obligate bipedality debate.

**C12 — Genus Homo**
Definition: Our genus, characterized by larger brain size (>600cc), reduced molar teeth, increased reliance on tools, and eventually language. H. habilis (~2.4–1.4 Ma, earliest tool maker, ~700cc); H. erectus (~1.9 Ma–100 ka, first to leave Africa, fire use, ~900cc); H. neanderthalensis (~400–40 ka, Europe/W. Asia, ~1,500cc brain, interbreeding with H. sapiens); H. sapiens (~300 ka to present, ~1,350cc).
Key relationships: Acheulean handaxe (H. erectus), anatomical vs. behavioral modernity, Upper Paleolithic revolution, modern human dispersal ~60–70 ka.

**C13 — Out of Africa and Modern Human Dispersal**
Definition: The dominant model: anatomically modern Homo sapiens evolved in Africa ~300 ka; behavioral modernity (symbolic thought, art, complex tools) ~100–50 ka; dispersed globally from Africa ~60–80 ka, replacing or admixing with archaic hominins. Interbreeding with Neanderthals (1–4% of non-African genomes) and Denisovans (Oceania/Asia) documented by ancient DNA.
Key relationships: Multiregional hypothesis (largely superseded), ancient DNA (Svante Pääbo, 2010 Nobel), Denisovans, Beringian land bridge, sahul, modern human behavioral modernity.

**C14 — Primatology**
Definition: The study of non-human primates (200+ species of prosimians, monkeys, and apes). Provides baseline for understanding human social behavior, cognition, and evolution. Great apes (chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas, orangutans) are most closely related to humans (~98.8% DNA similarity for chimpanzees).
Key relationships: Jane Goodall (chimpanzee tool use, Gombe, 1960), Dian Fossey (mountain gorillas), Frans de Waal (bonobo social behavior), primate social organization (fission-fusion: chimpanzees), theory of mind, mirror neurons.

**C15 — Archaeological Context**
Definition: The spatial and temporal relationships of artifacts, features, and ecofacts within a site. Primary context: objects found in their original position of use or deposition. Secondary context: objects moved or disturbed after original deposition. Stratigraphic context: position within soil layers (strata).
Key relationships: Provenience (precise 3D location), association (objects found together = contemporaneous), stratigraphy (law of superposition), matrix (surrounding soil), site formation processes (cultural and natural taphonomy).

**C16 — Archaeological Dating Methods**
Definition: Relative dating: stratigraphy (deeper = older, within undisturbed sites), typology (seriation of artifact styles), palynology. Absolute dating: radiocarbon (¹⁴C, <50,000 yr), dendrochronology (tree rings, <14,000 yr), thermoluminescence (TL, ~100,000 yr), optically stimulated luminescence (OSL), K-Ar and Ar-Ar (>100,000 yr, volcanic contexts).
Key relationships: Calibration curve (IntCal23 for ¹⁴C), Bayesian age modeling, cross-dating, dendrochronology masters chronology.

**C17 — Material Culture**
Definition: The physical objects created, used, and disposed of by human societies. Archaeological classification: artifact (portable, human-made), feature (non-portable, e.g., hearth, post-hole, floor), ecofact (biological remains: bone, plant, pollen), site (spatial cluster of remains), region.
Key relationships: Typology (classification by form/function), chaîne opératoire (operational sequence of tool manufacture), use-wear analysis, experimental archaeology, residue analysis.

**C18 — Lithic Analysis**
Definition: Analysis of stone tools (lithics), the most durable archaeological material. Classification: core (worked for flake removal), flake (detached from core), tool (shaped flake or core). Key technological traditions: Oldowan (~2.6 Ma, simple choppers), Acheulean (~1.76 Ma, handaxes), Mousterian/Middle Paleolithic (~300–40 ka, Levallois technique), Upper Paleolithic (~50–12 ka, blade technology).
Key relationships: Reduction sequence, debitage, knapping, retouch, use-wear, obsidian hydration dating, raw material sourcing.

**C19 — Faunal Analysis (Zooarchaeology)**
Definition: Analysis of animal remains (bone, shell, scales, feathers) to reconstruct subsistence, environment, seasonality, and ritual behavior. Minimum number of individuals (MNI) vs. number of identified specimens (NISP); taphonomy (what happened to bones after death); butchery marks, burning, gnawing.
Key relationships: Kill sites vs. processing sites, mortality profiles (prime-dominated = hunting; catastrophic = herd capture), species representation, stable isotopes for diet and migration.

**C20 — Paleoethnobotany**
Definition: Analysis of plant remains (seeds, charred wood, pollen, phytoliths, residues) to reconstruct past plant use, agriculture origins, and environment. Flotation (water separation of charred seeds) is the primary recovery technique.
Key relationships: Domestication (genetic and morphological changes under human selection), centers of origin (Vavilov centers), founder crops (wheat, barley, lentil, pea, flax in SW Asia), wild vs. domesticated morphology.

**C21 — Social Complexity and Urbanism**
Definition: The development of socially complex societies characterized by: political hierarchy, occupational specialization, writing/record-keeping, monumental architecture, and urban centers. Major early urban civilizations: Mesopotamia (Uruk, ~4000 BCE), Indus Valley (Harappan, ~2600 BCE), Egypt, Shang China (~1600 BCE), Mesoamerica (Olmec, Maya), Andes (Chavín, Tiwanaku, Inca).
Key relationships: Civilization, complex chiefdom, early state, redistribution economy, temple economy, palatial economy, collapse (Bronze Age collapse, Classic Maya collapse).

**C22 — Ancient DNA (aDNA)**
Definition: Genetic material preserved in archaeological remains enabling direct study of past population genetics, ancestry, migration, disease, and kinship. Revolutionized understanding of prehistory: Yamnaya steppe ancestry in Europe, population replacement events, Neanderthal-Homo sapiens admixture.
Key relationships: Library preparation, authentication criteria (damage patterns, contamination controls), population genetics (ADMIXTURE, PCA, D-statistics), kinship analysis, pathogen aDNA (Y. pestis in plague, M. tuberculosis).

**C23 — Bioarchaeology**
Definition: Analysis of human skeletal remains within archaeological context to reconstruct: diet (stable isotopes δ¹³C, δ¹⁵N), migration (Sr isotopes), health (dental pathology, Harris lines, porotic hyperostosis), trauma (violence, warfare), demography, and social identity.
Key relationships: Skeletal aging (epiphyseal fusion, dental wear, pubic symphysis), sexing (morphometric methods), stature estimation, taphonomy, commingled assemblages.

**C24 — Symbolic Culture and Cognitive Modernity**
Definition: The capacity for symbolic thought: using arbitrary symbols (language, art, ritual) to represent abstract concepts. Markers in the archaeological record: ochre use (pigment, ~300 ka Blombos Cave), engraved geometric patterns (~77 ka), shell beads (~130 ka), portable art (Venus figurines, ~35 ka), cave art (Altamira, Lascaux, ~40–17 ka), musical instruments (~40 ka).
Key relationships: Upper Paleolithic revolution (Europe ~45 ka), behavioral modernity (Africa earlier), Chauvet Cave (France, ~36 ka, oldest figurative cave art), Sulawesi cave art (Indonesia, ~45 ka).

**C25 — Cultural Contact and Colonialism**
Definition: Anthropological study of cultural interactions, especially between colonial powers and colonized peoples. Acculturation: culture change resulting from sustained contact. Culture contact produces: syncretism (blending), resistance, revitalization movements (Ghost Dance), creolization, and forced assimilation.
Key relationships: World systems theory (Wallerstein), dependency theory, post-colonial theory, indigenous rights (UN Declaration on Rights of Indigenous Peoples, UNDRIP 2007), repatriation (NAGPRA, US 1990).

**C26 — Gender and Sexuality in Anthropology**
Definition: Gender is the cultural construction of roles, behaviors, and identities associated with sex; it is cross-culturally variable. Third and multiple genders recognized in many societies (hijra in South Asia, Two-Spirit in some Native American cultures, fa'afafine in Samoa). Feminist anthropology reveals androcentric bias in earlier research.
Key relationships: Sex vs. gender distinction, intersectionality, Ruth Benedict (gender variation cross-culturally), Margaret Mead (controversy about Samoan adolescence), queer anthropology.

**C27 — Medical Anthropology**
Definition: Study of health, illness, and healing across cultures. Disease vs. illness vs. sickness: biomedical vs. experiential vs. social dimensions. Cultural explanatory models of illness affect help-seeking behavior and treatment adherence. Traditional medicine systems (Ayurveda, TCM, curanderismo) are culturally embedded.
Key relationships: Explanatory models (Kleinman), health disparities, structural violence (Paul Farmer), ethnomedicine, global health, pharmaceutical anthropology, medicalization.

**C28 — Environmental Anthropology and Political Ecology**
Definition: Study of human-environment relationships: how cultures perceive, use, and transform their environment; how political-economic systems create environmental inequality. Political ecology examines power relations in resource access, environmental degradation, and conservation conflicts.
Key relationships: Cultural ecology (Julian Steward), adaptation, ecological footprint, common-pool resources (Ostrom), indigenous land rights, environmental justice, conservation refugees.

**C29 — Forensic Anthropology**
Definition: Application of biological anthropological methods to legal contexts: identification of unknown human remains (sex, age, ancestry, stature, trauma), estimation of time since death, reconstruction of events, and testimony in court.
Key relationships: Skeletal trauma analysis (antemortem vs. perimortem vs. postmortem), taphonomy (decomposition rates, disarticulation), mass grave investigation, identification of disaster victims (DVI), Daubert standard (scientific evidence admissibility in US courts).

**C30 — Heritage and Archaeological Ethics**
Definition: Management of archaeological and cultural heritage raises ethical questions about: who owns the past, repatriation of human remains and objects (NAGPRA in US), indigenous consultation, commercial archaeology vs. research, looting and illicit trade, and representation of other cultures.
Key relationships: NAGPRA (Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, 1990), UNESCO Convention (1970, illicit traffic), Elgin/Parthenon Marbles controversy, community archaeology, descendant communities, Vermillion Accord (human remains).

## 5. Patterns

**P01 — Ethnographic Fieldwork Protocol**
Description: Extended (12+ months) participant observation in a community; immersion in daily life; maintain field notes (jottings → expanded notes → thematic memos); conduct semi-structured interviews; build trust over time; reflect on positionality and reflexivity; obtain informed consent; reciprocate community.
When to use: Cultural anthropology research; applied anthropology; development practice.
Example: Malinowski's Trobriand Islands fieldwork (1914–1918) established participant observation as the core ethnographic method; produced "Argonauts of the Western Pacific" on kula exchange.

**P02 — Archaeological Excavation Procedure**
Description: Survey and site selection; establish grid system and datum point; excavate in stratigraphic units (natural or arbitrary levels); record provenience of all finds in 3D; collect soil samples for flotation and residue analysis; photograph and draw sections; maintain field records and deposit database.
When to use: Archaeological data collection; salvage archaeology; research excavation.
Example: Harris Matrix records stratigraphic relationships between all excavated units, enabling reconstruction of site formation history and temporal sequence.

**P03 — Kinship Diagram Construction**
Description: Use standardized kinship notation (triangle = male, circle = female, horizontal line = marriage, vertical line = descent, equals sign = siblings) to map genealogical relationships within a group; identify descent group membership, marriage patterns, and residence rules.
When to use: Kinship analysis in ethnographic fieldwork; establishing social network structure.
Example: Genealogical survey of Trobriand village reveals matrilineal clan structure where all children belong to mother's clan; father is from a different clan.

**P04 — Morphological Assessment of Fossil Hominins**
Description: Measure cranial capacity, facial morphology, dental dimensions, postcranial anatomy; compare to known taxa; assess phylogenetic position using cladistic analysis (shared derived characters = synapomorphies); contextualize with stratigraphic age and associated fauna.
When to use: Paleoanthropological analysis of new hominin material.
Example: Discovery of Homo naledi (Berger et al., 2015, Rising Star Cave, South Africa): mosaic of primitive (small brain ~465cc, curved fingers) and derived (foot, teeth) features; dated to 335–236 ka despite primitive morphology.

**P05 — Stable Isotope Analysis for Diet Reconstruction**
Description: Extract collagen and/or apatite from skeletal remains; measure δ¹³C (C3 vs. C4 plant diet; marine protein) and δ¹⁵N (trophic level, protein intake) to reconstruct diet at individual and population level; compare across social groups, sites, or time periods.
When to use: Bioarchaeological diet reconstruction; social differentiation analysis; migration studies.
Example: δ¹³C analysis of Maya skeletons from Copán shows elite individuals had significantly higher maize consumption, while lower-status individuals had more varied diet including more C3 plants.

**P06 — Typological Seriation**
Description: Order artifact assemblages from multiple sites or deposits by their similarities, assuming gradual stylistic change produces a battleship-shaped frequency distribution for each type over time; combine with absolute dates to build chronological sequence.
When to use: Building relative chronologies for regions without absolute dates; ordering undated assemblages.
Example: Flinders Petrie used pottery seriation in Egypt (1899) to establish relative chronology of predynastic sites — the founding method of archaeological chronology.

**P07 — Emic/Etic Analysis**
Description: Document the insider (emic) perspective of culture participants regarding their own practices and beliefs; compare to the outsider (etic) analytical categories applied by the researcher; examine convergences and divergences; privilege emic understanding in ethnographic representation.
When to use: All ethnographic and anthropological interpretation.
Example: Azande "witchcraft" (Evans-Pritchard, 1937): emic explanation of misfortune through witch substance (mangu); etic analysis reveals social function of witchcraft accusations in managing interpersonal conflict.

**P08 — Comparative Method**
Description: Systematically compare cultural features across societies (using databases like HRAF — Human Relations Area Files) to test hypotheses about cultural evolution, ecological adaptation, or universal patterns; control for historical relatedness (Galton's Problem).
When to use: Cross-cultural hypothesis testing; cultural evolution studies.
Example: Testing whether polygyny correlates with high-quality male resource holding capacity using HRAF cross-cultural sample; controlling for language family to avoid pseudo-replication.

**P09 — Bioarchaeological Trauma Assessment**
Description: Examine skeletal remains for evidence of violence: cranial depression fractures (perimortem if beveled edges), parry fractures (defensive forearm injuries), projectile point embedded in bone, decapitation, scalping marks; distinguish interpersonal violence from ritual, execution, and warfare.
When to use: Archaeological violence studies; forensic archaeology; conflict analysis.
Example: Analysis of Crow Creek massacre site (S. Dakota, ~1325 CE): 486 individuals in a mass grave, ~90% showing severe perimortem trauma including scalping and mutilation — evidence for prehistoric warfare.

**P10 — Ancient DNA Population Structure Analysis**
Description: Extract aDNA from skeletal material; generate genome-wide SNP data; perform PCA, ADMIXTURE analysis, D-statistics (ABBA-BABA tests), and qpAdm modeling to determine ancestry proportions, migration events, and admixture with other groups.
When to use: Prehistoric migration and population history research.
Example: Haak et al. (2015): genome-wide aDNA from 69 Europeans shows ~75% of ancestry in Bronze Age Europeans derived from Yamnaya steppe pastoralists replacing earlier Neolithic farmers.

**P11 — Landscape Archaeology Survey**
Description: Survey archaeological sites across a landscape using systematic field walking, remote sensing (LiDAR, aerial photography, satellite imagery), GIS analysis, and geophysical prospection (ground-penetrating radar, magnetometry) to understand settlement patterns, land use, and regional organization.
When to use: Regional-scale archaeological research; predictive modeling; heritage management.
Example: LiDAR survey of Cambodian lowlands (Evans et al., 2016) revealed urban infrastructure of Angkor Wat complex extending far beyond temple cores — the largest pre-industrial city complex ever discovered.

**P12 — Ethnoarchaeology**
Description: Study of material culture production, use, and discard among living peoples to develop interpretive frameworks for the archaeological record; documents formation processes, use-wear patterns, and depositional behaviors.
When to use: Developing analogical reasoning for interpreting archaeological assemblages.
Example: Gould's work with Australian desert Aborigines on stone tool use and discard patterns; Kramer's ceramic studies in Iran as analog for interpreting prehistoric pottery assemblages.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP01 — Cultural Evolutionism (Unilinear)**
Why wrong: 19th-century anthropologists (Morgan, Tylor) ranked cultures on a single scale from "savagery" to "barbarism" to "civilization," implying Western societies represent the pinnacle of human development. This is empirically wrong, ethnocentrically biased, and was used to justify colonialism.
What to do instead: Apply multilinear cultural evolution (Julian Steward): different societies adapt to local environments in different ways; no single trajectory or hierarchy; no culture is more "evolved" than another in any normative sense.

**AP02 — Confirming Archaeological Narratives from a Single Line of Evidence**
Why wrong: Archaeological interpretation is inherently inferential; individual data types (pottery, faunal remains, isotopes, aDNA) each have taphonomic biases, preservation issues, and interpretive ambiguities. Single-line interpretation is fragile.
What to do instead: Apply consilience of evidence — converging lines of independent evidence (material culture + biological remains + isotopes + aDNA + spatial analysis) strengthen interpretations; contradictions require rethinking, not ignoring.

**AP03 — Treating Ethnographic Present as Static**
Why wrong: Describing cultures in an "ethnographic present" implies they are unchanging, bounded, pristine, and unaffected by history, colonialism, or global processes. This "timeless primitives" trope misrepresents all contemporary peoples as outside of history.
What to do instead: Situate cultural descriptions historically; acknowledge colonial impacts, globalization, and change; document how communities have always adapted and will continue to do so.

**AP04 — Race as Biological Category**
Why wrong: Anthropological genetics has definitively shown that "race" as popularly conceived (discrete biological groups with consistent anatomical, behavioral, and cognitive differences) does not reflect actual human biological variation. Genetic diversity within "races" exceeds diversity between them.
What to do instead: Analyze human biological variation as continuous, clinal, and complex; treat race as a social and political construct with real social consequences; use ancestry, population, or geographic origin where biological categories are needed.

**AP05 — Equating Material Complexity with Cultural Sophistication**
Why wrong: The archaeological record preserves only what survives — stone, ceramic, bone. Complex social organizations, sophisticated worldviews, rich oral traditions, and elaborate ritual can leave minimal material traces. Forager societies are not "simple" just because they leave few durable artifacts.
What to do instead: Assess social complexity through appropriate indicators; use ethnographic and historical analogy cautiously; never conflate material simplicity with cognitive or cultural simplicity.

**AP06 — Direct Historical Analogy Without Critical Evaluation**
Why wrong: Using the behavior or material culture of living groups as direct analogs for unrelated prehistoric peoples ignores cultural change over time, geographic variation, and the uniqueness of historical contexts.
What to do instead: Use the "direct historical approach" (working back from documented ethnographic groups to their ancestors in the archaeological record) where historical continuity can be demonstrated; evaluate analogies explicitly for relevance; prefer multiple, converging analogies.

**AP07 — Ignoring Indigenous Perspectives in Archaeological Interpretation**
Why wrong: Archaeological interpretation of indigenous heritage conducted without consultation with descendant communities risks misrepresentation, violation of cultural sensitivities, and perpetuation of colonial knowledge extraction. NAGPRA violations harm living communities.
What to do instead: Engage descendant communities as partners from project design; obtain free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC); share interpretations; incorporate indigenous knowledge as a legitimate epistemological perspective; support community-controlled archaeology.
