## 4. Core Concepts

**CC-001 — Energy Systems**
Three primary energy systems provide ATP for muscular contraction: (1) Phosphocreatine (PCr) system: immediate, anaerobic; powers maximal efforts of 0–10 seconds; PCr stored in muscle resynthesizes ATP via creatine kinase. (2) Glycolytic (lactate) system: anaerobic; powers intense efforts of 10 seconds–2 minutes; glucose/glycogen → pyruvate → lactate + ATP (net 2–3 ATP/glucose); produces H⁺ causing acidosis, not lactate per se, contributing to fatigue. (3) Oxidative (aerobic) system: aerobic; dominates efforts >2–3 minutes; glucose → 30–32 ATP, fat → 105–129 ATP per fatty acid via Krebs cycle and electron transport chain.

**CC-002 — VO₂max (Maximal Oxygen Uptake)**
The maximum rate at which the body can consume oxygen during maximal exercise (mL O₂/kg/min). The gold standard measure of aerobic capacity. Limited by cardiac output (Q = HR × SV), oxygen-carrying capacity (hemoglobin), and mitochondrial oxidative capacity. Elite male endurance athletes: ~70–85 mL/kg/min; elite females: ~60–75 mL/kg/min; sedentary adults: ~30–40 mL/kg/min; Oscar Pereiro Sio (2006 Tour de France winner): ~93 mL/kg/min. Trainable by 10–20% with endurance training.

**CC-003 — Lactate Threshold (LT) and Critical Power**
Lactate threshold (LT): the exercise intensity above which blood lactate begins to accumulate above resting levels (~2 mmol/L). LT2 (OBLA, Onset of Blood Lactate Accumulation): ~4 mmol/L, corresponds to maximal lactate steady state (MLSS) — the highest sustainable exercise intensity. Critical power (CP): the highest power output that can be sustained indefinitely aerobically; above CP, W' (anaerobic work capacity) is consumed and exercise is terminally limited. LT and CP are superior to VO₂max for predicting endurance performance.

**CC-004 — Muscle Fiber Types**
Skeletal muscle contains two primary fiber types: Type I (slow-twitch, oxidative): high mitochondrial density, myoglobin content, capillary density; fatigue-resistant; powers endurance activities; slower peak force. Type II (fast-twitch): IIa (fast oxidative-glycolytic, intermediate) and IIx (fast glycolytic, fastest, most fatigable). Fiber type proportion is largely genetically determined (~50% heritable) but training shifts IIx toward IIa. Elite marathon runners: ~80% Type I; elite sprinters: ~70–80% Type II.

**CC-005 — Cardiac Adaptations to Training**
Endurance training produces eccentric cardiac hypertrophy: increased left ventricular (LV) volume (athlete's heart), increased stroke volume (SV: 150–200 mL vs. 70 mL sedentary), and decreased resting heart rate (bradycardia: 35–50 bpm in elite cyclists vs. 60–75 bpm sedentary). Strength training produces concentric cardiac hypertrophy: increased LV wall thickness without volume increase. Resting bradycardia in athletes is caused by increased vagal tone, not reduced intrinsic HR; increased stroke volume compensates for lower HR to maintain cardiac output at rest.

**CC-006 — Newton's Laws Applied to Biomechanics**
First Law (inertia): a body at rest or in uniform motion continues unless acted on by a net force — explains momentum in running, the need for deceleration force to change direction. Second Law (F=ma): net force equals mass × acceleration — quantifies force required for acceleration and the effect of body mass on performance. Third Law (action-reaction): every force has an equal and opposite reaction — ground reaction forces (GRF) propel the runner; the swimmer pushes water back and is propelled forward.

**CC-007 — Ground Reaction Force (GRF) and Running Mechanics**
During running, the foot-strike generates GRFs measured by force plates: vertical GRF peaks at 2–3× body weight during midstance; anterior-posterior GRF shows a braking phase (foot landing, forward velocity decreases) and a propulsive phase (push-off). Optimal running economy involves minimizing ground contact time, maximizing leg stiffness (spring-mass model), and controlling vertical oscillation. Rearfoot striking produces a distinct impact transient (impact peak) not seen in forefoot or midfoot striking.

**CC-008 — Joint Torque and Levers**
Torque (moment of force) = Force × perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation (moment arm). The musculoskeletal system operates primarily as third-class levers (effort between fulcrum and load), producing high-velocity, low-force output from high-force, low-velocity muscle contractions. The bicep moment arm at the elbow is approximately 3–5 cm; a 10 kg load at 30 cm from the elbow requires ~60–100× greater muscle force. Moment arm changes with joint angle, explaining strength curve variation.

**CC-009 — Muscle Mechanics: Length-Tension and Force-Velocity**
Length-tension relationship: muscle produces maximum force at its optimal length (near resting length, maximum actin-myosin overlap). At very short or long lengths, force production decreases. Force-velocity relationship: muscle force decreases as shortening velocity increases (Hill's equation); at maximum shortening velocity (Vmax), force = 0. During eccentric (lengthening) contractions, force can exceed isometric maximum by ~20–50% — explaining the greater strength and hypertrophy stimulus of eccentric loading.

**CC-010 — Periodization**
The systematic planned variation of training variables (volume, intensity, frequency) over time to optimize performance and recovery. Linear periodization: gradual increase in intensity as volume decreases over a training cycle. Undulating (non-linear) periodization: variation within a week. Block periodization: sequential blocks emphasizing specific fitness qualities (accumulation → transmutation → realization). Hans Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) model: stress → fatigue → adaptation → supercompensation — the physiological basis of periodization.

**CC-011 — Progressive Overload and Supercompensation**
The principle that training stimulus must progressively increase to continue driving adaptation. Overload: training must exceed current capacity to stimulate adaptation. Supercompensation: following appropriate recovery after a training stressor, performance rises above baseline. If the next training stimulus arrives too early (overtraining) or too late (detraining), the supercompensation cycle is disrupted. Practical implementation: increase volume or intensity by ≤5–10% per week to minimize injury risk.

**CC-012 — SAID Principle (Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands)**
The body adapts specifically to the type, magnitude, and frequency of stress imposed. Distance runners develop oxidative capacity but not upper body strength; swimmers develop shoulder mobility but not leg power; power athletes develop fast-twitch fiber recruitment but not endurance. All training programs must be designed from the SAID principle: training specificity determines transfer to sport performance. Too much non-specific training dilutes specific adaptation.

**CC-013 — Resistance Training and Hypertrophy**
Skeletal muscle hypertrophy (increase in fiber cross-sectional area) is driven by three mechanisms: mechanical tension (primary driver: heavy loading via actin-myosin force generation and titin passive tension), metabolic stress (accumulated metabolites — lactate, H⁺, inorganic phosphate — during high-rep training), and muscle damage (DOMS-associated, least important). Protein synthesis must exceed protein breakdown; this requires adequate dietary protein (1.6–2.2 g/kg/day), training volume (10–20 sets/muscle/week), and sleep (GH released during slow-wave sleep).

**CC-014 — Bone Remodeling and Wolff's Law**
Bone remodels in response to mechanical loading: osteoblasts deposit new bone along lines of stress; osteoclasts resorb bone from areas of low stress (Wolff's Law, 1892). Weight-bearing exercise is the most powerful stimulus for bone density (BMD) increase. Peak bone mass is achieved by age ~30; thereafter, maintaining or slowing BMD loss requires ongoing loading. Impact loading (running, jumping) is more osteogenic than non-impact exercise (swimming, cycling).

**CC-015 — Sports Nutrition: Energy Availability and Fueling**
Energy availability (EA) = energy intake − exercise energy expenditure, expressed per kg of fat-free mass. Low EA (<30 kcal/kgFFM/day) triggers the Female Athlete Triad / Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S): hormonal suppression, bone stress, impaired recovery, immune dysfunction, and cardiovascular changes, affecting male and female athletes. Carbohydrate fueling: muscle glycogen is rate-limiting for high-intensity exercise >60 minutes; intraexercise carbohydrate (30–90 g/hr, glucose + fructose for >60g/hr to use multiple transporter pathways) maintains performance.

**CC-016 — Protein and Recovery Nutrition**
Post-exercise protein ingestion (0.3–0.4 g/kg body weight) stimulates muscle protein synthesis (MPS) and promotes recovery. Leucine is the primary amino acid signaling mTORC1 activation (MPS pathway); ~3–4 g leucine per meal (found in ~25–40 g whey protein) maximally stimulates MPS. Distribution matters: 4 meals of 0.3 g/kg protein spaced every 3–4 hours optimizes 24-hour MPS. Pre-sleep casein (30–40 g) extends MPS overnight. Total daily protein (1.6–2.2 g/kg/day) is more important than timing for hypertrophy.

**CC-017 — Hydration and Performance**
Even mild dehydration (2% body weight loss as sweat) impairs aerobic performance (~10%), cognitive function, and thermoregulation. Core body temperature rises during exercise; sweating (evaporative cooling) is the primary defense against heat stroke. Electrolyte loss (sodium primarily, 500–2,000 mg/L sweat) must be replaced in prolonged events to prevent hyponatremia (excess water intake without sodium) — which can be fatal. The American College of Sports Medicine guidelines: drink to thirst during exercise <60 min; weigh pre/post for longer events and replace accordingly.

**CC-018 — Creatine: The Most Evidenced Legal Ergogenic**
Creatine monohydrate supplementation increases intramuscular PCr stores by 15–25%, enhancing short-burst, high-intensity performance (sprinting, resistance training, repeated sprint sports) by 5–15%. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) considers creatine the most evidence-based, safe, and legal ergogenic supplement. Loading phase: 20 g/day for 5 days; maintenance: 3–5 g/day. Responsive in ~70% of individuals; non-responders have higher baseline PCr stores.

**CC-019 — Injury Mechanisms: Sprains, Strains, and Stress Fractures**
Sprain: ligament injury (Grade I: micro-tear; Grade II: partial tear; Grade III: complete rupture). Ankle lateral ligament complex (ATFL most commonly) is the most frequently sprained ligament in sports. Strain: muscle or tendon injury. Hamstring strains (biceps femoris long head, proximal musculotendinous junction) are the most common muscle injury in field sports. Stress fractures: bone fatigue failure from repetitive sub-maximal loading; common in tibial shaft, metatarsals, and navicular in distance runners; require 6–12 weeks non-weight-bearing recovery.

**CC-020 — ACL Injury and Prevention**
The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) restrains anterior tibial translation and rotational forces on the knee. Non-contact ACL injuries (most common in soccer, basketball, netball) occur during deceleration, cutting, or landing with the knee near extension and excessive valgus collapse. Female athletes suffer ACL injury at 3–6× the rate of males due to hormonal, anatomical (Q-angle, intercondylar notch width), and neuromuscular factors. The FIFA 11+ warm-up protocol reduces ACL injury risk by approximately 50% in female soccer players.

**CC-021 — Overtraining Syndrome (OTS)**
Chronic imbalance between training load and recovery resulting in sustained performance decrement, mood disturbance, and immunosuppression unresolved by normal rest. Distinguished from functional overreaching (short-term, resolves in days-weeks). OTS symptoms: decreased performance, persistent fatigue, mood disturbance, increased illness susceptibility, sleep disruption, hormonal dysregulation (reduced LH/FSH, testosterone, IGF-1; elevated cortisol). Recovery requires weeks to months of reduced or ceased training; prevention through monitoring (HRV, subjective wellness, performance metrics) and planned recovery.

**CC-022 — Heart Rate Variability (HRV) as Recovery Marker**
HRV measures the variation in time between consecutive heartbeats (RNN intervals). Higher HRV reflects greater parasympathetic (vagal) tone and is associated with better recovery, fitness, and health. HRV is suppressed by: high training load, insufficient sleep, illness, stress, and alcohol. Daily HRV monitoring with a validated device (Polar H10, Oura Ring) enables individualized training load management: training hard on high-HRV days and recovering on low-HRV days. The natural log of the root mean square of successive differences (lnRMSSD) is the most practical HRV metric for sport.

**CC-023 — Sleep and Athletic Performance**
Sleep is the most powerful recovery tool. During slow-wave sleep (SWS, NREM3): growth hormone (GH) is secreted (primary anabolic stimulus), tissue repair occurs, and memory consolidation for motor skills is enhanced. Total sleep deprivation (24 h): 30% decrease in reaction time, equivalent to 0.1% BAC. Chronic mild sleep restriction (6 hours/night for 2 weeks): progressive accumulation of cognitive and physical impairment. Elite athletes sleep 8–10 hours/night; sleep extension (9–10 hours) improves reaction time, sprint speed, and shooting accuracy in multiple sport-specific studies.

**CC-024 — Sports Psychology: Arousal and the Inverted-U Hypothesis**
The Yerkes-Dodson Law (1908): performance peaks at an intermediate level of arousal, declining at both low arousal (under-activation, boredom) and high arousal (anxiety, panic). The optimal arousal level is task-dependent: fine motor tasks (archery, golf putting) peak at low arousal; explosive power tasks (weightlifting, sprint starts) peak at high arousal. Individual Zones of Optimal Functioning (IZOF, Hanin): each athlete has a personal optimal arousal zone, which may not be the moderate level.

**CC-025 — Flow State**
A psychological state of optimal experience (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990) characterized by complete absorption, effortless action, loss of self-consciousness, and intrinsic motivation. Achieved when challenge slightly exceeds skill level. Neurologically associated with transient hypofrontality (reduced activity in the prefrontal cortex, the seat of self-referential processing and anxiety), increased alpha/theta brainwave activity, and dopamine/norepinephrine release. Flow is sought through deliberate practice of the conditions: clear goals, immediate feedback, and challenge-skill balance.

**CC-026 — Aerodynamics and Sports Performance**
Aerodynamic drag force: F_d = ½ρv²C_d A (ρ: air density, v: velocity, C_d: drag coefficient, A: frontal area). Drag increases with the square of velocity — dominant resistance for cyclists above ~30 km/h (>90% of total resistance). Time trialists minimize A·C_d via tucked position, streamlined helmets, and skin suits. Drafting (riding in slipstream) reduces drag by 20–40%. Altitude racing: lower air density reduces drag and aerobic performance simultaneously — the net effect determines whether altitude racing faster.

**CC-027 — Swimming Hydrodynamics**
Swimming resistance: form drag (frontal area and shape), friction drag (surface skin), and wave drag (at water surface, dominant above ~1.5 m/s). Technique dominates over fitness for recreational swimmers: a well-streamlined position reduces form drag by 50%. Buoyancy force (Archimedes): body density determines natural float position; muscle is denser (~1.06 g/cm³) than fat (~0.9 g/cm³), so lean athletes sink more readily. Propulsion via hydrofoil action of the hand (pitch ~30°, angle of attack optimized) rather than simple paddling maximizes propulsive efficiency.

**CC-028 — Strength-to-Weight Ratio and Power-to-Weight Ratio**
In weight-bearing activities (running, cycling climbing), power-to-weight ratio (W/kg) determines performance. Elite climbing cyclists achieve 5.5–6.5 W/kg for 20-minute efforts. Reducing body mass while maintaining power output improves P/W; however, below critical lean mass, performance degrades (loss of muscle mass reduces absolute power, immune function, and bone density). The optimal racing weight concept: the mass at which P/W is maximized, specific to the athlete and event.

**CC-029 — Motor Learning and Skill Acquisition**
Fitts and Posner (1967) three-stage model: Cognitive stage (explicit attention to each component, error-prone), Associative stage (refinement, fewer errors, less cognitive load), Autonomous stage (automaticity, executive resources freed for tactical decisions). Deliberate practice (Ericsson): effortful, focused, feedback-rich repetition just beyond current ability — the mechanism of expert skill development (~10,000 hours as a rough heuristic for elite status, not a law). Variable practice (practice in varying conditions) produces better long-term retention than blocked practice.

**CC-030 — Thermoregulation in Sport**
Core temperature during intense exercise rises to 39–40°C (high but tolerable); heat stroke occurs above ~40.5°C. Heat dissipation: radiation (significant at rest, negligible during exercise), evaporation (sweating, primary during exercise — each gram of water evaporation removes 0.58 kcal), convection, conduction. Acclimatization to heat (10–14 days in hot environment): increases plasma volume, reduces sweat Na⁺ concentration, lowers exercising core temperature and HR, improves thermoregulatory efficiency. Cold water immersion (CWI, ice baths) post-exercise: constricts blood vessels, reduces inflammation markers and DOMS (delayed-onset muscle soreness), though may attenuate long-term strength adaptation if used chronically.

**CC-031 — Paralympic Classification**
Paralympic athletes are classified by functional impairment type and impact on sport performance, not medical diagnosis. Ten impairment types: impaired muscle power, impaired range of motion, limb deficiency, leg length difference, short stature, hypertonia (elevated muscle tone), ataxia, athetosis, vision impairment, intellectual impairment. Each sport has sport-specific classes (e.g., wheelchair racing T51–T54 by arm function, running T11–T13 by visual impairment). Classification aims to minimize impact of impairment on outcome, so success reflects athletic talent and training.

**CC-032 — Wearable Sensors and Performance Analytics**
GPS units (5–10 Hz sampling) track distance, speed, and acceleration in field sports. Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs: accelerometers, gyroscopes, magnetometers) quantify loading, jump height, landing impact, and swimming stroke mechanics. Optical heart rate monitors (photoplethysmography, PPG) track HR and HRV. Force plates measure peak force, rate of force development (RFD), and jump mechanics. WHOOP, Oura Ring, Garmin, and Polar wearables provide 24/7 physiological monitoring; data validity varies — cadence-based heart rate during cycling (wrist PPG) is unreliable.

**CC-033 — Anti-Doping Science**
The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) Prohibited List bans anabolic steroids, EPO, growth hormone, stimulants, diuretics, and blood doping. Detection methods: IRMS (Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometry) distinguishes synthetic from endogenous hormones via ¹³C/¹²C ratio. Biological Passport (longitudinal tracking of blood parameters: hemoglobin, reticulocyte percentage) detects blood manipulation. Microdosing (small, frequent EPO doses) is harder to detect; advanced algorithms modeling passport variability are evolving countermeasures. The Athlete Biological Passport has resulted in hundreds of sanctions since 2008.

## 5. Patterns

**P-001 — Progressive Overload**
Training volume or intensity must systematically increase over time to continue driving physiological adaptation. The body adapts to a given stimulus within 3–6 weeks; without progression, performance plateaus. Progressive overload is the foundational principle of all training — applied to resistance training (increase weight, volume, or density), endurance training (increase distance, intensity, or elevation), and skill training (increase complexity or speed).

**P-002 — Train-Low, Compete-High (Glycogen Periodization)**
Strategic periodically training in a glycogen-depleted state (train-low) upregulates mitochondrial biogenesis, fat oxidation enzymes, and metabolic flexibility. Competition is performed fully fueled (compete-high). Prevents the maladaptation of training exclusively on high carbohydrate, without reducing the carbohydrate availability needed for quality high-intensity training sessions. Sleep-low (overnight glycogen depletion) is a practical implementation.

**P-003 — The Deload Week**
Planned reduction of training volume (typically 40–60%) every 4–8 weeks to allow accumulated fatigue to dissipate and supercompensation to express. Intensity is often maintained. Deload weeks coincide with the realization phase of block periodization; they are proactive fatigue management, not reactions to feeling tired. Without planned deloads, cumulative fatigue masks fitness gains and injury risk accumulates.

**P-004 — Injury Prevention via Load Management**
The acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR, Gabbett): the ratio of the previous week's training load (acute) to the rolling 4-week average (chronic). ACWR > 1.5 (spike load) is associated with significantly elevated injury risk; ACWR 0.8–1.3 is the "sweet spot." Monitoring weekly load and preventing spikes is the primary evidence-based injury prevention strategy. Session RPE × minutes is a valid low-cost load monitoring tool.

**P-005 — Optimal Rest Intervals for Adaptation**
Rest interval length should match training goal: Power and maximal strength (>85% 1RM): 3–5 minutes (full PCr resynthesis, ATP regeneration). Hypertrophy: 60–90 seconds (metabolic stress accumulation, some fatigue carries over). Muscular endurance: <60 seconds. Longer rest intervals allow higher quality reps; shorter intervals accumulate more metabolic stress per session. Meta-analyses show longer rests (3 min) produce greater strength and size gains than 60-second rests across equivalent total volume.

**P-006 — Specificity of Adaptation in Concurrent Training**
The interference effect (Hickson, 1980): combining heavy endurance and strength training in the same session or period can attenuate gains in both. Mechanism: AMPK (endurance) pathway and mTOR (strength/hypertrophy) pathway are partially antagonistic. Mitigation: separate endurance and strength sessions by ≥6 hours; prioritize the primary training goal; high-volume, low-intensity endurance is most compatible with strength training; high-intensity interval training is less interfering than long slow distance.

**P-007 — The Warm-Up Protocol**
A comprehensive warm-up: general cardiovascular activation (5 min light aerobic), dynamic stretching (leg swings, hip circles, arm circles — improves range of motion without reducing strength, unlike static pre-exercise stretching), sport-specific activation (movement patterns that match upcoming demands), and potentiation sets (sub-maximal lifts before maximal effort). Post-activation potentiation (PAP): heavy resistance effort preceding explosive movement enhances subsequent power output for 5–15 minutes.

**P-008 — Nutrition Timing: The Anabolic Window**
The "anabolic window" (post-exercise protein timing) is relevant but the window is wider than originally claimed (4–6 hours rather than 30 minutes). For athletes training fasted or >4–5 hours since the previous protein-containing meal, post-exercise protein is more time-critical. For athletes who ate a protein-containing meal 1–2 hours pre-exercise, there is no urgency. Total daily protein intake is the primary determinant of hypertrophy outcomes; timing is a secondary optimization.

**P-009 — Rate of Force Development (RFD) Training**
RFD is the speed at which force is developed — critical for sports where the ground contact time is <200 ms (sprinting, jumping, reactive cutting). Explosive strength training (Olympic lifting, jump training, sprint training, plyometrics) specifically improves early RFD (0–50 ms) via enhanced neural drive and fiber recruitment. Heavy strength training improves maximal force but not early RFD without ballistic training. Athletes need both maximum strength AND RFD for power sports.

**P-010 — Block Periodization for Advanced Athletes**
Sequential training blocks each concentrating on a specific quality: Accumulation block (4 weeks): high volume, low intensity, broad fitness base. Transmutation block (3–4 weeks): moderate volume, high intensity, converting general fitness to sport-specific fitness. Realization block (1–2 weeks): low volume, high intensity, taper — supercompensation expresses for competition. More effective than undulating periodization for advanced athletes who cannot develop all qualities simultaneously.

**P-011 — Cool-Down and Active Recovery**
Light aerobic activity post-exercise accelerates lactate clearance (lactate is metabolized as an energy substrate in active muscle, not just converted in the liver) and maintains venous return (prevents blood pooling in lower extremities). Gradual reduction in exercise intensity allows the cardiovascular system to downregulate smoothly. Post-exercise static stretching improves long-term flexibility but does not prevent DOMS; its psychological value (parasympathetic activation, mindful transition) may be the primary benefit.

**P-012 — Fatigue Management via Monitoring**
Multi-modal fatigue monitoring: subjective wellness questionnaires (Hooper Index: fatigue, sleep, stress, DOMS — each rated 1–7), HRV (morning supine, 2 min), counter-movement jump height (simple force plate or jump mat), and session RPE (15-minute post-exercise, RPE 6–20 Borg scale). Together these provide redundant indicators of accumulated fatigue and guide day-to-day training modification. No single marker is sufficient; convergent evidence from multiple sources is the evidence-based standard.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP-001 — Static Stretching Immediately Before Power Activities**
Pre-exercise static stretching (holding 30–60 seconds) acutely reduces maximal force production, rate of force development, and sprint performance by 3–8% for up to 30–60 minutes post-stretch (neural inhibition, reduced muscle stiffness). It should not precede power activities (sprinting, jumping, weightlifting). Dynamic stretching has no such acute performance detriment and is the appropriate warm-up component for these activities.

**AP-002 — Training Through Pain**
Distinguishing fatigue/discomfort from injury pain is critical: training through injury pain (sharp, localized, joint-specific, worsening with loading) accelerates structural damage and delays recovery. The adage "no pain, no gain" applies to training discomfort (burning, DOMS), not injury pain. Early diagnosis and modification of training load is the evidence-based response to acute injury; ignoring pain is the leading cause of acute-to-chronic injury transition.

**AP-003 — Excessive DOMS Chasing**
Deliberately maximizing muscle soreness (Delayed-Onset Muscle Soreness, DOMS) as a proxy for training effectiveness. DOMS results from muscle damage, primarily from eccentric exercise; it is neither necessary nor sufficient for hypertrophy. Advanced athletes experience less DOMS from equivalent training (repeated bout effect), not because their training is less effective. Training that maximizes DOMS while ignoring volume, intensity, and specificity is counterproductive.

**AP-004 — Neglecting Recovery as "Wasted Time"**
Viewing rest and recovery as nonproductive undermines the training process. Adaptation occurs during recovery, not during training (training is the stimulus; recovery is the process). Chronic underrecovery produces overtraining syndrome, injury, illness, and performance regression. High-performance programs build in 1–2 rest days/week, deload weeks, and periodized recovery blocks as non-negotiable structural components.

**AP-005 — Overemphasizing Isolation Exercises for Athletes**
Machine-based isolation exercises (leg extension, leg curl, cable cross-over) develop individual muscle strength but do not train the neuromuscular coordination patterns, proprioceptive feedback, or multi-joint movement sequences required for sport. Athletes need compound, multi-joint, free-weight exercises (squat, deadlift, pull-up, Olympic lifts) as the foundation; isolation exercises are supplementary. SAID principle: the body adapts specifically to imposed demands — sport requires integrated movement patterns.

**AP-006 — Excessive Volume for Beginners**
Beginning athletes do not need high training volume to drive adaptation — almost any training stimulus produces rapid gains through neural adaptation (Beginners' gains). Excessive volume overloads novice athletes' recovery capacity, increases injury risk, and produces no additional adaptation beyond what a lower-volume program achieves. 2–3 sets per exercise, 3 days/week is sufficient and evidence-based for novice resistance training programs.

**AP-007 — Using BMI as a Performance Health Metric for Athletes**
Body Mass Index (BMI = kg/m²) does not distinguish muscle from fat mass. Elite athletes (rugby players, heavyweight weightlifters, American football linemen) are routinely classified as "obese" by BMI despite low body fat percentages. BMI was designed as a population-level epidemiological tool; it should not be used to assess individual athlete health or performance. Body composition (DEXA, underwater weighing, or validated skinfold protocols) is the appropriate tool.

**AP-008 — Copying Elite Athlete Programs**
Elite athlete training programs are built for individuals with 10–20 years of adaptation, exceptional recovery capacity, full-time training availability, and professional support staff. Applying elite training volumes (100+ km/week for elite runners, 20+ sessions/week for elite swimmers) to amateur athletes produces injury, overtraining, and burnout. Dose-response relationships must be calibrated to the individual's training age, recovery capacity, and time constraints.

**AP-009 — Confusing Correlation with Causation in Sports Science Research**
Many sports science studies are observational and under-powered (small N, specific populations, short duration). Correlation between a training variable and performance does not establish that the variable causes the performance improvement. Controlled crossover designs, randomized controlled trials, and systematic meta-analyses provide more robust evidence than single case studies or coach testimonials. Critical appraisal of sports science evidence quality is essential before adopting interventions.

## 7. Facts

**F-001** — Eliud Kipchoge ran the first sub-2-hour marathon (1:59:40) on October 12, 2019, in Vienna (Ineos 1:59 Challenge, not under competition conditions); his VO₂max was measured at ~92 mL/kg/min, and his running economy was the most efficient ever recorded at the time.

**F-002** — The current official marathon world record is Kelvin Kiptum's 2:00:35 (Chicago, October 2023); the women's world record is Ruth Chepngetich's 2:09:56 (Chicago, October 2024).

**F-003** — Usain Bolt's 100m world record of 9.58 seconds (Berlin, 2009) equates to a peak speed of approximately 12.4 m/s (44.6 km/h), achieved approximately 65–70 m into the race after peak acceleration.

**F-004** — A Type IIx (previously called IIb) muscle fiber can contract approximately 10× faster than a Type I fiber but fatigues within seconds; a Type I fiber can sustain contraction for minutes to hours due to its high oxidative capacity.

**F-005** — The quadriceps group (rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, medialis, intermedius) is the largest muscle group in the body by mass; the gluteus maximus is the largest individual muscle; the latissimus dorsi has the greatest surface area.

**F-006** — Peak bone mineral density in humans occurs at approximately age 25–30; by age 70, most adults have lost 20–30% of their peak BMD; resistance and impact exercise can slow this loss to approximately 1–2% per decade instead of the typical 3–5%.

**F-007** — The average elite male marathon runner's body fat percentage is approximately 6–8%; the average elite female marathon runner's is approximately 10–15%; below approximately 5% (males) or 12% (females) body fat, hormonal and health consequences become clinically significant.

**F-008** — The ACWR (acute:chronic workload ratio) study by Gabbett (2016, British Journal of Sports Medicine) demonstrating increased injury risk above ACWR 1.5 is one of the most cited papers in sports injury science and has fundamentally changed load management practices in professional sport.

**F-009** — Eccentric training (controlled lowering of loads, typically 3–6 seconds) produces greater hypertrophy and strength gains per unit of mechanical work than concentric training, because higher forces can be produced at lower metabolic cost during eccentric contractions.

**F-010** — The FIFA 11+ warm-up program was shown in a cluster RCT of 2,020 female soccer players (Soligard et al., BMJ 2008) to reduce all injuries by 32% and severe injuries by 50% when implemented consistently.

**F-011** — Olympic weightlifting (snatch, clean and jerk) produces the highest power outputs of any human movement measurable: elite weightlifters generate peak power of 4,000–5,000 W during the pull phase — approximately 6–8 W/kg body weight.

**F-012** — Caffeine is the most widely consumed ergogenic substance; at doses of 3–6 mg/kg body weight, caffeine improves endurance performance by approximately 2–4% via adenosine receptor antagonism (reducing perceived effort and central fatigue) and may also improve maximal strength and power.

**F-013** — Creatine monohydrate was first described as a constituent of meat by Michel Eugène Chevreul in 1832; its performance-enhancing effects in sprint exercise were demonstrated by Harris, Söderlund, and Hultman in 1992 (Clinical Science).

**F-014** — VO₂max is typically measured via incremental maximal exercise testing (Bruce Protocol on treadmill, ramp protocol on cycle ergometer) with expired gas analysis; the highest 30-second VO₂ during the test is accepted as VO₂max if a plateau or near-plateau is observed.

**F-015** — The four major muscle injuries in sport by frequency: hamstring strain (70% of all muscle injuries in sprinting sports), quadriceps strain, groin/adductor strain, and calf strain (gastrocnemius medial head).

**F-016** — Marathon carbohydrate fueling guidelines (current consensus): 60–90 g/hr for efforts >2.5 hours, using a multiple transportable carbohydrate blend (glucose:fructose ratio 2:1 to 1:1) to utilize both SGLT1 and GLUT5 intestinal transporters and avoid GI distress from transporter saturation.

**F-017** — Altitude training camps typically occur at 2,000–2,500 m above sea level; the "live high, train low" protocol (LHTL) maximizes erythropoietic adaptation (EPO production) from altitude hypoxia while training at lower altitude for quality; it increases hemoglobin mass by approximately 3–5% over 4 weeks.

**F-018** — The anabolic hormones most relevant to training adaptation: testosterone (anabolic, increased in response to heavy compound resistance exercise), growth hormone (GH, released during sleep and high-intensity exercise), IGF-1 (mediates many GH anabolic effects), and insulin (highly anabolic in the context of carbohydrate + protein ingestion post-exercise).

**F-019** — The 2016 Rio Olympics featured the largest gender testing dispute since Caster Semenya's 2009 case; World Athletics introduced testosterone eligibility regulations for differences of sex development (DSD) athletes (hyperandrogenism) applicable to specific track events (400m–1 mile), which have been upheld by the Court of Arbitration for Sport and challenged through the European Court of Human Rights.

**F-020** — Sprint mechanics: block clearance (maximum force application against fixed block), acceleration (high force at low velocity, large shin angle from vertical), maximum velocity (~60m): high step frequency (4.5–5.0 steps/s) and stride length optimization; deceleration: inevitable in 100m, minimized by Bolt's exceptional mechanics.

**F-021** — The Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale (1970, original 6–20 scale; 0–10 modified Borg CR10 scale) correlates linearly with heart rate and other physiological markers; session-RPE (perceived effort for the full session, reported 15–30 minutes post-exercise) × session duration in minutes = training load in arbitrary units (AU).

**F-022** — DEXA (Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry) is the criterion standard for body composition assessment (fat mass, lean mass, bone mineral density) in sports science; accuracy within 1–2% body fat in controlled conditions; radiation dose is very low (~10 μSv, equivalent to ~1 hour of background radiation).

**F-023** — The sport of cycling produces some of the highest sustained human power outputs: Tour de France GC contenders average approximately 400W for 40-minute climbs; track sprint cyclists can produce >2,000 W peak power for 1–2 seconds in match sprints.

**F-024** — The stretch-shortening cycle (SSC): a countermovement prior to concentric contraction (e.g., countermovement jump vs. squat jump) enhances concentric force output by 10–30% via elastic energy storage in tendons (primarily), pre-stretch of muscle spindle potentiation, and muscle-tendon unit pre-tensing.

**F-025** — The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) recommends adults perform at least 150–300 minutes/week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity or 75–150 minutes/week of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening activities on 2+ days/week — guidelines largely consistent with WHO physical activity recommendations (2020).

**F-026** — Caffeine's ergogenic effect is primarily via adenosine A1 and A2A receptor antagonism in the brain (reducing perceived exertion and central fatigue), not via peripheral lipolysis, as previously believed; habitual caffeine consumers develop partial tolerance, though periodic abstinence can restore full ergogenic effect.

**F-027** — Deep water running (aqua jogging) maintains cardiovascular fitness and can partially replace running training during lower-limb injuries; it does not fully substitute for running-specific neuromuscular adaptations but prevents significant detraining during recovery periods.

**F-028** — The Wingate Anaerobic Test (30-second all-out cycling, Monark ergometer) measures peak anaerobic power, mean anaerobic power, and fatigue index; elite sprinters produce peak power of 15–20 W/kg; trained cyclists average ~10–13 W/kg; sedentary adults approximately 7–10 W/kg.

**F-029** — Altitude illness: acute mountain sickness (AMS) affects approximately 25% of visitors to 2,500 m and 50–85% at 4,500 m; symptoms (headache, nausea, dizziness, fatigue) are caused by cerebral and pulmonary edema from hypoxia-induced fluid shifts; acclimatization over 2–4 days at intermediate altitude prevents AMS.

**F-030** — The female athlete triad (now called RED-S: Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport): insufficient energy availability → hormonal suppression → bone stress; originally described in female athletes but now recognized in male athletes as well; long-distance runners, cyclists, wrestlers, and gymnasts are at highest risk.

**F-031** — Tibialis anterior (dorsiflexor) peak force is approximately 200 N; gluteus maximus (hip extensor) peak force approximately 1,500–2,000 N; vastus lateralis (knee extensor) approximately 4,000–6,000 N during maximal isometric effort — illustrating the enormous variation in musculoskeletal force-generating capacity across the body.

**F-032** — Rotator cuff tears affect approximately 20% of adults over age 65 and 25–30% of professional overhead athletes (baseball pitchers, swimmers, tennis players); the supraspinatus tendon is most commonly injured; the tendon's "critical zone" (poor vascularity, 1 cm from insertion) is the primary tear site.

**F-033** — Swimming efficiency (propelling efficiency, η): elite swimmers propel approximately 9–14% of the energy they expend in the water, with the remainder dissipated as heat and turbulence; cycling efficiency in experienced cyclists is approximately 20–25%; this illustrates why swimming is energetically expensive relative to cycling.

**F-034** — The Olympic decathlon comprises 10 events (100m, long jump, shot put, high jump, 400m on day 1; 110m hurdles, discus, pole vault, javelin, 1500m on day 2); scoring tables convert performance into points; the world record is Ash Anderson's 9,126 points (Eugene, 2024).

**F-035** — Foam rolling (self-myofascial release): acute effects include increased range of motion (~5–10°) without performance decrement (unlike static stretching); proposed mechanisms include neural inhibition via Golgi tendon organ stimulation and increased tissue extensibility from thixotropy (mechanical loading reduces viscoelastic stiffness of fascia temporarily). Long-term effects on muscle quality are less established.

**F-036** — Sports injury epidemiology: American football has the highest injury rate of any US sport (approximately 8.1 injuries/1000 athlete-exposures); gymnastics has the highest proportion of serious injuries; soccer produces the most total injuries by volume due to the number of participants.

**F-037** — The Fédération Internationale de Natation (FINA, now World Aquatics) banned polyurethane swimsuits in 2010 after the "supersuit era" (2008–2009) produced 43 world records in 12 months; the suits reduced drag by 5–10% and provided buoyancy that artificially inflated performance.

**F-038** — The heart rate maximum (HR_max) decreases approximately 1 beat/min/year with aging; the widely used formula HR_max = 220 − age is a rough population estimate with a standard deviation of ~10–12 beats/min; individual variation is large enough to make it unreliable for prescription without direct measurement.

**F-039** — Paralympic sports classification grew from two impairment categories (wheelchair and visually impaired) at the 1960 Rome Games to ten impairment classes and 25 sports at the Tokyo 2020 Games; the Paralympic Games attract approximately 4,400 athletes from 160 countries.

**F-040** — Tendinopathy (the continuum model, Cook & Purdam): reactive tendinopathy (acute response to load, temporary thickening) → tendon dysrepair (disorganized collagen, neovascularization) → degenerative tendinopathy (cell death, collagen disruption, calcification, irreversible in areas). Progressive tendon loading rehabilitation (heavy slow resistance training, Alfredson eccentric protocol for Achilles) is the evidence-based treatment for chronic tendinopathy.

**F-041** — Isokinetic dynamometry (Biodex, Cybex): measures muscle torque at a controlled velocity; normal hamstring:quadriceps ratio (H:Q conventional ratio) ≥ 0.6 at 60°/s; H:Q < 0.6 is associated with elevated hamstring injury risk; functional H:Q (eccentric hamstring : concentric quadriceps) ≥ 1.0 is the more sports-relevant benchmark.

**F-042** — Motor unit recruitment follows the Henneman size principle (1957): smaller, lower-threshold motor units (Type I fibers) are recruited first; as force requirement increases, larger higher-threshold motor units (Type II) are progressively recruited. Maximum voluntary contraction requires near-maximal neural drive to activate all available motor units.

**F-043** — Sport-related concussions affect approximately 3.8 million people annually in the US (CDC); 80–90% resolve within 7–10 days; persistent post-concussion syndrome (>28 days) affects approximately 10–15% of cases; second-impact syndrome (second concussion before full recovery) can cause rapid, potentially fatal cerebral edema.

**F-044** — The phosphocreatine (PCr) store in muscle (~80–100 mmol/kg dry weight) is fully depleted by approximately 8–10 seconds of maximal sprint; recovery is ~50% in 30 seconds, ~90% in 3–5 minutes — establishing the physiological rationale for rest interval duration in sprint-based training.

**F-045** — The vertical jump (counter-movement jump, CMJ) is the most commonly used field test of lower body explosive power in sports science; CMJ height correlates strongly with sprint performance, Olympic weightlifting performance, and overall athletic potential; force plate CMJ allows decomposition into force-time characteristics (impulse, peak force, RFD).

**F-046** — Glycogen supercompensation (carbohydrate loading): a 3-day very low carbohydrate diet followed by a 3-day high carbohydrate diet (10–12 g/kg/day) increases muscle glycogen by 150–200% above baseline, extending time-to-exhaustion in events >90 minutes by 2–3%. The classic 6-day depletion-repletion protocol has been simplified; 24–48 hours of very high carbohydrate intake alone achieves significant supercompensation in trained athletes.

**F-047** — The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) has a tensile strength of approximately 2,160 N in young adults, declining with age; ACL injury occurs when forces exceed this threshold during rapid deceleration, pivoting, and landing — typically through non-contact mechanisms in 70% of sport injuries.

**F-048** — Sports science research has demonstrated that structured sleep extension (8.5–10 hours/night) over 5–7 weeks improved sprint speed by 4%, serve accuracy in tennis by 10%, and free throw percentage in basketball by 9% across multiple sport-specific studies (Mah et al., various).

**F-049** — The Relative Age Effect (RAE): in youth sport, children born early in the selection year are overrepresented at elite levels due to maturation advantage; approximately 50% of elite youth athletes are born in the first quarter of the year; the RAE attenuates but does not disappear at senior elite level.

**F-050** — Muscle glycogen is the primary fuel for exercise at intensities above 65% VO₂max; the average 70 kg athlete stores approximately 400–500 g of muscle glycogen (~1,600–2,000 kcal) and approximately 90–110 g of liver glycogen; at marathon race pace (~80% VO₂max), glycogen stores are depleted in approximately 90–120 minutes, explaining "hitting the wall" at the marathon 30–35 km mark without carbohydrate supplementation.
