## 4. Core Concepts

**C01 — Textile Fiber Classification**
Definition: Textile fibers classified by origin. Natural: cellulosic (cotton, linen/flax, hemp, jute, ramie), protein (wool, silk, cashmere, alpaca, mohair, angora). Manufactured: regenerated cellulosic (viscose/rayon, lyocell/Tencel, modal, acetate), synthetic (polyester, nylon/polyamide, acrylic, polypropylene, spandex/elastane/Lycra), mineral (glass fiber, aramid, carbon fiber).
Key relationships: Fiber length (staple vs. filament), fineness (micron, denier, dtex), tenacity, elongation, moisture regain, thermal properties.

**C02 — Cotton**
Definition: Most widely used natural fiber (~50% of global fiber production). Cellulosic fiber from seed hairs of Gossypium species. Key qualities: absorbency, breathability, comfort, dyeability. Egyptian/Pima (extra-long staple, ELS, ~34+ mm): premium. Upland cotton (~26–28 mm): commodity. Organic cotton: grown without synthetic pesticides; ~1% of cotton.
Key relationships: Ginning (separating fiber from seed), fiber length (staple length), Micronaire (fineness + maturity), tensile strength, trash content, Fair Trade cotton.

**C03 — Wool and Protein Fibers**
Definition: Wool (from sheep, primarily Merino): unique scale structure → felting; natural crimp → elasticity and loft; natural flame resistance; moisture-wicking. Graded by diameter (microns): Merino <18.5 μm (ultrafine) → >30 μm (coarse). Cashmere: from Kashmir goats (<16 μm), luxury. Silk: continuous filament from Bombyx mori silkworm cocoon; naturally lustrous.
Key relationships: Wool's scale structure (felting/shrinkage risk), superwash treatment (acid + polymer coating to prevent felting), cashmere vs. merino vs. lambswool, silk sericin (removed in degumming), spider silk (extreme strength-to-weight).

**C04 — Synthetic Fibers**
Definition: Manufactured polymers drawn into fibers. Polyester (PET): most produced synthetic (~60% of synthetic fiber); high strength, low moisture absorption, wrinkle resistance, quick-dry; recycled PET (rPET) from bottles increasingly mainstream. Nylon (polyamide 6, 6.6): high strength, elasticity, abrasion resistance. Spandex/Elastane: 600%+ elongation; always blended. Acrylic: wool-like warmth; criticized for microplastic shedding.
Key relationships: Filament vs. staple synthetic fiber, texturizing (crimping filaments for bulk and softness), denier (fiber linear density: grams per 9,000m), microfiber (<1 denier), microplastic shedding (washing synthetic textiles releases microplastics).

**C05 — Yarn Production**
Definition: Fibers converted to yarn by spinning. Ring spinning: traditional, highest quality, twisted staple fibers into continuous yarn. Open-end (rotor) spinning: faster, lower cost, slightly coarser; most commodity denim. Air-jet spinning: fastest, smooth yarn, limited fiber types. Filament yarn: continuous synthetic filament, no spinning needed (textured, flat, or partially oriented). Yarn count systems: English count (Ne, cotton — higher = finer), metric count (Nm), denier/dtex (lower = finer).
Key relationships: Twist direction (S vs. Z twist), twist per inch/cm, ply (single vs. 2-ply vs. multi-ply), yarn hairiness, tenacity, elongation.

**C06 — Weaving**
Definition: Interlacing of warp (lengthwise, held on loom) and weft (crosswise, inserted by shuttle/rapier/air-jet) yarns. Basic weaves: plain (tabby, most common, stable), twill (diagonal line, used in denim 3/1 twill), satin (floating warp or weft for luster, low abrasion resistance). Complex weaves: dobby (small geometric patterns), jacquard (unlimited pattern complexity, pixel-level control).
Key relationships: Warp, weft, selvedge, thread count (ends per inch × picks per inch), cover factor, fabric hand, loom types (shuttle, rapier, projectile, air-jet, water-jet).

**C07 — Knitting**
Definition: Interlocking loops of yarn. Weft knitting (courses run widthwise): jersey (T-shirt), rib (2×2 rib), purl — made on flat or circular knitting machines; stretchy, may run. Warp knitting (wales run lengthwise): tricot, raschel — made on warp knitting machines; stable, used for lingerie, technical fabrics, nets, lace.
Key relationships: Course (row of loops), wale (column of loops), gauge (needles per inch), loop structure, stitch density, pilling tendency, knit elasticity.

**C08 — Nonwoven Fabrics**
Definition: Fabrics made by bonding fibers directly without yarn formation or weaving/knitting. Production methods: needle-punching (mechanical entanglement), spunbond (extruded and bonded filaments), meltblown (very fine fibers for filtration), wetlaid (paper-like), hydroentanglement (spunlace). Applications: medical (surgical gowns, N95 masks), hygiene (diapers), geotextiles, automotive, filtration.
Key relationships: Basis weight (g/m²), tensile strength (MD/CD), filtration efficiency, barrier properties, disposable vs. durable nonwovens, SMS (spunbond-meltblown-spunbond composites).

**C09 — Dyeing and Coloration**
Definition: Application of color to textile materials. Dye classes by fiber affinity: reactive dyes (cotton, covalent bond, wash-fast, most common for cotton), acid dyes (wool, silk, nylon), disperse dyes (polyester, require high temperature/pressure), vat dyes (indigo for denim, reduced then oxidized in fiber), direct dyes (cotton, lower washfastness).
Key relationships: Colorfastness (wash, light, rub — ISO methods), metamerism (colors match under one light, differ under another), dye exhaustion, mordant (metal salt fixing agent for natural dyes), eco-toxicology of synthetic dyes (azo dyes, REACH restricted substances).

**C10 — Textile Printing**
Definition: Application of color or pattern to fabric surface. Screen printing (rotary or flat screen): most common for mass production; each color = separate screen. Digital inkjet printing: flexible, no setup cost, unlimited colors, short run, growing rapidly. Block printing: artisanal, hand-carved blocks. Resist printing: batik (wax resist), tie-dye, shibori.
Key relationships: Screen mesh count, squeegee, ink types (water-based, discharge, plastisol for garment printing), color registration, repeat size, sublimation printing (polyester: dye converts gas → bonds with fiber).

**C11 — Textile Finishing**
Definition: Physical or chemical treatments applied post-fabric formation to modify appearance, hand, or performance. Mechanical finishes: calendering (smoothing/glazing), napping/raising (soft surface), shearing (leveling pile). Chemical finishes: mercerization (NaOH treatment of cotton → increased luster, strength, dye uptake), water repellency (DWR — durable water repellency, PFAS concerns), fire retardancy, soil release, antimicrobial, softeners.
Key relationships: PFAS (PTFE/C8/C6 chemistry in DWR, environmental persistence), nano-finishes, wrinkle resistance (resin finish, formaldehyde concerns), OEKO-TEX Standard 100 (harmful substance limits).

**C12 — Fashion Design Process**
Definition: Structured creative and technical process: trend research → concept development → design sketching → fabric selection → prototype (toile/muslin) → fitting and correction → final garment → grading → production. Collection development follows seasonal calendar (Spring/Summer, Fall/Winter) for most fashion houses.
Key relationships: Mood board, color palette, silhouette, proportion, design principles (line, shape, color, texture, pattern), creative direction, design brief.

**C13 — Pattern Making and Grading**
Definition: Pattern making: translating design into 2D flat pattern pieces that, when sewn together, create a 3D garment. Methods: flat pattern (from block/sloper), draping (directly on dress form). Grading: scaling a base pattern up and down through a size range using standardized size increments. CAD pattern systems (Gerber, Lectra) automate both.
Key relationships: Dart (controlling fabric shape), ease (allowance beyond body measurement for movement and comfort), seam allowance, notches, grain line, dress form (standard size), tolerance (cut tolerance ±2–3mm).

**C14 — Garment Construction**
Definition: Assembly of garment from pattern pieces using industrial sewing machines. Seam types: plain seam (most common), French seam (enclosed), flat-felled seam (denim), overlock/serger (finishing edges). Stitches per inch (SPI): 12–14 for medium-weight garments. Quality control: defect rate (AQL — Acceptable Quality Level), measurements, appearance.
Key relationships: Lockstitch (301), chain stitch (401), overlock (504, 516), SPI, seam strength, stitch tension, thread type (core-spun polyester for strength + cotton-feel), AQL inspection.

**C15 — Fashion History — Key Movements**
Definition: Key fashion periods: 19th century: Worth (first haute couture house, 1858); 1920s: Chanel (emancipation, jersey knit); 1947: Dior's "New Look" (hourglass silhouette revival); 1960s: Quant (miniskirt), space age, Mod; 1970s: disco, ethnic influence, denim ascendancy; 1980s: power dressing, streetwear emergence; 1990s: minimalism (Calvin Klein), grunge; 2000s: fast fashion dominance; 2010s: athleisure, sustainability awareness; 2020s: digital fashion, inclusivity, climate urgency.
Key relationships: Haute couture (custom, handmade, top tier), prêt-à-porter (ready-to-wear), mass market, streetwear, luxury tiers.

**C16 — The Global Fashion Supply Chain**
Definition: Most fashion supply chains span multiple countries: raw fiber (US cotton, Chinese polyester) → spinning (Bangladesh, India, China) → weaving/knitting (Taiwan, China, Italy) → cutting and sewing (Bangladesh, Vietnam, Cambodia, Ethiopia, Turkey) → finishing and retail (global). Supply chain visibility and traceability are major challenges.
Key relationships: CMT (Cut-Make-Trim, buyer provides fabric), FOB (Free on Board, factory sources fabric), full package (factory does everything), lead time (10–12 weeks typical fast fashion), audit (SMETA, BSCI, SA8000).

**C17 — Fast Fashion**
Definition: The business model of rapid conversion of catwalk trends into low-cost, high-volume garments with very short product lifecycles. Originated with Zara (Inditex) in the 1990s; industrialized by H&M, SHEIN, and others. Characterized by 52+ "micro-seasons"/yr, low prices driven by low wages and cheap synthetic materials, high consumption and disposal rates.
Key relationships: Trend-to-shelf time (Zara: ~3 weeks vs. traditional: 6 months), throwaway fashion, textile waste (~92 million tonnes/yr), SHEIN (100,000+ new styles/year), fashion industry environmental impact.

**C18 — Luxury Fashion**
Definition: The market segment defined by exceptional quality, craftsmanship, heritage, and exclusivity. Luxury fashion brands: Chanel, Hermès, Louis Vuitton (LVMH), Gucci (Kering), Prada. Business model: strict scarcity control, high gross margins (60–80%), aspirational marketing, heritage narratives, craft certification.
Key relationships: LVMH (Louis Vuitton Moët Hennessy, ~75 luxury brands, largest luxury group), Kering (Gucci, Saint Laurent, Bottega Veneta), exclusivity, logo value, resale market (Rebag, The RealReal), counterfeiting.

**C19 — Technical and Functional Textiles**
Definition: Textiles designed primarily for performance rather than aesthetics. Categories: protective (ballistic: Kevlar, chemical resistance, FR/arc flash), medical (wound dressings, compression, surgical implants, scaffolds for tissue engineering), geotextiles (soil stabilization, erosion control, drainage), automotive (seat belts, airbags, tire cords), aerospace, sports performance.
Key relationships: High-performance fibers (aramid/Kevlar, UHMWPE/Dyneema, carbon fiber, glass fiber, basalt fiber), moisture management, UPF (UV protection factor), antimicrobial (silver, zinc oxide), smart/e-textiles (conductive yarns, integrated electronics).

**C20 — Sustainable Fashion and Circular Economy**
Definition: Sustainable fashion seeks to reduce negative environmental and social impacts throughout the textile lifecycle. Circular economy model: design for longevity, repairability, and recyclability; maintain materials in use as long as possible; eliminate waste. Key strategies: recycled fibers (rPET, recycled cotton), natural/organic fibers, fiber-to-fiber recycling, rental/resale models, producer take-back.
Key relationships: Lifecycle assessment (LCA), cradle-to-cradle vs. cradle-to-grave, textile recycling (mechanical vs. chemical), extended producer responsibility (EPR), EU Strategy for Sustainable Textiles (2022), GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard), Higg Index.

**C21 — Textile Sustainability Standards and Certifications**
Definition: Key certifications: GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard): organic fiber + responsible processing; OEKO-TEX Standard 100: tests for harmful substances in finished products; Bluesign: chemicals, water, energy management in production; Fair Trade: social standards + price premium; Cradle to Cradle: circular design certification.
Key relationships: Greenwashing, certification scope (farm to retail vs. processing only), supply chain traceability, Textile Exchange (tracks sustainable fiber volumes), EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR).

**C22 — Color Trend Forecasting**
Definition: The systematic prediction of future color and trend directions for fashion and textiles. Key forecasting organizations: Pantone Color Institute (Color of the Year), WGSN (Worth Global Style Network), Trendalytics, Edited. Color forecasting occurs 18–24 months ahead; processes: runway analysis, cultural signals, consumer sentiment, industry consensus.
Key relationships: Pantone Matching System (PMS), NCS (Natural Color System), color psychology, colorway (coordinated group of colors), cross-category color adoption (fashion → interiors → consumer goods).

**C23 — Textile Trade and Regulation**
Definition: Global textile trade governed by: WTO Agreement on Textiles and Clothing (1995–2005, phased out quotas), Rules of Origin (determining textile nationality for preferential tariffs), REACH regulation (EU chemical restrictions), CA Prop 65 (California chemical disclosure), US Customs (fiber content labeling, country of origin marking, FTC care labeling).
Key relationships: HS codes (textile-specific chapters 50–63), GSP (Generalized System of Preferences, US preferential tariffs for developing countries), AGOA (Africa Growth and Opportunity Act), Free Trade Agreements (CAFTA, KORUS), anti-dumping duties.

**C24 — Fashion and Identity**
Definition: Clothing functions as a social communication system (Barthes, "The Fashion System"): communicating social class, profession, cultural identity, gender, and subculture affiliation. Fashion is a cultural practice expressing identity, belonging, and resistance (subcultures: punk, hip-hop, goth, etc.).
Key relationships: Semiotics (signs in fashion), fashion system (Barthes), trickle-down vs. trickle-up vs. bubble-up theory of fashion diffusion, identity theory in dress, cultural appropriation in fashion.

**C25 — Knit Structure Engineering (Technical)**
Definition: Knit fabric engineered properties: loop size, tuck stitches, float stitches, cable patterns, and jacquard programming determine stretch, recovery, compressive properties, permeability, and aesthetic. 3D knitting (whole-garment knitting: Shima Seiki WHOLEGARMENT, Stoll) produces complete garments without seams, reducing waste ~30% vs. cut-and-sew.
Key relationships: Gauge (standard: 7gg, 12gg, 16gg, 18gg industrial; finer = finer yarn), yarn count selection for gauge, full-fashioned knitting, seamless knitting, spacer fabric (double-layer knit with spacer yarns for padding/breathability).

## 5. Patterns

**P01 — Fabric Selection for End Use**
Description: Define end-use requirements (comfort, durability, care, aesthetics, performance, cost); evaluate candidate fibers and constructions against requirements matrix; consider fiber content (single fiber vs. blend), fabric weight (g/m²), construction (woven/knit), finish; order sample and test against specifications.
When to use: Product development; garment design; technical textile specification.
Example: Children's swimwear: requires chlorine resistance (nylon > polyester), quick-dry (synthetic filament), UV protection (tight construction + UPF 50+ finish), comfort (smooth knit, flat seams), colorfastness to chlorinated water → 80/20 nylon/spandex, circular knit, solution-dyed yarn.

**P02 — Dye Recipe Development**
Description: Identify dye class compatible with fiber; conduct shade matching (visual + spectrophotometer); optimize dye concentration, auxiliary chemistry (salt, soda ash for reactive; acid for protein fibers), temperature, time, and liquor ratio; assess colorfastness (ISO 105 wash, light, rub tests); optimize for process reproducibility and batch-to-batch consistency.
When to use: Color development in dye house; seasonal color adoption.
Example: Reactive dyeing of cotton: target shade = medium navy blue; dye concentration 3% owf (on weight of fabric); NaCl 80 g/L, Na₂CO₃ 20 g/L, 60°C, 60 min; wash-off with hot water + detergent; Grade 4–5 wash fastness ISO 105-C06.

**P03 — Garment Lifecycle Assessment (LCA)**
Description: Define functional unit (1 garment worn X times over Y years); collect inventory data for each lifecycle stage (raw material, fiber production, spinning, fabric, dyeing, garment, transport, consumer use, end-of-life); calculate environmental impacts (GHG, water use, chemical use, land use); identify hotspots; test alternative scenarios.
When to use: Sustainability strategy; product marketing claims; design for sustainability.
Example: Cotton T-shirt LCA: 71% of water use occurs during cotton cultivation; 60% of energy at consumer use phase (washing/drying); key interventions: organic cotton, cold-wash campaign, durable design for 2× more wears.

**P04 — Trend Research and Concept Development**
Description: Monitor runway collections (NYFW, LFW, MFW, PFW), street style, social media (TikTok, Instagram), cultural events, and macro consumer signals; identify emerging color, silhouette, texture, and cultural themes 12–18 months ahead; synthesize into seasonal concept (mood board, color palette, key items); translate to product range plan.
When to use: Seasonal collection development; trend-responsive buying.
Example: WGSN trend report S/S 2026: identifies "Coastal Grandmother" aesthetic continuing + emerging "Nomadic Utility" driven by outdoor lifestyle crossover → buyer allocates 30% of range to linen/cotton naturals, functional pockets, earth tones.

**P05 — Quality Control — AQL Inspection**
Description: Define defect categories (critical: safety; major: function/appearance; minor: cosmetic); determine AQL level (1.0, 2.5, 4.0) by defect severity; determine sample size from lot size (MIL-STD-1916 or ISO 2859); inspect sample; accept or reject lot based on acceptance number.
When to use: Finished goods inspection before shipment; incoming fabric inspection; in-line production audit.
Example: 5,000-piece T-shirt lot; AQL 2.5 major defects; sample n=200; acceptance number Ac=10; rejection number Re=11; inspect: thread pulls, seam defects, printing defects, measurement spec compliance; 8 major defects found → lot accepted.

**P06 — Supply Chain Social Audit**
Description: Conduct unannounced inspection of garment factory against social compliance standard (SMETA, SA8000, WRAP); assess working hours, wages (minimum wage compliance), health and safety, child labor, forced labor, freedom of association; document findings; require corrective action plan (CAP) for violations.
When to use: Supplier qualification; annual supplier monitoring; buyer compliance requirement.
Example: Post-Rana Plaza (2013), Accord/Alliance safety inspections covered 1,800+ Bangladesh factories; identified structural deficiencies; required remediation within 18 months; prevented repeat tragedy.

**P07 — Pattern Grading**
Description: From base pattern (typically size M or US size 10/12), apply size-specific increments to each pattern point along grade lines; maintain design proportions; produce full size range (e.g., XS–4XL or US 0–24); nest graded patterns for marker making (fabric utilization optimization).
When to use: Production scaling of any new garment style.
Example: Women's tops graded from US size 8 (base) to 2–22; chest circumference increases 2.5 cm per size; waist increases 2.5 cm; hip increases 2.5 cm; shoulder width increases 0.5 cm; sleeve length increases 1 cm per 2 sizes.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP01 — Greenwashing Fiber Claims Without Full LCA**
Why wrong: Marketing a garment as "sustainable" based on one metric (organic cotton, recycled fiber) ignores the total impact. Organic cotton uses less pesticide but similar water and land to conventional cotton. rPET saves fossil resources but still requires significant energy and sheds microplastics. No fiber is inherently sustainable.
What to do instead: Conduct or commission full lifecycle assessment; make specific, evidence-based claims; use recognized certifications (GOTS, OEKO-TEX, Bluesign) with defined scope; disclose what is and is not covered.

**AP02 — Ignoring Country-of-Origin Rules in International Trade**
Why wrong: Preferential tariff rates (GSP, AGOA, FTAs) require goods to meet rules of origin (substantial transformation — typically fabric-forward or yarn-forward). Claiming preferential origin without meeting requirements constitutes customs fraud with severe penalties.
What to do instead: Verify rules of origin for each preferential program; maintain records demonstrating compliance; consult trade counsel for complex supply chain structures; use ACE (Automated Commercial Environment) ruling letters for binding determinations.

**AP03 — Using Average Colorfastness Data as a Spec**
Why wrong: Average colorfastness of a dye class varies by shade depth, fiber batch, dye chemistry, water hardness, and processing conditions. A wash fastness grade of 4 at the dye house may degrade to grade 3 after consumer washing if processing conditions vary.
What to do instead: Specify minimum colorfastness grades; test on production samples (not lab samples); include worst-case conditions in testing protocol; require pre-production approval of colorfastness; test at multiple depths if shade family varies.

**AP04 — Pattern Making Without Fit Model**
Why wrong: Digital pattern making and computer grading without fitting on actual human bodies produces patterns that may be technically correct but commercially unwearable. Bodies are not mathematical averages; fit testing reveals issues invisible in flat pattern analysis.
What to do instead: Fit all new styles on at least one live fit model in the target size; conduct fit sessions at prototype stage; test graded extremes (smallest and largest sizes) on appropriate fit models; capture and resolve fit comments systematically.

**AP05 — Ignoring Fiber Blend Recycling End-of-Life**
Why wrong: Blended fabrics (polyester/cotton, wool/acrylic) cannot currently be recycled through standard mechanical or chemical processes without loss of both fiber components. Designing blends without considering end-of-life adds to textile waste.
What to do instead: Design for recyclability by using mono-material or easily separable blends; select blends compatible with current recycling infrastructure; participate in take-back programs; communicate care and end-of-life options to consumers.

**AP06 — Applying Luxury Brand Marketing to Mass Market Products**
Why wrong: Luxury brand value is built on enforced scarcity, heritage narrative, exceptional craftsmanship, and experiential retail — none of which translate to mass market economics. Mass luxury ("affordable luxury") often undermines the brand equity of both ends of the market.
What to do instead: Clearly position in appropriate market tier; build brand equity through consistent quality and design coherence appropriate to price point; avoid logo-only luxury signals without substance.

**AP07 — Ignoring Chemical Compliance in Export Markets**
Why wrong: Export markets have different restricted substance lists: EU REACH (chemicals, azo dyes, PFAS, heavy metals), CPSC (US children's flammability, lead, phthalates), OEKO-TEX, CA Prop 65. Products failing compliance can be detained, recalled, and result in liability. Chemical compliance varies by fiber, dye, finish, and intended use.
What to do instead: Map chemical compliance requirements for all target markets; conduct RSL (Restricted Substances List) testing on finished goods; implement MRSL (Manufacturing Restricted Substances List) for supply chain chemicals; partner with accredited testing labs (Intertek, SGS, Bureau Veritas).
