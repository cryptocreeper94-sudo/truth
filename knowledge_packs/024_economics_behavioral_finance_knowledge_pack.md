## 4. Core Concepts

**CC-001 — Scarcity and Opportunity Cost**
The foundational economic problem: resources (time, capital, labor, land) are finite relative to unlimited wants. Every choice requires forgoing the next-best alternative — the opportunity cost. Opportunity cost is explicit in every production possibility frontier (PPF), budget constraint, and capital allocation decision.

**CC-002 — Supply and Demand**
The law of demand: quantity demanded falls as price rises (ceteris paribus). The law of supply: quantity supplied rises as price rises. Equilibrium price and quantity occur where supply meets demand. Shifts in curves (income, preferences, input costs, expectations, number of producers) are distinct from movements along curves.

**CC-003 — Elasticity**
Price elasticity of demand (PED): % change in quantity demanded / % change in price. PED > 1: elastic (luxury goods, substitutes available). PED < 1: inelastic (necessities, addictive goods). Cross-price elasticity distinguishes substitutes (positive) from complements (negative). Income elasticity distinguishes normal goods (positive) from inferior goods (negative).

**CC-004 — Consumer Surplus and Producer Surplus**
Consumer surplus: difference between willingness-to-pay and actual price paid (area above price, below demand curve). Producer surplus: difference between price received and minimum acceptable price (area below price, above supply curve). Total surplus = CS + PS = social welfare measure. Deadweight loss (DWL): welfare destroyed by market distortions (taxes, price floors/ceilings, monopoly).

**CC-005 — Utility and Marginal Utility**
Utility is an ordinal measure of satisfaction from consumption. The law of diminishing marginal utility: each additional unit consumed adds less utility than the previous. Consumer equilibrium: MU₁/P₁ = MU₂/P₂ (equal marginal utility per dollar across all goods). This rationality assumption underlies standard consumer theory.

**CC-006 — Production Function and Diminishing Returns**
The production function Q = f(K, L, ...) maps inputs to output. In the short run, at least one input is fixed; diminishing marginal returns to variable inputs set in beyond a point (adding more labor to a fixed capital stock eventually adds less and less output). In the long run, all inputs are variable; economies/diseconomies of scale apply.

**CC-007 — Market Structures**
Perfect competition: many firms, homogeneous product, free entry/exit, price-taking behavior; long-run economic profit = 0. Monopoly: one firm, price-setting, DWL. Oligopoly: few interdependent firms, strategic interaction (Cournot, Bertrand, Stackelberg models). Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products, short-run profit possible, long-run profit = 0.

**CC-008 — Externalities and Market Failure**
Externalities are costs or benefits imposed on third parties not reflected in market prices. Negative externalities (pollution): markets over-produce relative to social optimum. Positive externalities (vaccination, education): markets under-produce. Pigouvian taxes/subsidies correct for externalities by internalizing social costs. Coase theorem: if property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are zero, private bargaining achieves efficiency regardless of initial rights assignment.

**CC-009 — Public Goods and Information Asymmetry**
Public goods are non-excludable and non-rival (national defense, open-source software); markets under-provide them (free-rider problem). Information asymmetry: adverse selection (hidden information before contracting — Akerlof's lemons market) and moral hazard (hidden action after contracting — insurance, principal-agent problems). Signaling (Spence) and screening are market responses.

**CC-010 — GDP and National Accounts**
Gross Domestic Product (GDP) measures the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a period. Three equivalent measurement approaches: expenditure (C + I + G + NX), income (wages + profits + rents + interest), and production (value added at each stage). Nominal GDP uses current prices; real GDP adjusts for inflation using a base-year price index.

**CC-011 — Inflation and Price Levels**
Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, measured by CPI (Consumer Price Index), PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures deflator), or PPI (Producer Price Index). Demand-pull inflation: excess aggregate demand. Cost-push inflation: supply shocks (oil prices, supply chain disruption). Hyperinflation (>50%/month, Cagan): money loses value faster than it can circulate; examples: Weimar Germany (1923), Zimbabwe (2008).

**CC-012 — Unemployment**
Types: frictional (between jobs, search), structural (skills mismatch, technological displacement), cyclical (insufficient aggregate demand in recessions). Natural rate of unemployment (NAIRU): the rate consistent with stable inflation, combining frictional and structural. Okun's Law: a 1 percentage point rise in unemployment corresponds to approximately 2% drop in real GDP.

**CC-013 — Business Cycles**
Alternating periods of expansion (rising GDP, falling unemployment) and contraction/recession (two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth, NBER's more nuanced definition). Driven by aggregate demand shocks, supply shocks, credit cycles, and technological waves. Leading indicators: stock prices, housing starts, yield curve. Lagging indicators: unemployment, CPI.

**CC-014 — Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply (AD-AS Model)**
Aggregate demand (AD): negative relationship between price level and real output (wealth effect, interest rate effect, exchange rate effect). Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS): positive relationship between price level and output (sticky wages/prices). Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS): vertical at potential output. AD shocks cause cyclical fluctuations; supply shocks shift SRAS, causing stagflation (1970s oil shocks).

**CC-015 — Monetary Policy**
Central bank tools: policy interest rate (Fed Funds Rate, ECB Deposit Rate), open market operations (buying/selling government securities), reserve requirements, and quantitative easing (QE — asset purchase programs expanding the money supply beyond the zero lower bound). Transmission mechanism: policy rate → market rates → investment/consumption → output/inflation. Taylor Rule: prescribes policy rate as a function of output gap and inflation gap.

**CC-016 — Fiscal Policy**
Government spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand. Expansionary: increase spending or cut taxes (deficit spending, Keynesian stimulus). Contractionary: cut spending or raise taxes. Fiscal multiplier: the ratio of change in GDP to change in government spending; multipliers exceed 1 when the economy is below potential and the central bank does not offset. Ricardian equivalence: if consumers are fully forward-looking, deficit spending has no demand effect (Barro).

**CC-017 — Monetary Theory: Quantity Theory and Monetarism**
Fisher equation: MV = PQ (money supply × velocity = price level × real output). Monetarism (Friedman): velocity is stable; therefore, money growth drives inflation with a long and variable lag. Central prescription: a constant money growth rule rather than discretionary policy. Challenged by unstable velocity in practice (especially post-GFC QE).

**CC-018 — Bond Markets and Interest Rates**
A bond is a fixed-income instrument paying periodic coupons and par value at maturity. Bond price and yield are inversely related: as yields rise, prices fall, and vice versa. Yield curve: plot of yields vs. maturity. Normal: upward sloping. Inverted: short rates > long rates; has preceded every U.S. recession since the 1950s. Duration measures interest rate sensitivity: 1% yield rise causes a bond price decline ≈ its modified duration in percent.

**CC-019 — Equity Markets and Valuation**
Equity represents ownership claims on firm assets and earnings. Fundamental valuation: Dividend Discount Model (DDM), Discounted Cash Flow (DCF), price-to-earnings (P/E), price-to-book. Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH, Fama): prices instantly reflect all available information (weak, semi-strong, strong forms). Empirical anomalies (momentum, value premium, small-cap premium) challenge strong-form EMH.

**CC-020 — Derivatives**
Financial instruments whose value derives from an underlying asset. Options: right (not obligation) to buy (call) or sell (put) at a strike price by expiration; priced via Black-Scholes-Merton. Futures: obligation to buy/sell at a future date and price; priced via cost-of-carry. Swaps: exchange of cash flow streams (interest rate, currency, credit default swaps). Derivatives can hedge risk or amplify speculation.

**CC-021 — Risk and Return**
Expected return must compensate for risk. Systematic risk (beta, correlated with the market) cannot be diversified away and commands a risk premium (CAPM: E[R] = Rf + β(E[Rm] − Rf)). Idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away in a portfolio. Sharpe Ratio: (return − risk-free rate) / standard deviation — a measure of risk-adjusted return. Volatility is not the same as fundamental uncertainty (Knight's distinction).

**CC-022 — Behavioral Economics: Prospect Theory**
Kahneman and Tversky (1979): people evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point (not absolute wealth), are loss-averse (losses loom larger than equivalent gains, coefficient ~2.25), and exhibit probability weighting (overweighting small probabilities, underweighting large probabilities). Prospect theory replaces expected utility theory as a descriptive model of risky choice.

**CC-023 — Cognitive Biases in Economic Decision-Making**
Key biases: anchoring (over-reliance on first information presented), availability heuristic (judging probability by ease of recall), representativeness heuristic (ignoring base rates), overconfidence (excessive certainty in own judgments), confirmation bias (seeking confirming evidence), sunk cost fallacy (weighting past irrecoverable costs in future decisions), framing effects (choice depends on presentation).

**CC-024 — Nudge Theory and Choice Architecture**
Thaler and Sunstein (2008): the arrangement of choices (defaults, order, framing) significantly affects decisions without restricting options ("libertarian paternalism"). Default enrollment in pension plans dramatically increases participation. Organ donation opt-out (presumed consent) countries have dramatically higher donation rates than opt-in countries, demonstrating default power.

**CC-025 — Time Inconsistency and Hyperbolic Discounting**
Individuals exhibit present bias: they discount the near future more steeply than the distant future (hyperbolic rather than exponential discounting), leading to inconsistent plans (preferring $110 in 31 days over $100 in 30 days, but preferring $100 now over $110 tomorrow). Commitment devices (Ulysses contracts) exploit this by precommitting to future behavior.

**CC-026 — Mental Accounting**
People partition money into separate mental accounts (rent, entertainment, emergency) and treat money differently depending on its account, violating fungibility. Windfall gains are more readily spent; losses in one account are not offset by gains in another. Mental accounting explains the house-money effect, the sunk cost fallacy, and certain tax refund spending patterns.

**CC-027 — International Trade and Comparative Advantage**
Ricardo's principle: a country benefits from specializing in goods where it has a comparative (not absolute) advantage, even if it is less efficient at producing all goods absolutely. Trade enables global production efficiency gains. Trade liberalization creates aggregate gains but distributional losers (Stolper-Samuelson: abundant factor gains, scarce factor loses), creating political economy tensions.

**CC-028 — Exchange Rates and Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)**
Nominal exchange rate: price of one currency in terms of another. Real exchange rate: adjusted for relative price levels. Purchasing power parity (PPP): exchange rates should equalize price levels across countries in the long run (law of one price). Deviations from PPP persist due to non-traded goods and services. The Big Mac Index (Economist) is a lighthearted PPP benchmark.

**CC-029 — Development Economics**
Studies economic growth and poverty in low- and middle-income countries. Solow growth model: steady-state growth driven by technological progress. Human capital (education, health), institutional quality, infrastructure, and geographic factors are empirical determinants of long-run growth. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) by Banerjee, Duflo, and Kremer (Nobel 2019) brought experimental methods to development economics.

**CC-030 — Inequality and the Gini Coefficient**
The Gini coefficient measures income or wealth inequality on a scale from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximum inequality). The Lorenz curve plots cumulative income share vs. population share; the Gini is twice the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality. Global wealth inequality (Gini ~0.90) exceeds income inequality (~0.65).

**CC-031 — Central Banking and Reserve Management**
Central banks manage monetary policy, financial stability, and often payment systems. The Federal Reserve operates a dual mandate (maximum employment and stable prices); the ECB has a primary price stability mandate. Reserve requirements (largely phased out), open market operations, and the discount window are the classic toolbox; QE, forward guidance, and negative rates extended it post-2008.

**CC-032 — Systemic Risk and Financial Contagion**
Systemic risk: the risk that failure of one institution or market destabilizes the entire financial system. Contagion channels: counterparty credit risk (AIG 2008), fire sales (deleveraging lowers asset prices, triggering further margin calls), funding liquidity freezes (repo market stress). Macroprudential regulation (capital buffers, stress testing, SIFI designation) targets systemic risk.

**CC-033 — Incentive Design and Principal-Agent Problem**
A principal (employer, regulator, investor) delegates to an agent (employee, firm, manager) whose interests may diverge. Solutions: performance-based pay, monitoring, bonding. Poorly designed incentives produce perverse outcomes: sales commissions incentivizing mis-selling (Wells Fargo 2016), pay-per-service medicine incentivizing over-treatment, VaR-based risk limits incentivizing tail risk accumulation.

**CC-034 — Auction Theory**
Auctions allocate goods among competing bidders. Types: English (ascending open), Dutch (descending open), sealed-bid first-price, sealed-bid second-price (Vickrey). Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanisms are incentive-compatible: bidders' dominant strategy is truthful revelation. Revenue equivalence theorem: under standard assumptions, all four auction formats yield equal expected revenue. Spectrum auctions and ad-exchange real-time bidding apply auction theory at scale.

**CC-035 — Monetary Union and Optimal Currency Area (OCA)**
An optimal currency area (Mundell, 1961) requires high factor mobility, integrated fiscal transfers, and synchronized business cycles to function without independent monetary policy. The Eurozone's design tension — monetary union without fiscal union — manifested in the European sovereign debt crisis (2010–2012) as peripheral economies lacked adjustment tools.

## 5. Patterns

**P-001 — Mean Reversion**
Asset prices, economic growth rates, and corporate profit margins tend to revert toward long-run averages. Exceptionally high profit margins attract competitors; recessions are followed by recoveries; extreme valuations revert. Mean reversion has predictive power at long horizons even when short-run prices follow a random walk.

**P-002 — Yield Curve Inversion as Recession Predictor**
An inverted yield curve (short-term rates exceeding long-term rates) has preceded every U.S. recession since the 1950s with a lead time of 6–18 months. The mechanism: inversion reflects market expectation of future rate cuts (i.e., expected future economic weakness) and tightens bank net interest margins, reducing lending.

**P-003 — Boom-Bust Credit Cycles**
Credit expansion fuels asset price booms; when credit contracts (Minsky Moment), asset prices collapse, and borrowers face distress. Hyman Minsky's financial instability hypothesis: stability breeds instability as rising confidence leads to progressively riskier financing (hedge → speculative → Ponzi finance).

**P-004 — Crowding Out**
Government borrowing in a non-accommodating monetary environment raises interest rates, reducing private investment ("crowding out"). Degree depends on spare capacity, central bank response, and openness of the capital account. In liquidity trap conditions (zero lower bound), crowding out is minimal.

**P-005 — Price Stickiness and Menu Costs**
Prices adjust slowly to shocks due to menu costs (literal costs of changing prices), long-term contracts, implicit contracts with customers, and coordination failures. Price stickiness is the foundation of Keynesian macro models: nominal demand shocks have real effects in the short run.

**P-006 — The Equity Risk Premium (ERP)**
Equities historically return approximately 5–7% per year above the risk-free rate (ERP) as compensation for bearing equity risk. The ERP varies over time with investor risk aversion, macroeconomic uncertainty, and valuation levels. Negative real bond yields in the 2010s pushed investors up the risk spectrum ("TINA: There Is No Alternative").

**P-007 — Network Effects and Platform Economics**
Markets with network effects: the value to each user increases with total users (Metcalfe's Law). This creates winner-take-most dynamics, high switching costs, and tipping points. Platform businesses (marketplaces, social networks, payment systems) exhibit multi-sided network effects, creating especially powerful lock-in.

**P-008 — Race to the Bottom in Regulatory Arbitrage**
Firms relocate to jurisdictions with lighter regulation (tax, labor, environmental) to gain competitive advantage, pressuring jurisdictions to lower standards. Countered by international regulatory coordination (Basel capital accords, OECD BEPS anti-tax-avoidance rules, EU carbon border adjustment).

**P-009 — Moral Hazard in Too-Big-to-Fail**
Institutions perceived as too systemically important to fail take excessive risks, knowing they will be rescued. Deposit insurance creates depositor moral hazard (no incentive to monitor banks). Implicit TBTF guarantees create bank moral hazard. Responses: higher capital requirements, bail-in mechanisms (BRRD), living wills, resolution frameworks.

**P-010 — Anchoring in Wage and Price Setting**
Inflation expectations are self-fulfilling: if workers expect 5% inflation and bargain accordingly, firms raise prices 5%, validating the expectation. Central bank credibility anchors inflation expectations, reducing the output cost of disinflation. De-anchoring (1970s) required severe recessions (Volcker shock) to restore price stability.

**P-011 — Diversification and Correlation Breakdown**
Portfolio diversification reduces idiosyncratic risk only when asset correlations are low. In crises, correlations spike toward 1 as all risky assets sell off simultaneously (a "risk-off" event). This correlation instability undermines the reliability of risk models calibrated to normal periods for tail risk scenarios.

**P-012 — Comparative Advantage and Gains from Specialization**
Even within a firm or individual, comparative advantage implies gains from specialization and exchange. A CEO who types faster than their assistant should still delegate typing if the opportunity cost of CEO time is higher. This principle scales from individual specialization to international trade, making it one of economics' most powerful and counterintuitive ideas.

**P-013 — Tragedy of the Commons**
Shared resources without private property rights or governance are over-exploited (Hardin, 1968). Ostrom (Nobel 2009) showed communities can self-organize sustainable commons governance through rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions, challenging Hardin's pessimism and the binary of privatization vs. government regulation.

**P-014 — Rational Expectations and Policy Ineffectiveness**
Lucas critique (1976): econometric models built on past behavior break down when policies change because rational agents adjust behavior in response to anticipated policy. New Classical economics: systematic monetary policy is ineffective because rational agents anticipate and offset it; only surprises have real effects. This challenged Keynesian demand management.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP-001 — The Lump of Labor Fallacy**
The belief that there is a fixed amount of work to be distributed — hence automation or immigration "steals jobs." In reality, productivity growth and population growth create new demands and new jobs. Employment is a flow, not a stock; the fallacy ignores general equilibrium effects.

**AP-002 — Confusing Nominal and Real Values**
Failing to adjust for inflation when comparing economic values across time. Nominal GDP growth of 8% during 6% inflation represents only 2% real growth. Policy errors result from responding to nominal rather than real signals.

**AP-003 — Ignoring Externalities in Cost-Benefit Analysis**
Evaluating projects only on private costs and benefits, ignoring social costs (pollution, congestion, public health) and social benefits (spillovers, knowledge diffusion). Leads to over-production of negative externality goods and under-investment in positive externality activities.

**AP-004 — The Zero-Sum Fallacy**
Believing all economic exchange is zero-sum: one party gains only at the other's expense. Voluntary trade is positive-sum (both parties gain or they would not trade). GDP growth is not zero-sum; trade surpluses of one country need not imply deficits are harmful. Mercantilist policies flow from this fallacy.

**AP-005 — The Gambler's Fallacy in Financial Markets**
Believing that past independent random outcomes influence future probabilities: "the market has fallen 10 days in a row, it must rise tomorrow." In efficient markets, past price changes do not predict future changes (random walk). Behavioral finance traders who exploit perceived patterns pay transaction costs for illusory signals.

**AP-006 — Correlation as Causation in Economic Policy**
Observing that two variables move together and inferring a causal relationship sufficient to justify policy. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths are correlated (both driven by summer). Establishing causal economic relationships requires natural experiments, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, or randomized controlled trials.

**AP-007 — Overconfidence in Economic Models**
Treating economic models as descriptions of reality rather than as simplified tools. The 2008 financial crisis exposed the fragility of DSGE models and VaR-based risk management calibrated to pre-crisis data. All economic models fail outside their calibration domain; epistemic humility requires stress testing across scenarios.

**AP-008 — Sunk Cost Decision-Making**
Continuing to invest in failing projects because of past (irrecoverable) investment rather than expected future return. Companies continue unprofitable product lines, governments continue failed infrastructure projects, and investors hold losing stocks, all because of sunk costs. Future decisions should depend only on marginal costs and benefits.

**AP-009 — The Fixed Pie Assumption in Negotiation**
Assuming that negotiation is purely distributive (one party's gain is the other's loss), missing integrative (mutually beneficial) trade opportunities. Finding complementary interests — different priorities across issues — creates value that a zero-sum framing destroys.

## 7. Facts

**F-001** — World GDP (PPP) reached approximately $164 trillion in 2023 (IMF); the United States, China, and the European Union together account for roughly 55% of global PPP-adjusted GDP.

**F-002** — The 2008 Global Financial Crisis erased approximately $10 trillion in U.S. household wealth, led to 8.7 million job losses, and required $700 billion in TARP bailout authority (though most was repaid).

**F-003** — The U.S. Federal Reserve's balance sheet expanded from ~$900 billion before the 2008 GFC to ~$8.9 trillion at its 2022 peak following multiple rounds of QE.

**F-004** — Adam Smith's "The Wealth of Nations" (1776) introduced the concept of the invisible hand and division of labor with the famous pin factory example, laying the foundation for classical economics.

**F-005** — The Black-Scholes-Merton options pricing model (1973) won the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economics for Fischer Black (posthumous), Myron Scholes, and Robert Merton; it assumes log-normal returns, constant volatility, and no transaction costs.

**F-006** — Kahneman and Tversky's Prospect Theory paper (1979, Econometrica) is one of the most cited papers in economics, with over 60,000 citations; Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002 (Tversky died in 1996).

**F-007** — The top 10% of households in the U.S. own approximately 69% of total household wealth (Federal Reserve 2023 Distributional Financial Accounts); the top 1% own approximately 31%.

**F-008** — The Gini coefficient for income in the U.S. is approximately 0.49 (2022, Census Bureau), placing it among the highest inequality levels of developed economies; Nordic countries range 0.25–0.30.

**F-009** — China's real GDP grew at an average of approximately 9.5% per year from 1978 to 2018, lifting approximately 800 million people out of extreme poverty — the largest poverty reduction in human history.

**F-010** — The Eurozone sovereign debt crisis (2010–2015) saw Greek 10-year bond yields peak at approximately 44% (March 2012) as markets priced in high probability of default; three bailout programs totaling ~€288 billion were required.

**F-011** — The U.S. CPI increased by 9.1% year-on-year in June 2022, the highest since November 1981, driven by supply chain disruptions, energy price shocks, and post-pandemic demand surges.

**F-012** — The 1997 Asian financial crisis began with Thailand's baht devaluation (July 1997) and spread to Indonesia, South Korea, and Malaysia, causing GDP contractions of 10–14% in the most affected economies.

**F-013** — The Volcker Shock (1979–1982): Fed Chair Paul Volcker raised the Fed Funds Rate to 20% to break double-digit inflation, triggering the deepest U.S. recession since the Great Depression (unemployment peaked at 10.8%), but successfully reduced inflation from >13% to ~3%.

**F-014** — The Great Depression (1929–1933) saw U.S. GDP fall by approximately 30%, unemployment rise to 25%, and approximately 9,000 banks fail; Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued in A Monetary History of the United States (1963) that Fed policy mistakes turned a recession into depression.

**F-015** — Elinor Ostrom was the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Economics (2009) for her work demonstrating that communities can sustainably manage common pool resources without privatization or government control.

**F-016** — The Efficient Market Hypothesis in its semi-strong form implies that fundamental analysis cannot consistently generate alpha; Fama won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2013 jointly with Shiller, who demonstrated predictability of long-run returns via valuation ratios (CAPE), creating productive tension.

**F-017** — Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway has compounded at approximately 19.8% annually from 1965 to 2023 (vs. 9.9% for the S&P 500 including dividends), representing a case study that selective fundamental value investing can persistently beat the index over very long horizons.

**F-018** — The S&P 500 has delivered a total real return (inflation-adjusted, dividends reinvested) of approximately 6.5–7% per year over the past century, the primary empirical basis for the equity risk premium.

**F-019** — The 1944 Bretton Woods Conference established the IMF, World Bank, and the gold-dollar exchange standard (USD fixed to gold at $35/oz); Nixon ended dollar convertibility to gold in August 1971 (Nixon Shock), transitioning the world to fiat floating exchange rates.

**F-020** — The Cantillon Effect: newly created money does not raise all prices simultaneously; those who receive it first (banks, financial institutions) benefit before prices rise, distributing QE's costs to holders of existing money via purchasing power dilution.

**F-021** — Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe (2007–2008) peaked at an estimated 89.7 sextillion percent per month (November 2008, CATO Institute), requiring Z$100 trillion notes; the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned in 2009 in favor of a multi-currency system.

**F-022** — The Nobel Prize in Economics (formally the Sveriges Riksbank Prize) was first awarded in 1969 to Ragnar Frisch and Jan Tinbergen; it was not among Nobel's original prizes established in 1895.

**F-023** — The Laffer Curve illustrates that tax revenue is zero at both 0% and 100% tax rates, with a peak at some intermediate rate; the curve is theoretically correct but empirically contested regarding where current rates sit relative to the revenue-maximizing rate.

**F-024** — The World Trade Organization (WTO) replaced GATT in 1995 and adjudicates trade disputes; average applied tariff rates have fallen from approximately 40% in 1947 to approximately 6% globally by 2023 due to successive trade liberalization rounds.

**F-025** — Purchasing power parity-adjusted GDP per capita in the U.S. is approximately $80,000 (2023); the global average is approximately $21,000; the poorest countries (Burundi, South Sudan) are below $1,000.

**F-026** — The Okun's Law coefficient (output gap per percentage point of unemployment) has been estimated empirically at approximately 2 for the U.S., though it varies across countries and time periods.

**F-027** — The marginal propensity to consume (MPC) out of transitory income is approximately 0.2–0.5 empirically (lower than Keynesian theory of near-1), higher for liquidity-constrained households; MPC out of permanent income changes is higher (closer to 1 per Friedman's permanent income hypothesis).

**F-028** — Behavioral experiments show that approximately 75% of people prefer $100 certain over a 50% chance of $200, demonstrating risk aversion; the same people often prefer a 50% chance of losing $200 over a certain loss of $100, demonstrating loss aversion asymmetry.

**F-029** — The default enrollment effect in U.S. 401(k) plans: automatic enrollment (opt-out default) raises participation rates from approximately 50–60% to over 90%, with no change in the contribution rate for those who were already participating (Thaler & Benartzi, Save More Tomorrow).

**F-030** — The equity home bias puzzle: investors hold disproportionately more domestic equities than optimal diversification would prescribe, despite well-documented benefits of international diversification; behavioral familiarity bias, information asymmetry, and regulatory factors contribute.

**F-031** — The Fisher Effect: nominal interest rate = real interest rate + expected inflation. If monetary policy raises nominal rates 1%, real rates rise in the short run (sticky prices); in the long run, only the inflation component adjusts (Fisher neutrality of money).

**F-032** — The U.S. federal minimum wage has been $7.25/hour since 2009; adjusted for inflation, it peaked in 1968 at approximately $12.50 in 2023 dollars. Most empirical research on moderate minimum wage increases finds small disemployment effects, with larger effects for very large increases.

**F-033** — Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) proposes that currency-issuing sovereigns cannot involuntarily default in their own currency and face an inflation constraint rather than a fiscal constraint; it is a heterodox position rejected by mainstream macroeconomics as incomplete in its treatment of inflation dynamics.

**F-034** — The bond market is approximately $130 trillion globally (ICMA 2023), larger than the global equity market (~$100 trillion), making it the largest financial market in the world.

**F-035** — The 2021 meme stock episode (GameStop): retail investors coordinating via Reddit's r/WallStreetBets drove GME shares from ~$20 to ~$483, causing approximately $6 billion in losses for short-sellers and highlighting the social dynamics of modern retail investing.

**F-036** — Carbon pricing covers approximately 23% of global greenhouse gas emissions as of 2023 (World Bank State and Trends of Carbon Pricing), with prices ranging from <$1/ton in some jurisdictions to over $130/ton in the EU ETS.

**F-037** — The Mundell-Fleming model shows that a small open economy cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and independent monetary policy (the "impossible trinity" or Trilemma).
