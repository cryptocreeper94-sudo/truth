## 4. Core Concepts

**CC-001 — Probability Space and Basic Probability**
A probability space (Ω, ℱ, P): sample space Ω (set of all possible outcomes), σ-algebra ℱ (events), probability measure P satisfying: P(∅) = 0, P(Ω) = 1, countable additivity. Addition rule: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). Multiplication rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A). Independence: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). Complementary events: P(A') = 1 − P(A). Conditional probability: P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B). Law of total probability: P(A) = Σ P(A|Bᵢ)P(Bᵢ) for a partition {Bᵢ}.

**CC-002 — Bayes' Theorem**
P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B). In statistical inference: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. P(H|data) = P(data|H) × P(H) / P(data). Bayes' theorem inverts conditional probability: if we know the probability of observing data given a hypothesis, and we have a prior belief about the hypothesis, we can compute the probability of the hypothesis given the data. Base rate neglect (the classic failure): ignoring P(H) in favor of P(data|H) leads to systematic overestimation of positive test results for rare conditions (Bayes' theorem with low prior dramatically changes posterior).

**CC-003 — Random Variables and Distributions**
A random variable X maps outcomes to real numbers. PMF (probability mass function) for discrete: P(X = x). PDF (probability density function) for continuous: f(x), where P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫_a^b f(x)dx. CDF (cumulative distribution function): F(x) = P(X ≤ x). Expected value (mean): E[X] = Σ x P(X=x) or ∫ x f(x)dx. Variance: Var(X) = E[(X−μ)²] = E[X²] − (E[X])². Standard deviation σ = √Var(X). Moments: E[Xⁿ] (nth raw moment); MGF (moment generating function) M(t) = E[e^{tX}] generates all moments via derivatives.

**CC-004 — Key Probability Distributions**
Discrete: Bernoulli(p) — single trial; Binomial(n,p) — k successes in n trials, mean np, variance np(1-p); Poisson(λ) — rare events, P(X=k) = e^{-λ}λᵏ/k!, mean = variance = λ; Negative Binomial — number of failures before rth success; Geometric — number of trials until first success. Continuous: Normal N(μ,σ²) — the central distribution; Exponential(λ) — time between Poisson events, memoryless; Gamma(α,β) — sum of α exponentials; Beta(α,β) — on [0,1], used as Bayesian prior for probabilities; Uniform[a,b]; t-distribution (heavy-tailed Normal, used for small samples); F-distribution (ratio of chi-squares, used in ANOVA/regression); Chi-square (sum of squared normals, used in goodness-of-fit).

**CC-005 — The Normal Distribution**
N(μ, σ²): symmetric bell-shaped distribution; empirical rule: 68% of data within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ. Standard normal Z ~ N(0,1): Z = (X − μ)/σ. Normality is not universal: income, many biological measurements, and extreme events follow non-normal distributions; the normal is the limiting distribution of sample means (CLT) not necessarily of individual observations. Tests for normality: Shapiro-Wilk (best for small n), Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Q-Q plot (visual). Why normal: (1) CLT; (2) maximum entropy distribution given fixed mean and variance; (3) mathematical tractability.

**CC-006 — Sampling and Estimation**
A sample is a subset of the population used to make inferences. Probability sampling methods: simple random sampling (SRS), stratified (population divided into strata, sample from each), cluster (sample clusters, then sample within clusters), systematic (every kth element). Sample statistics as estimators of population parameters: X̄ estimates μ; S² estimates σ². Bias: E[estimator] − parameter (unbiased: bias = 0; S² is unbiased for σ², σ² calculated with n in denominator is biased). Efficiency: lower variance estimator is more efficient. Consistency: estimator converges in probability to true value as n → ∞. Mean Squared Error (MSE) = Bias² + Variance — the bias-variance tradeoff for estimators.

**CC-007 — Confidence Intervals**
A 95% confidence interval (CI) for μ: X̄ ± z_{0.025} × σ/√n (known σ) or X̄ ± t_{n-1, 0.025} × S/√n (unknown σ). Correct interpretation: if we repeated the sampling procedure many times, approximately 95% of the computed CIs would contain the true parameter. Incorrect interpretations: "there is a 95% chance μ is in this specific interval" (μ is fixed, not random in frequentist statistics); "95% of observations are in this interval" (that's a prediction interval). Width is inversely proportional to √n (doubling precision requires 4× sample size). Confidence intervals for proportions: Wilson interval (superior to normal approximation for small n and extreme proportions).

**CC-008 — Hypothesis Testing**
Null hypothesis (H₀) vs. alternative hypothesis (H₁). Test statistic: a function of the data that measures departure from H₀. P-value: P(observing data this extreme or more | H₀ true). Decision rule: reject H₀ if p < α (significance level, usually 0.05). Type I error (false positive, α): rejecting H₀ when it's true. Type II error (false negative, β): failing to reject H₀ when it's false. Power = 1 − β: probability of correctly rejecting H₀. Sample size calculation: given α, power, and effect size, determine n needed. One-tailed vs. two-tailed tests: use two-tailed unless there is a strong scientific reason to hypothesize direction in advance.

**CC-009 — Common Hypothesis Tests**
t-test: one-sample (test μ = μ₀), two-sample independent (test μ₁ = μ₂), paired (test differences). Assumptions: normality (or large n for CLT), independence, for two-sample: equal or unequal variance (Welch's t-test for unequal). Chi-square test: goodness of fit (observed vs. expected frequencies) and test of independence (contingency table). F-test (ANOVA): tests equality of multiple means simultaneously; protects against inflation of Type I error from multiple pairwise t-tests. Mann-Whitney U test: nonparametric analog of two-sample t-test. Wilcoxon signed-rank: nonparametric paired test. Kruskal-Wallis: nonparametric ANOVA.

**CC-010 — Linear Regression**
Model: Y = β₀ + β₁X₁ + ... + β_p X_p + ε; ε ~ N(0, σ²). OLS (ordinary least squares): minimizes Σ(y_i − ŷ_i)² — BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator) under Gauss-Markov assumptions. The Gauss-Markov assumptions: linearity, strict exogeneity (E[ε|X] = 0 — no omitted variables, no measurement error), no perfect multicollinearity, homoscedasticity (constant variance). R²: proportion of variance in Y explained by the model; adjusted R²: penalizes for adding predictors; R² ≠ a measure of fit quality in all contexts (a high R² model can make terrible predictions out-of-sample). F-test for overall significance: tests H₀: all βᵢ = 0 (except β₀).

**CC-011 — Logistic Regression**
For binary outcomes (Y ∈ {0,1}). Model: log(p/(1-p)) = β₀ + β₁X₁ + ... + β_p X_p (log-odds = linear predictor). p = 1/(1 + e^{−η}) (sigmoid function). Estimated by MLE (maximum likelihood estimation), not OLS (no closed-form solution; uses iterative algorithms, e.g., Newton-Raphson, IRLS). Interpretation: eᵝ = odds ratio — for a one-unit increase in X, the odds of Y=1 multiply by eᵝ. Assumptions: binary outcome, observations independent, no perfect multicollinearity, linearity of log-odds in predictors, large sample (for MLE asymptotic validity). Deviance and AIC for model comparison.

**CC-012 — Bayesian Inference**
Posterior distribution: P(θ|data) ∝ L(data|θ) × P(θ). The posterior summarizes uncertainty about the parameter θ after seeing the data. Prior distribution P(θ): encodes prior knowledge; conjugate priors (Beta for Bernoulli/Binomial; Normal-Inverse-Gamma for Normal; Dirichlet for Multinomial) give analytically tractable posteriors. Credible interval (Bayesian CI): the interval containing θ with specified posterior probability — direct probability statement about parameter (unlike frequentist CI). Bayesian model comparison: Bayes factors (ratio of marginal likelihoods); BIC approximates the log Bayes factor. MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo): Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs sampling — generates samples from the posterior when it is analytically intractable.

**CC-013 — Multiple Testing**
When testing m hypotheses simultaneously, the probability of at least one false positive (family-wise error rate, FWER) inflates dramatically: FWER = 1 − (1−α)^m (for independent tests); at α = 0.05 and m = 20, FWER ≈ 64%. Bonferroni correction: use α/m as threshold — conservative but controls FWER. False discovery rate (FDR): Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controls the expected proportion of false positives among rejections — less conservative, preferred in high-throughput settings (genomics: ~10⁶ SNP tests). Multiple testing is the primary driver of the reproducibility crisis in psychology, medicine, and social science — thousands of hypothesis tests (including unreported ones) produce false positives that appear as scientific discoveries.

**CC-014 — Experimental Design**
Randomized controlled experiment: the gold standard for causal inference. Key design features: Randomization (eliminates confounding); Control group (provides counterfactual); Blinding (reduces measurement bias: single-blind patient, double-blind patient+assessor, triple-blind patient+assessor+analyst); Replication (sufficient sample for statistical power). Factorial designs: test multiple factors simultaneously; 2ᵏ factorial design tests k factors at 2 levels; fractional factorial designs (k factors in 2^{k-p} runs) are efficient for screening. Blocking: control for known nuisance variables by stratifying the experimental units (randomized complete block design). Crossover design: each subject receives both treatments (controls for between-subject variability).

**CC-015 — Causal Inference**
The fundamental problem of causal inference: the same unit cannot be observed under both treatment and control — we observe only the factual, not the counterfactual. Rubin's potential outcomes framework: Y_i(1) (outcome if treated), Y_i(0) (outcome if not treated); individual treatment effect = Y_i(1) − Y_i(0) (never observed); ATE = E[Y(1) − Y(0)]. Identification assumptions: ignorability (no unmeasured confounders — SUTVA + conditional independence given observables). Methods for observational data: propensity score matching/weighting, regression adjustment, difference-in-differences (DiD), instrumental variables (IV), regression discontinuity (RD), interrupted time series, synthetic control. Pearl's do-calculus: formal framework for identifying causal effects from causal graphs (DAGs).

**CC-016 — Observational Study Confounding**
Confounding: a third variable C is a cause of both the exposure X and the outcome Y; the association between X and Y partly or wholly reflects the C-X and C-Y relationships rather than a causal X→Y relationship. The back-door criterion (Pearl): identify and adjust for a sufficient set of confounders that blocks all back-door paths (non-causal paths from X to Y via confounders). Collider bias: conditioning on a collider (a variable caused by both X and Y) induces spurious association between X and Y (Berkson's bias, selection bias). Negative control exposures and outcomes can detect residual confounding. Sensitivity analysis: quantify how strong unmeasured confounding would need to be to explain away an observed association.

**CC-017 — Model Selection and Regularization**
Bias-variance tradeoff: total expected error = bias² + variance + irreducible noise; complex models: low bias, high variance (overfitting); simple models: high bias, low variance (underfitting). Cross-validation (CV): k-fold CV; LOOCV; the standard method for estimating out-of-sample predictive error. Information criteria: AIC = 2k − 2 ln(L̂) (penalizes for parameters, asymptotically equivalent to LOOCV); BIC = k ln(n) − 2 ln(L̂) (stronger penalty, consistent model selection). Regularization: adds a penalty for model complexity to the loss function. L2 (Ridge): β̂_ridge = argmin ||y − Xβ||² + λ||β||² — shrinks coefficients toward zero, retains all predictors. L1 (LASSO): λ||β||₁ — induces sparsity (some coefficients exactly zero). Elastic net: combines L1 + L2.

**CC-018 — Machine Learning Fundamentals**
Supervised learning: learn a mapping f: X → Y from labeled examples {(x_i, y_i)}. Training set: used to fit model. Validation set: used for hyperparameter tuning. Test set: used for final evaluation (must not be used for any design decisions). Classification: predict a discrete label; regression: predict a continuous value. Loss functions: MSE (regression); cross-entropy = −Σy log(ŷ) (classification). Decision trees: partition feature space by binary splits; interpretable but high variance; Random Forest: ensemble of trees with feature bagging — reduces variance; Gradient Boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM): sequentially fits trees to residuals — often best performance on tabular data. Neural networks: universal approximators; optimized by stochastic gradient descent (SGD).

**CC-019 — Resampling Methods**
Bootstrap: resample with replacement from the observed data B times; compute statistic on each bootstrap sample; the distribution of bootstrap statistics approximates the sampling distribution. Used for: constructing CIs (percentile, BCa methods), standard errors for complex estimators, hypothesis tests. Jackknife: leave-one-out; compute statistic on n−1 observations each time; used for bias estimation and standard errors. Cross-validation (CV): partition data into k folds; train on k−1 folds, validate on the held-out fold; repeat k times; average test errors. LOOCV (Leave-One-Out CV): k = n; estimates expected prediction error; computationally intensive; nearly unbiased but high variance. Time series: walk-forward validation instead of k-fold (to preserve temporal order).

**CC-020 — Nonparametric Methods**
Do not assume a specific parametric distribution for the data. Advantages: robust, fewer assumptions. Disadvantages: lower power for small samples if parametric assumptions hold. Key methods: Mann-Whitney U (two-sample comparison of distributions — tests whether P(X > Y) = 0.5), Kruskal-Wallis (k-sample comparison), Spearman correlation (monotone association via ranks; ρ = Pearson correlation of ranks), Kendall's τ (proportion of concordant minus discordant pairs). Permutation tests: compute the test statistic, randomly permute group labels B times, compute statistic on each permutation; p-value = fraction of permutation statistics exceeding observed. Kernel density estimation (KDE): nonparametric density estimation via smoothing of observed points.

**CC-021 — Time Series Analysis**
Stationarity: a time series is stationary if its mean, variance, and autocovariance structure are constant over time. Weak stationarity: constant mean and autocovariance depends only on lag. ARIMA(p,d,q) models: AR (autoregressive: Xₜ = Σᵢ φᵢXₜ₋ᵢ + ε), MA (moving average: Xₜ = εₜ + Σⱼ θⱼεₜ₋ⱼ), I (integrated: d differences to achieve stationarity). ACF (autocorrelation function) and PACF (partial ACF) guide model selection. Seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA): adds seasonal AR, MA, and differencing terms. Ljung-Box test: tests whether autocorrelations up to lag m are jointly zero (residual white noise check). Forecasting evaluation: out-of-sample MAPE, RMSE, MAE. Unit root tests: Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF), KPSS.

**CC-022 — Principal Component Analysis (PCA)**
PCA finds directions of maximum variance in the data. Steps: standardize features (z-score); compute the covariance matrix Σ; compute eigenvectors (principal components, PCs) and eigenvalues of Σ; project data onto the top k PCs. PCs are orthogonal and ordered by variance explained (eigenvalue magnitude). Total variance explained by k PCs = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ λᵢ / Σᵢ λᵢ. Scree plot: eigenvalue vs. PC index; the "elbow" suggests the number of components to retain. PCA is used for: dimensionality reduction, visualization (2D plot of PC1 vs. PC2), noise reduction, removing multicollinearity before regression. PCA ≠ factor analysis (FA): FA models latent structure; PCA is a data transformation.

**CC-023 — Clustering**
K-means: partition n observations into k clusters; minimize within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS = Σᵢ Σ_{x∈Cᵢ} ||x − μᵢ||²); local optimum (multiple random restarts needed); requires specifying k; sensitive to outliers. Choosing k: elbow method (WCSS vs. k), silhouette score, gap statistic. Hierarchical clustering: builds a tree (dendrogram); agglomerative (bottom-up): merge closest pair; divisive (top-down): split largest cluster. Linkage: single (minimum distance), complete (maximum distance), average (UPGMA), Ward (minimize increase in total WCSS). DBSCAN: density-based clustering; identifies core points, border points, and noise (outliers); does not require k; finds arbitrarily shaped clusters.

**CC-024 — Survival Analysis**
Studies time-to-event data where some observations are censored (event not yet observed at end of follow-up). Kaplan-Meier estimator: nonparametric estimate of the survival function S(t) = P(T > t); accounts for censoring; plotted as a step function. Log-rank test: nonparametric test of equality of two or more survival curves. Cox proportional hazards model: h(t|X) = h₀(t) × exp(β₁X₁ + ... + βₚXₚ); hazard ratio exp(β) is the multiplicative change in hazard per unit increase in X; h₀(t) is the unspecified baseline hazard (semi-parametric model). Proportional hazards assumption: the hazard ratio between groups is constant over time (testable via Schoenfeld residuals or log-log plots). Used in clinical trials, reliability engineering, and event modeling.

**CC-025 — Missing Data**
Missing completely at random (MCAR): missingness does not depend on observed or unobserved data; sample complete cases is unbiased. Missing at random (MAR): missingness depends on observed data but not on the missing value itself; multiple imputation (MI) or FIML (full-information maximum likelihood) gives valid inference. Missing not at random (MNAR): missingness depends on the unobserved value; requires sensitivity analysis; listwise deletion is biased. Multiple imputation: create M (typically 5–20) completed datasets by imputing from the predictive distribution; analyze each dataset; combine results using Rubin's rules (average estimates, pool standard errors). Single imputation (mean, median, mode) is generally inadequate — understates variance and distorts associations.

**CC-026 — Effect Sizes**
A statistically significant result is not necessarily meaningful; effect size quantifies the magnitude of an effect independently of sample size. Cohen's d: (μ₁ − μ₂) / σ_pooled; small (0.2), medium (0.5), large (0.8) by convention. Pearson's r: correlation coefficient; r² = proportion of variance explained; r = 0.1 (small), 0.3 (medium), 0.5 (large). Odds ratio (OR) and relative risk (RR): measures of association in contingency tables. η² (eta squared) and partial η²: proportion of variance explained in ANOVA. Reporting effect sizes alongside p-values is a minimum standard for scientific communication; the APA has required reporting of effect sizes since 1994.

**CC-027 — Data Visualization Principles**
Tufte's principles: maximize data-ink ratio (eliminate non-data ink), avoid chartjunk (decorative elements that add no information), use small multiples (same scale, multiple panels for comparison), the lie factor (visual representation should not exceed the numerical change it depicts). Grammar of graphics (Wilkinson → ggplot2): data + aesthetic mappings + geometric objects + scales + coordinate system + facets. Chart types by data type: categorical comparisons (bar chart, dot plot); distributions (histogram, density, boxplot, violin); relationships (scatter, line); part-to-whole (pie — generally avoid, use bar; treemap); change over time (line chart); geographic (choropleth, proportional symbol). Color: use sequential (low-high), diverging (low-zero-high), or qualitative (categorical) palettes appropriately; avoid red-green for colorblind accessibility.

**CC-028 — The Reproducibility Crisis and Open Science**
The reproducibility crisis: the Open Science Collaboration (2015) found that only ~36% of 97 psychology studies replicated at p<0.05; similar replication failures found in cancer biology (Begley & Ellis, 2012: 6/53 preclinical studies replicated) and economics (~60% replication rate). Causes: p-hacking (repeated testing until p < 0.05), HARKing (hypothesizing after results are known), publication bias (only positive results published), small samples, low prior probability of tested hypotheses, lack of pre-registration. Solutions: pre-registration (register hypotheses and analysis plan before data collection), open data, open materials, replication studies, multi-site studies, registered reports (peer review before data collection), statistical reform (effect sizes + CIs, Bayesian methods, equivalence testing).

**CC-029 — Fairness and Algorithmic Bias**
Statistical models can encode and amplify historical biases present in training data. Disparate impact: a model may produce disparate outcomes across demographic groups even when demographic features are excluded — because proxy variables (zip code, names, purchasing behavior) correlate with protected characteristics. Fairness metrics (in tension with each other): demographic parity (equal positive prediction rates across groups), equalized odds (equal TPR and FPR across groups), calibration (predicted probabilities accurate within groups), individual fairness (similar individuals treated similarly). The impossibility theorem (Chouldechova, 2017): when base rates differ between groups, demographic parity, equalized odds, and calibration cannot all be simultaneously satisfied. Algorithmic auditing, bias testing, and diverse development teams are necessary but insufficient alone.

**CC-030 — Power Analysis and Sample Size Determination**
Statistical power depends on: effect size (larger effect → higher power for fixed n), sample size n (larger n → higher power), significance level α (larger α → higher power), one vs. two-sided test. Cohen's power tables or G*Power software: given three of (α, power, effect size, n), compute the fourth. Common target: 80% power (1-β = 0.8) at α = 0.05 for a medium effect size. Small samples are systematically underpowered: a study with 80% power correctly detects a true effect 4/5 of the time; a 20% chance of a false negative is large. Winner's curse: effects estimated from studies that barely achieve significance are biased upward — expected effect size in a discovered result exceeds the true effect, especially in low-powered studies.

**CC-031 — Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)**
Tukey (1977): "Exploratory Data Analysis" as a distinct phase preceding confirmatory analysis. EDA tools: numerical summaries (mean, median, mode, range, IQR, skewness, kurtosis); graphical summaries (histogram, boxplot, scatter matrix, heatmap of correlations, Q-Q plot). Outlier detection: IQR method (>1.5×IQR from quartiles), Z-score method (>3σ from mean — assumes normality), DBSCAN (density-based, non-parametric), isolation forest (ML-based). Data quality checks: missing value rates, duplicate records, range and plausibility checks, distribution shifts across subgroups. EDA is not hypothesis testing — it generates hypotheses that must then be tested on held-out data or in pre-registered follow-up studies.

**CC-032 — Text Data and NLP Fundamentals**
Text preprocessing pipeline: tokenization (split into tokens/words), lowercasing, stop word removal, stemming/lemmatization (reduce to root form). Bag-of-words (BoW): document as vector of term frequencies; TF-IDF (term frequency-inverse document frequency): TF-IDF(t,d) = TF(t,d) × log(N/DF(t)) — downweights common terms. Word embeddings: Word2Vec (CBOW, skip-gram), GloVe (global vectors) — dense low-dimensional representations capturing semantic similarity; words with similar contexts have similar embeddings. Sentiment analysis: lexicon-based or supervised ML. Topic models: LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation) — probabilistic model; each document is a mixture of topics; each topic is a distribution over words. Transformer-based language models (BERT, GPT) have largely superseded earlier NLP methods for most tasks.

**CC-033 — Anomaly Detection**
Anomaly (outlier) detection: identifying observations that deviate significantly from expected patterns. Applications: fraud detection, network intrusion, industrial fault detection, medical diagnosis. Methods: Univariate (Z-score, IQR, Grubbs' test); Multivariate (Mahalanobis distance: accounts for correlations; PCA-based: high reconstruction error); Density-based (DBSCAN, LOF — local outlier factor compares local density of a point to its neighbors); Model-based (predict expected value, flag large residuals); Machine learning (isolation forest: anomalies isolated in fewer random splits; autoencoder: anomalies have high reconstruction error). Semi-supervised: train on normal data only, flag novel observations as anomalies. Precision-recall tradeoff in imbalanced anomaly detection.

**CC-034 — Responsible Data Practice and Data Governance**
Data governance: policies, procedures, and standards for managing data assets. Data quality dimensions: accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness. Privacy by design: anonymization (removal of direct identifiers), pseudonymization (replace with coded identifier), differential privacy (add calibrated random noise such that individual records cannot be identified — ε-differential privacy: mechanism M satisfies ε-DP if for all adjacent datasets D, D' and any output S: P(M(D) ∈ S) ≤ e^ε × P(M(D') ∈ S)). Re-identification risks: even "anonymized" data with age, zip code, and gender identifies >87% of Americans (Sweeney, 2000). Data minimization: collect only data necessary for the stated purpose. Retention limits and right to erasure.

## 5. Patterns

**P-001 — The Modeling Workflow**
(1) Define the question and outcome clearly; (2) EDA: understand data structure, missingness, distributions, relationships; (3) Split data: train/validation/test before any modeling; (4) Preprocessing: imputation, encoding, scaling (fit preprocessing on train only, apply to validation/test); (5) Model selection: candidate models, cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning; (6) Final evaluation: apply best model to test set (once only); (7) Interpretation: coefficients, feature importances, SHAP values, partial dependence plots; (8) Communicate uncertainty: CIs, prediction intervals, sensitivity analyses; (9) Document and version the full pipeline for reproducibility.

**P-002 — Pre-Registration for Confirmatory Research**
Before collecting data: (1) specify the primary hypothesis; (2) define the primary outcome measure; (3) specify the analysis plan (test, covariates, multiple testing correction); (4) determine sample size via power analysis; (5) register on a public registry (OSF, AsPredicted, ClinicalTrials.gov). After data collection: conduct only the pre-specified analysis; additional exploratory analyses are permitted but must be labeled as exploratory. Pre-registration prevents: HARKing, p-hacking, undisclosed multiple testing, post-hoc hypothesis revision.

**P-003 — Checking Model Assumptions**
Every statistical model has assumptions; inference is invalid when they are violated. Linear regression: (1) linearity (residuals vs. fitted, partial residual plots); (2) independence (time-series plots, Durbin-Watson); (3) homoscedasticity (residuals vs. fitted — fan shape indicates heteroscedasticity → use HC standard errors); (4) normality of residuals (Q-Q plot — only needed for small n); (5) no influential outliers (Cook's distance, leverage). Logistic regression: check for separation (estimates go to infinity), examine Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit. Time series: check residual autocorrelation (ACF of residuals, Ljung-Box test).

**P-004 — Simpson's Paradox Awareness**
A trend in a combined dataset disappears or reverses when the data is stratified. Classic: UC Berkeley admissions study (Bickel, 1975) — overall, men had higher admission rates; within each department, women had higher or equal rates — because women applied to more competitive departments. This arises when there is a confounding variable (department) that is associated with both the exposure (gender) and outcome (admission rate). Always examine whether associations persist within strata defined by important covariates; aggregate associations can be misleading.

**P-005 — The Importance of Representativeness**
The sample must be representative of the population to which inferences are drawn. Selection bias: systematic differences between those who are included and those who are excluded. Volunteer bias: people who volunteer for studies differ systematically from non-volunteers (healthier-worker effect). Survivorship bias: analyzing only subjects who survived to be observed (WW2 bomber example — armor the untouched parts). Healthy user bias: patients prescribed preventive medications have better health behaviors generally. Before generalizing results, assess: who was included and excluded from the sample? On what factors might they differ from the target population?

**P-006 — Regularization for High-Dimensional Data**
When p (predictors) approaches or exceeds n (sample size), OLS is undefined (infinite solutions) or severely overfit. Regularization adds a penalty: Ridge (L2): β̂ = argmin ||y−Xβ||² + λ||β||²; LASSO (L1): sparsity-inducing, performs variable selection; elastic net (L1 + L2): handles correlated predictors better than pure LASSO. Choosing λ: cross-validation (plot CV error vs. log λ; choose λ at minimum or within 1 SE of minimum). Practical rule: when p > n/10, consider regularization; when p > n, regularization is necessary. Standardize predictors before applying regularization (equal penalty scale).

**P-007 — Reporting Uncertainty**
Every statistical estimate should be accompanied by a measure of uncertainty: standard errors, confidence intervals (frequentist) or credible intervals (Bayesian). Uncertainty must be propagated through calculations: if A = f(B, C), the uncertainty in A depends on the uncertainties in B and C and their correlation (delta method for propagation). Prediction intervals are wider than confidence intervals for the mean — they account for individual variation around the mean. In communication, pair point estimates with intervals and interpret them correctly: "I estimate [X] with 95% confidence interval [L, U]" — the interval quantifies the statistical precision, not certainty about the true value.

**P-008 — Test-Train Split Discipline**
The test set must never be used for any design decision; it is reserved for final evaluation only. Cross-validation is used for model selection and hyperparameter tuning (on the training set). The moment you use test set information to select a model, the test set is "contaminated" and no longer provides an honest estimate of generalization performance. Common violations: tuning model hyperparameters until the test loss improves; selecting features using correlation with the test set outcome; examining test set errors and adding rules to handle them.

**P-009 — Bootstrapping for Complex Estimators**
When analytical standard error formulas are unavailable or derived under restrictive assumptions: (1) resample with replacement from the observed data B = 1,000–10,000 times; (2) compute the statistic of interest on each bootstrap sample; (3) the standard deviation of the bootstrap distribution estimates the standard error; (4) the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution are the 95% percentile CI (BCa CI preferred for bias correction and acceleration). The bootstrap is not a panacea: it fails when the estimator is not smooth, the sample is small, or the statistic is an extreme quantile.

**P-010 — Directed Acyclic Graphs for Causal Reasoning**
Before analyzing observational data, draw a causal DAG: nodes are variables, directed edges represent hypothesized causal effects. The DAG makes causal assumptions explicit and testable. Use the DAG to determine: which variables to condition on (confounders — in the back-door criterion), which to avoid conditioning on (colliders — conditioning opens a spurious path), whether the causal effect is identified, and which sensitivity analyses are needed. DAGitty (online tool) and the ggdag R package facilitate DAG construction and analysis. The DAG approach, developed by Judea Pearl, unifies and extends the Rubin potential outcomes framework.

**P-011 — The Multiple Comparisons Awareness Heuristic**
Whenever looking at data with many possible associations (subgroup analyses, multiple endpoints, multiple time points, gene expression data with thousands of genes), explicitly account for multiple comparisons. Apply Bonferroni correction for FWER control (when each false positive is costly) or Benjamini-Hochberg for FDR control (when a small fraction of false positives is acceptable, as in genomics). Report all tests performed (including non-significant ones) — selective reporting creates a misleading impression of the evidential value of the findings. Exploratory analyses must be labeled as such.

**P-012 — Feature Engineering Principles**
Good features improve model performance more than algorithm selection. Principles: (1) Domain knowledge: transform raw data to capture domain-meaningful patterns (log-transform skewed variables; create interaction terms when they have substantive meaning; compute ratios rather than using raw numerator and denominator); (2) Sparsity: one-hot encode categorical variables (avoid ordinal encoding for nominal variables); (3) Missing indicator features: add a binary indicator for whether a value was missing; (4) Temporal features: extract hour-of-day, day-of-week, month, seasonality; (5) Text: TF-IDF or embeddings; (6) Scaling: standardize or min-max scale for distance-based methods (KNN, SVMs, neural nets).

**P-013 — Calibration of Probabilistic Predictions**
A model is well-calibrated if its predicted probabilities match observed frequencies: among predictions of p = 0.7, approximately 70% of events actually occur. Reliability diagram (calibration plot): plot mean predicted probability vs. observed event rate in bins; well-calibrated models lie on the diagonal. Calibration metrics: Brier score = E[(y − p̂)²]; Expected Calibration Error (ECE). Calibration of classifier scores: Platt scaling (logistic regression on model scores), isotonic regression. Tree-based models (Random Forest, gradient boosting) are often poorly calibrated; neural networks are overconfident; calibration is critical for risk communication and decision-making.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP-001 — Misinterpreting the P-Value**
The p-value is P(observing data this extreme or more | H₀ is true). Common misinterpretations: "p = 0.03 means there's a 3% chance H₀ is true" (false — the probability of H₀ given data ≠ the probability of data given H₀); "p > 0.05 means H₀ is true" (failure to reject ≠ evidence of absence); "p < 0.05 means the result is important" (statistical significance is not clinical/practical significance); "p < 0.05 means the result will replicate" (a single p < 0.05 provides weak replication guarantee, especially with low prior probability). The ASA Statement on P-Values (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016) and its 2019 supplement explicitly address these misinterpretations.

**AP-002 — P-Hacking and HARKing**
P-hacking: repeatedly testing data until p < 0.05 appears by collecting more data, removing outliers, trying different tests, or analyzing different subsets. Each additional test inflates the Type I error rate. HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known): presenting a post-hoc hypothesis as if it were pre-specified. Both are widespread — Simmons et al. (2011) showed that p-hacking with 4 "degrees of freedom" produces a 60% false positive rate when the null is true. Simmons estimated >50% of published psychology results are false positives due to these practices.

**AP-003 — Ignoring Multicollinearity**
In multiple regression, highly correlated predictors produce unstable coefficient estimates with large standard errors (even if F-test is significant). The variance inflation factor (VIF) measures multicollinearity: VIF_j = 1/(1 − R²_j) where R²_j is the R² of regressing predictor j on all other predictors; VIF > 10 indicates severe multicollinearity. Symptoms: large standard errors, sign flips when predictors are added/removed, confidence intervals that include impossible values. Solutions: remove one of the correlated predictors (based on domain logic), use PCA to create orthogonal components, or use Ridge regression (regularization reduces sensitivity to multicollinearity).

**AP-004 — Data Leakage**
Including information in model features that would not be available at prediction time in the real world. Examples: including future data in features for a historical prediction; fitting preprocessing parameters (mean, standard deviation, imputation values) on the entire dataset before splitting train/test (the test set mean "leaks" into training via the scaling); including the outcome as a predictor (directly or through a proxy); using post-treatment variables as predictors when predicting treatment effects. Leakage produces optimistically biased model evaluations that do not generalize to real-world deployment.

**AP-005 — Averaging Rates When Denominators Differ**
Averaging percentages or rates without accounting for the different denominators (sizes) of the groups being combined. The average of (90% and 10%) is not 50% if the first group is 10 times larger than the second. Use weighted averages when combining rates from groups of different sizes. This is a specific form of Simpson's paradox — the overall average obscures the within-group patterns when group sizes are confounded with the rate of interest.

**AP-006 — Over-Interpreting Correlation**
A correlation r = 0.3 explains only r² = 9% of the variance — substantial unexplained variation remains. Spurious correlation: many variables correlate purely by chance, especially with large n (the "Texas sharpshooter" — the null hypothesis of zero correlation is almost certainly false for any two variables measured on the same sample with large n). Correlation does not imply causation: smoking correlates with lung cancer (causal), yellow fingers correlate with lung cancer (confounded by smoking). Ecological fallacy: correlation observed at the group level (countries' chocolate consumption and Nobel laureates) does not necessarily hold at the individual level.

**AP-007 — Failing to Check Assumptions**
Applying statistical tests without verifying their assumptions leads to invalid inference. Using a t-test when data are heavily skewed (non-normal with small n) inflates Type I error. Using chi-square when expected cell counts are < 5 (use Fisher's exact test). Using linear regression when the relationship is highly nonlinear. Using ANOVA when variances are dramatically unequal (use Welch's ANOVA). Using simple OLS with autocorrelated time-series residuals (use ARMA errors or HAC standard errors). Assumption checking is not optional — it is part of the analysis.

**AP-008 — Presenting Unadjusted Effect Sizes for Confounded Data**
In observational studies, crude (unadjusted) associations can be dramatically misleading. Example: "patients who received Drug X had worse outcomes than those who did not" — but Drug X was only given to the sickest patients (confounding by indication). Always report both crude and adjusted estimates; explain the rationale for which confounders were included in the adjustment model; acknowledge residual confounding as a limitation. Unadjusted associations from observational data should almost never be used as the primary measure of an association in a policy-relevant claim.

**AP-009 — Extrapolating Beyond the Data Range**
Regression models are linear approximations of the observed data relationship; they should not be used for prediction far outside the observed range of the predictors (extrapolation). A relationship that is approximately linear between age 30 and 60 may not continue linearly beyond those bounds. The confidence interval for predictions widens rapidly outside the observed data range (leverage effect). Practically, the structural form of the relationship may change: demand for a product at an extreme price point may not follow the model estimated at moderate prices.

**AP-010 — Treating All Missing Data as Missing Completely at Random**
Listwise deletion (complete case analysis) produces unbiased estimates only under MCAR — the most restrictive assumption. In most practical settings, missingness is not random: patients who drop out of a clinical trial often do so because their condition is worsening; survey non-responders are different from responders; laboratory values may not be measured because the clinician judged a test unnecessary based on the patient's condition. Sensitivity analyses across different assumptions about the missing data mechanism, and the use of multiple imputation under MAR, are standard practice in rigorous analysis.

**AP-011 — Using Accuracy as the Sole Metric for Imbalanced Classification**
In a dataset where 99% of observations are class 0, a model that predicts class 0 for everything achieves 99% accuracy — but is completely useless. For imbalanced classification: use precision-recall AUC, F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall), ROC-AUC (less sensitive to class imbalance than accuracy), sensitivity, specificity, and positive/negative predictive values. The optimal threshold for a classification model depends on the relative costs of false positives and false negatives — choose the threshold that minimizes expected cost, not the one that maximizes accuracy.

## 7. Facts

**F-001** — Francis Galton introduced correlation and regression to the mean (1886): he measured heights of parents and their adult children; children of tall parents were taller than average but shorter than their parents (regression to the mean); Galton called the slope "regression" — the term persists; Karl Pearson formalized the correlation coefficient r (Pearson, 1896).

**F-002** — William Sealy Gosset (pseudonym "Student") derived the t-distribution in 1908 while working as a brewer at Guinness; Guinness allowed publication only anonymously to protect trade secrets; Gosset needed a distribution for small samples (n < 30) because the sample standard deviation S is a poor estimate of σ for small n; the t-distribution has heavier tails than the normal — accounting for additional uncertainty from estimating σ.

**F-003** — Ronald A. Fisher's contributions to statistics: analysis of variance (ANOVA, 1921), the F-distribution (named for Fisher), maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), sufficient statistics, experimental design (Latin squares, factorial designs, blocking), the randomization test (1935), the p-value < 0.05 convention (1926, arbitrary choice by Fisher himself), and the fiducial argument. His 1925 book "Statistical Methods for Research Workers" shaped the practice of statistics for a century; Fisher was also a founding figure of population genetics.

**F-004** — Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson formalized hypothesis testing in 1928–1933, introducing the alternative hypothesis H₁, Type I and II errors, and the concept of power; their framework differs philosophically from Fisher's — Fisher rejected the concept of an alternative hypothesis and objected to the Neyman-Pearson framework; the hybrid "NHST" taught today awkwardly combines both frameworks in ways that neither Fisher nor Neyman-Pearson would have endorsed.

**F-005** — The Central Limit Theorem was first stated (informally) by de Moivre (1733) for the binomial distribution; Laplace proved a more general form (1812); Lyapunov (1901) proved it under conditions now bearing his name; Lindeberg (1922) proved the most general classical version. The CLT is the mathematical reason why so many real phenomena approximate the normal distribution — they are sums of many small, approximately independent effects.

**F-006** — John Tukey coined the terms "bit" (binary digit, 1948), "software" (1958), and "exploratory data analysis"; he developed the boxplot, stem-and-leaf plot, and many other visualization tools; he worked on the Fast Fourier Transform (Cooley-Tukey algorithm, 1965); his 1977 book "Exploratory Data Analysis" established EDA as a discipline; Tukey also worked on nuclear weapons testing at the Institute for Defense Analyses.

**F-007** — Bradley Efron introduced the bootstrap method in 1979 ("Bootstrap Methods: Another Look at the Jackknife," Annals of Statistics); it was initially met with skepticism — how can you learn from repeatedly sampling the same data? The theoretical justification (the bootstrap estimates the sampling distribution by treating the empirical CDF as the true CDF) was developed through the 1980s; Efron received the International Prize in Statistics (2018) and the US Medal of Science (2007) for the bootstrap and other contributions.

**F-008** — The LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) was introduced by Robert Tibshirani in 1996; it simultaneously performs coefficient shrinkage and variable selection (producing sparse models with some coefficients exactly zero); this sparsity property (arising from the L1 penalty's non-differentiability at zero) distinguishes it from Ridge regression; the LASSO is one of the most-cited statistical papers of all time (>60,000 citations).

**F-009** — Randomized controlled trial: the first RCT widely recognized as such was the Medical Research Council streptomycin trial for tuberculosis (1948, UK), designed by Austin Bradford Hill; however, prior randomization experiments include the British Admiralty testing James Lind's scurvy treatments (1747) and an 1835 experiment on typhoid fever; Hill's systematic methodological innovations (allocation concealment, blinding, pre-specification) distinguish the 1948 trial as the modern RCT prototype.

**F-010** — The Bonferroni correction is named for Carlo Emilio Bonferroni (1892–1960) who proved the Bonferroni inequality (P(∪Aᵢ) ≤ ΣP(Aᵢ)); the multiple testing correction was first applied statistically by Dunn (1961) based on this inequality; the correction is conservative (controls FWER but at the cost of reduced power) and is now often replaced by Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) in high-throughput settings.

**F-011** — The Great Reproducibility Crisis: The Reproducibility Project: Psychology (OSC, 2015) — 97 studies, 36% replicated at p < 0.05; Replication Cancer Biology (Bhattacharjee et al., 2016) — only 6 of 53 preclinical results replicated; Science of Science (Camerer et al., 2018) — 62% of 21 social science studies replicated; estimated ~50% of published psychology findings do not replicate when attempted with pre-registered protocols and adequate power.

**F-012** — Anscombe's quartet (1973): four datasets with nearly identical summary statistics (mean of x, mean of y, variance of x, variance of y, correlation, and regression line) but dramatically different distributional shapes when plotted; illustrates that summary statistics alone are insufficient — always visualize data; the Datasaurus Dozen (Matejka & Fitzmaurice, 2017) extends this to 12 datasets with identical statistics but very different shapes including a dinosaur silhouette.

**F-013** — Andrew Gelman's "garden of forking paths" (2013): a metaphor for researcher degrees of freedom — the many implicit analytical choices (outlier handling, covariate selection, transformation, test choice, subgroup analysis) made throughout a study; even without conscious p-hacking, these choices collectively produce Type I error rates far above the nominal α; Gelman advocates for Bayesian inference, continuous model checking, and "Type S" (sign) and "Type M" (magnitude) error analysis.

**F-014** — The data-generating process (DGP): the underlying mechanism that produces observed data; statistical models are approximations of the DGP; all models are wrong (George Box, 1976: "all models are wrong, but some are useful"); model adequacy depends on whether the model captures the features of the DGP relevant to the inferential question; robustness checks (varying model specification) assess sensitivity of conclusions to modeling assumptions.

**F-015** — Pearson's r and its caveats: r measures linear association; r = 0 does not imply independence (a perfect non-linear relationship can have r = 0); r is sensitive to outliers (a single outlier can change r dramatically); r is not robust to skewness or non-normality; r = 1 implies perfect linear relationship, not equality (Y = 2X has r = 1); spurious correlations (Tyler Vigen's database) include: number of Nicholas Cage films per year vs. swimming pool drownings (r = 0.67, 2000–2009).

**F-016** — The Netflix Prize (2006–2009): $1 million competition to improve Netflix's movie recommendation algorithm (Cinematch) by 10% RMSE; the winning team (BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos) achieved 10.06% improvement using ensemble methods combining matrix factorization, neighborhood methods, and regression; the prize-winning model was never deployed by Netflix because its production architecture had changed; the competition catalyzed development of collaborative filtering and matrix factorization methods.

**F-017** — The confusion matrix: for binary classification — True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), False Negatives (FN). Derived metrics: Accuracy = (TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN); Precision = TP/(TP+FP); Recall (Sensitivity) = TP/(TP+FN); Specificity = TN/(TN+FP); F1 = 2×Precision×Recall/(Precision+Recall); Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) = (TP×TN−FP×FN)/√((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)) — preferred for imbalanced classes because it uses all four cells and is only high when the model performs well on both classes.

**F-018** — The chi-square distribution: the sum of squares of k independent standard normal random variables follows χ²(k); used in chi-square goodness-of-fit test (k−1 degrees of freedom), chi-square test of independence (df = (r−1)(c−1) for r×c contingency table), and as the sampling distribution of S²(n−1)/σ² in normal populations. Pearson's chi-square statistic X² = Σ(O−E)²/E follows χ²(k−1) asymptotically; exact alternatives (Fisher's exact test, Monte Carlo simulation) are preferred when expected cell counts are small.

**F-019** — The Kaplan-Meier estimator (1958, Edward Kaplan and Paul Meier): one of the most cited papers in all of science (~70,000 citations for the original JASA paper); estimated survival function S(t) = product of (1 − dᵢ/nᵢ) over all event times tᵢ ≤ t, where dᵢ = number of events and nᵢ = number at risk; accounts for censored observations; the log-rank test (Mantel, 1966; Cox, 1972) compares two survival curves nonparametrically; the Cox proportional hazards model (Cox, 1972) is estimated with ~30,000 citations — one of the most influential statistical papers.

**F-020** — Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE): choose parameters θ to maximize L(θ; data) = P(data; θ) — the probability of observing the data given the parameters. Under regularity conditions: MLE is consistent (converges to true value), asymptotically normal (allows CI construction from Fisher information), and asymptotically efficient (achieves the Cramér-Rao lower bound — lowest possible variance for unbiased estimators). For normal linear regression, MLE = OLS. For logistic regression, MLE is computed iteratively (no closed form). For censored data (survival analysis), the likelihood accounts for censoring.

**F-021** — K-fold cross-validation: partition data into k equal folds; for each fold i, train on all folds except i, evaluate on fold i; average test errors across k folds. The standard is k = 5 or k = 10 (empirically good tradeoff between bias and variance of the CV error estimate). LOOCV (k = n): nearly unbiased but high variance and computationally expensive (O(n) model fits); for linear models, LOOCV can be computed analytically via the hat matrix: CV = (1/n)Σ[(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)/(1 − hᵢᵢ)]² where hᵢᵢ is the leverage of observation i.

**F-022** — Bootstrapping confidence intervals: the BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated) interval is generally preferred over the percentile bootstrap interval because it corrects for bias in the estimate and for nonstationarity of the standard error as the parameter changes; the BCa CI requires computing the acceleration constant a via jackknife; in R: boot.ci(boot.object, type = "bca"); recommended by Efron and Tibshirani in "An Introduction to the Bootstrap" (1993), the standard reference.

**F-023** — Effect size in social psychology: a review of 322 meta-analyses in social psychology (Richard et al., 2003) found median r = 0.21 (d ≈ 0.43) across findings — considerably smaller than Cohen's "medium" effect size; this means social psychology effects explain approximately 4% of variance on average; large-scale preregistered studies often find smaller effects than the original small studies; the "decline effect" (Schooler, 2011) — published effect sizes tend to diminish upon replication.

**F-024** — Power analysis software: G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) computes sample size and power for most standard statistical tests; widely used in psychology, biology, and medicine; many published studies are severely underpowered — a survey of neuroscience fMRI studies found median power of only 8–31% for small effects (Button et al., 2013); underpowered studies produce unreliable results — even when they produce p < 0.05, the effect size is inflated (winner's curse).

**F-025** — Instrumental variables (IV) estimation: an instrument Z is valid if (1) it is correlated with the exposure X (relevance), (2) it affects the outcome Y only through X (exclusion restriction — untestable), and (3) it is independent of confounders (exogeneity). The two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimator: first stage: regress X on Z; second stage: regress Y on predicted X from first stage. IV estimates are interpreted as local average treatment effects (LATE): the effect for "compliers" (those whose X changes when Z changes). Weak instruments (F < 10 in first stage) produce severely biased IV estimates.

**F-026** — Regression discontinuity design (RDD): treatment is assigned based on whether a "running variable" crosses a threshold (scholarship if test score ≥ threshold; vote share ≥ 50% wins election; age 65 receives Medicare); in a sharp RDD, treatment is a deterministic function of the running variable; the local average treatment effect at the threshold is estimated by comparing outcomes just above and below the cutoff; identification requires no manipulation of the running variable (McCrary density test) and continuity of potential outcomes at the cutoff.

**F-027** — The Gauss-Markov theorem: under the classical regression assumptions (linear model, strict exogeneity, no perfect multicollinearity, spherical errors — homoscedastic + uncorrelated), OLS is BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator); "best" means lowest variance among all linear unbiased estimators; the theorem does not require normality — that's needed only for exact (finite-sample) inference; under heteroscedasticity, OLS remains unbiased but is no longer efficient — GLS (generalized least squares) or HC standard errors are appropriate.

**F-028** — The Winner's Curse in research: the effect size estimate in the first significant study is nearly always larger than the true effect; because underpowered studies only achieve significance when chance inflates the effect estimate; the expected effect size conditional on significance = true effect + upward bias (proportional to 1/power); this explains why effect sizes shrink upon replication; it also means that relying on a single significant study to set sample size for a replication leads to systematic under-powering of the replication.

**F-029** — Differential privacy (Dwork et al., 2006): a rigorous mathematical definition of privacy for statistical algorithms; mechanism M satisfies ε-differential privacy if for any two datasets D, D' differing in one record and any output set S: P(M(D) ∈ S) ≤ e^ε × P(M(D') ∈ S); smaller ε → stronger privacy; achieves privacy by adding calibrated Laplace or Gaussian noise; the US Census Bureau adopted DP for the 2020 Census (ε ≈ 19.61 for state-level data — a weak privacy guarantee); Apple and Google use DP for local data collection; DP provably prevents re-identification regardless of auxiliary information available to the adversary.

**F-030** — The Iris dataset (Fisher, 1936): the most famous dataset in statistics and ML; 150 observations, 3 species of iris (setosa, versicolor, virginica), 4 measurements (sepal length/width, petal length/width); Fisher used it to introduce linear discriminant analysis; used in virtually every statistics and ML textbook to illustrate classification; the dataset has a known data error (two observations in the original Fisher paper have swapped values — Bezdek et al., 1999).

**F-031** — The difference-in-differences (DiD) design: compares the change in outcomes over time in a treated group to the change in a control group; identifies the causal effect under the parallel trends assumption (in the absence of treatment, the treated and control groups would have followed parallel trends); the estimate: DiD = (Ȳ_treated,post − Ȳ_treated,pre) − (Ȳ_control,post − Ȳ_control,pre); used extensively in policy evaluation (minimum wage effects, Medicaid expansion, Card & Krueger 1994); the parallel trends assumption is testable using pre-treatment data.

**F-032** — Logistic regression output: eᵝ gives the odds ratio per unit increase in X; the probability (not the odds) of the event given X is p = σ(β₀ + β₁X) = 1/(1 + e^{−(β₀+β₁X)}); the odds ratio is symmetric (OR for exposure vs. control = 1/OR for control vs. exposure) while the risk ratio is not; the OR approximates the RR (risk ratio) when the outcome is rare (< 10%); for common outcomes, the OR overstates the magnitude of the RR and should not be interpreted as a risk ratio.

**F-033** — Principal Component Analysis: Karl Pearson (1901) introduced PCA geometrically; Harold Hotelling (1933) developed the algebraic formulation; PCA is equivalent to the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the centered data matrix X: X = UΣVᵀ; the principal components are the columns of V; the scores (projections of data onto PCs) are the columns of UΣ; the eigenvalues of XᵀX are the squared singular values and equal n−1 times the PC variances.

**F-034** — K-means algorithm (Lloyd's algorithm, 1957; published 1982): (1) randomly initialize k centroids; (2) assign each observation to the nearest centroid (Euclidean distance); (3) update each centroid to the mean of its assigned observations; (4) repeat until convergence (or max iterations). K-means is guaranteed to converge but only to a local optimum; run multiple times with different random initializations; k-means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007) initializes centroids with probability proportional to distance from existing centroids — reduces convergence to poor local optima.

**F-035** — Extreme value theory (EVT): describes the behavior of the maximum (or minimum) of a large sample. Generalized extreme value distribution (GEV, Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko theorem): the maximum of n i.i.d. samples from almost any distribution converges to one of three distributions (Gumbel, Fréchet, Weibull) — collectively the GEV family. Applied to: flood return periods, financial tail risk (VaR, CVaR), wind speed design standards, extreme weather events. Block maxima method vs. peaks-over-threshold (GPD — Generalized Pareto Distribution). EVT is particularly important for risk management because it specifically models the tail behavior that is most dangerous.

**F-036** — The replication crisis prompted major methodological reforms: Journal of Experimental Psychology (2014) and other journals adopted mandatory pre-registration and statistical reporting standards; the 2019 American Statistical Association statement "Moving to a World Beyond 'p < 0.05'" called for abandoning "statistical significance" as a binary threshold; methods reformers advocate: standardized effect sizes, confidence intervals, Bayesian inference, equivalence tests (TOST), and multi-site replication studies as standard practice.

**F-037** — Cross-validation variants for specific contexts: time series data: walk-forward validation (train on t₁...t_k, test on t_{k+1}; advance by one period); no shuffling. Spatial data: spatial CV (reserve geographically separated regions for test). Grouped data (multiple observations per subject): group k-fold (entire subjects assigned to folds, not individual observations — prevents data leakage from the same subject appearing in both train and test). Stratified k-fold: for classification, maintain the class proportion in each fold.

**F-038** — Entropy and information theory in statistics: Shannon entropy H(X) = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x) (bits); measures uncertainty; maximum entropy distribution given constraints identifies least informative prior (Jaynes). Kullback-Leibler divergence D_KL(P||Q) = Σ p(x) log(p(x)/q(x)): measures "information lost" when Q approximates P; not symmetric; the AIC = 2k − 2 ln(L̂) can be interpreted as an estimate of the expected KL divergence between the true DGP and the model.

**F-039** — SHAP values (Lundberg & Lee, 2017): game-theoretic (Shapley value) approach to explaining ML model predictions; SHAP value for feature j in observation i = the average marginal contribution of feature j across all possible feature orderings; satisfies: efficiency (SHAP values sum to prediction − base value), symmetry, dummy, and linearity — the four Shapley axioms; computed by TreeSHAP in O(TLD²) time for tree models (L = leaves, D = depth, T = trees); global feature importance: mean(|SHAP|) across all observations; SHAP dependence plots show effect of a feature on model output.

**F-040** — Data scaling: standardization (z-score): z = (x − μ)/σ; mean 0, standard deviation 1; sensitive to outliers (uses mean and SD); min-max scaling: x' = (x − x_min)/(x_max − x_min); maps to [0,1]; very sensitive to outliers (uses range). Robust scaling: x' = (x − median)/IQR; less sensitive to outliers. Log transformation: reduces right skewness, makes multiplicative relationships additive; appropriate when values span multiple orders of magnitude; not applicable for non-positive values without shift. Box-Cox transformation: generalizes log; maximizes normality.

**F-041** — Structural equation modeling (SEM): extends path analysis and confirmatory factor analysis; models both latent variables (unobserved constructs measured by multiple indicators) and observed variables; estimated by maximum likelihood (or robust variants); fit indices: CFI > 0.95 (comparative fit), RMSEA < 0.06 (root mean square error of approximation), SRMR < 0.08 (standardized root mean square residual) — commonly used thresholds (Hu & Bentler, 1999); used in psychology, sociology, economics, and education research for testing theoretical models.

**F-042** — The jackknife resampling method: leave-one-out; compute the statistic θ̂ on the full sample and on each n−1 subset; jackknife bias = (n−1)(θ̄_JK − θ̂) where θ̄_JK is the mean of jackknife estimates; jackknife standard error = √[(n−1)/n × Σ(θ̂_{(i)} − θ̄_JK)²]; the delete-d jackknife generalizes to deleting d observations; more computationally intensive than the delete-1 jackknife; computationally simpler than the bootstrap; fails for non-smooth statistics (e.g., the sample median requires bootstrap or delete-d jackknife with d growing with n).

**F-043** — The two cultures in statistics (Breiman, 2001): statistical modeling (data generated by a stochastic model, goal is inference about the DGP — classical statistics) vs. algorithmic modeling (black box, goal is accurate prediction — machine learning). Breiman argued statisticians were too focused on restrictive models; the tension persists: ML achieves best predictive performance but sacrifices interpretability and uncertainty quantification; statisticians emphasize valid inference but may underfit complex relationships; integration of the two cultures (interpretable ML, conformal prediction, causal ML) is an active research frontier.

**F-044** — XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016): gradient boosting with regularized objective function; ensemble of decision trees trained sequentially, each fitting the residuals of the previous ensemble; regularization terms (L1 and L2 on leaf weights) prevent overfitting; missing value handling built-in; second-order gradient information (Newton boosting) for faster convergence; parallel computation across features; XGBoost won >50% of Kaggle competitions between 2015 and 2017; the dominant algorithm for tabular data competition winning through 2023.

**F-045** — Conformal prediction: a framework for distribution-free uncertainty quantification; constructs prediction sets that contain the true outcome with guaranteed (1−α) coverage for any distribution, assuming exchangeable observations; no distributional assumptions required; the prediction set for a new observation x_{n+1} is {y : score(y, x_{n+1}) ≤ τ_α}, where τ_α is the (1−α) quantile of calibration scores; split conformal prediction is computationally efficient and applicable to any black-box predictor; provides rigorous prediction intervals for ML models without any parametric assumptions.

**F-046** — Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA): a generative probabilistic model for text corpora; documents are mixtures of topics; topics are distributions over words; each word in a document is generated by: (1) sampling a topic from the document's topic distribution; (2) sampling a word from that topic's word distribution; parameters estimated by variational inference or Gibbs sampling; topics are interpretable clusters of co-occurring words; choosing the number of topics k: perplexity (lower = better fit), coherence (Cv coherence measures topic interpretability), or domain knowledge; LDA does not capture word order or semantics (unlike transformer models).

**F-047** — Network analysis metrics: degree centrality (number of connections); betweenness centrality (fraction of shortest paths passing through a node — identifies bridges); closeness centrality (inverse of average shortest path length — reaches others quickly); eigenvector centrality (PageRank: importance weighted by importance of connections); clustering coefficient (fraction of a node's neighbors that are also connected to each other — measures local density). Community detection: Louvain algorithm (maximizes modularity Q = fraction of edges within communities minus expected fraction under a random null model); widely used in biology (gene regulatory networks), sociology (social networks), and economics (trading networks).

**F-048** — Quantile regression (Koenker & Bassett, 1978): estimates conditional quantiles of the response distribution rather than the conditional mean; robust to outliers in Y; provides a comprehensive view of the distribution; the τth quantile regression minimizes the "check" loss function ρ_τ(u) = u(τ − I(u < 0)); the 0.5 quantile regression is the least absolute deviations (LAD) estimator; quantile regression does not assume homoscedasticity; used in economics (wage distributions, income inequality), ecology (habitat suitability envelope), and risk management (Value at Risk = a conditional quantile).

**F-049** — Structural break tests: tests for parameter instability in time series models. Chow test: tests for a break at a known breakpoint using an F-test comparing restricted (common parameters) and unrestricted (separate parameters) models. Quandt-Andrews test: takes the supremum of Chow statistics over all candidate breakpoints in the middle 70% of the sample (unknown breakpoint). CUSUM test (Brown, Durbin, Evans, 1975): plots cumulative sum of recursive residuals; systematic deviation from zero indicates structural break. Structural breaks are common in macroeconomic data: the 2008 financial crisis, COVID-19, policy regime changes create breaks in otherwise stable relationships.
