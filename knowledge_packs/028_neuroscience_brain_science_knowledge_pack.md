## 4. Core Concepts

**CC-001 — Neuron**
The fundamental signal-processing cell of the nervous system. Composed of a cell body (soma), dendrites (input), and an axon (output). Neurons transmit information via electrical signals (action potentials) and chemical signals (neurotransmitters at synapses). The human brain contains approximately 86 billion neurons.

**CC-002 — Action Potential**
An all-or-none electrical signal propagating along an axon, generated by voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels. The membrane potential rapidly depolarizes from −70 mV (resting) to +40 mV, then repolarizes. Propagation speed ranges from 0.5 m/s (unmyelinated C fibers) to 120 m/s (large myelinated Aα fibers).

**CC-003 — Myelin and Saltatory Conduction**
Myelin sheaths (produced by oligodendrocytes in the CNS, Schwann cells in the PNS) insulate axons and dramatically increase conduction velocity. Action potentials jump between nodes of Ranvier (saltatory conduction), reducing metabolic cost. Demyelination (multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barré) severely impairs conduction.

**CC-004 — Synapse**
The specialized junction where one neuron communicates with another or with a muscle/gland. Chemical synapses release neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft; electrical synapses (gap junctions) pass ions directly. Synaptic transmission is unidirectional (presynaptic → postsynaptic) and highly modifiable.

**CC-005 — Neurotransmitter**
A chemical messenger released at synapses. Major types: glutamate (primary excitatory), GABA (primary inhibitory), dopamine (reward, motor control), serotonin (mood, arousal), acetylcholine (neuromuscular junction, autonomic, memory), norepinephrine (arousal, stress), histamine, glycine, endocannabinoids, neuropeptides.

**CC-006 — Receptor and Ionotropic/Metabotropic Classification**
Ionotropic receptors are ligand-gated ion channels providing fast (millisecond) synaptic responses (AMPA, NMDA, GABA-A, nicotinic ACh). Metabotropic receptors couple to G-proteins producing slower, modulatory effects lasting seconds to minutes (mGluR, GABA-B, dopamine D1/D2, muscarinic ACh).

**CC-007 — Synaptic Plasticity**
The ability of synaptic strength to change in response to activity. Long-Term Potentiation (LTP): persistent strengthening following high-frequency stimulation; the dominant cellular model of learning and memory. Long-Term Depression (LTD): persistent weakening. Both depend critically on NMDA receptor activation and AMPA receptor trafficking.

**CC-008 — Glial Cells**
Non-neuronal support cells outnumbering neurons roughly 1:1. Astrocytes: metabolic support, blood-brain barrier maintenance, synaptic modulation (tripartite synapse). Microglia: CNS immune cells, synaptic pruning. Oligodendrocytes: myelination. Schwann cells: PNS myelination. Ependymal cells: CSF production. Radial glia: neural stem cells in development.

**CC-009 — Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB)**
A highly selective semipermeable border formed by specialized brain endothelial cells, tight junctions, astrocytic end-feet, and pericytes. Protects the brain from pathogens and blood-borne toxins while allowing selective passage of oxygen, glucose, lipophilic drugs, and specific transport substrates. Major obstacle for CNS drug delivery.

**CC-010 — Neuroanatomy: Major Brain Divisions**
Forebrain (prosencephalon): cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, thalamus, hypothalamus, hippocampus, amygdala. Midbrain (mesencephalon): superior/inferior colliculi, substantia nigra, VTA. Hindbrain (rhombencephalon): cerebellum, pons, medulla oblongata. Spinal cord: 31 pairs of spinal nerves, ascending sensory and descending motor tracts.

**CC-011 — Cerebral Cortex**
The outer gray matter layer (~2–4 mm thick) of the cerebral hemispheres, comprising approximately 16 billion neurons. Organized into six horizontal layers (I–VI) with distinct cell types and connectivity. Functionally divided into lobes: frontal (executive function, motor), parietal (somatosensory, spatial), temporal (auditory, memory, language), occipital (visual).

**CC-012 — Hippocampus**
A medial temporal lobe structure critical for episodic memory formation and spatial navigation. Receives multimodal input via the entorhinal cortex. Patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), following bilateral hippocampal removal (1953), could form no new long-term declarative memories, establishing the hippocampus as essential for memory consolidation. Contains place cells (O'Keefe, 1971).

**CC-013 — Amygdala**
An almond-shaped medial temporal lobe nucleus complex critical for emotional processing, especially fear conditioning and threat detection. The basolateral amygdala (BLA) integrates sensory and contextual input; the central nucleus (CeA) drives fear responses (freezing, autonomic arousal, HPA axis activation). Also modulates memory consolidation for emotionally significant events.

**CC-014 — Basal Ganglia**
A collection of subcortical nuclei (striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra, VTA) involved in action selection, habit formation, reward learning, and motor control. The direct pathway facilitates movement; the indirect pathway suppresses competing actions. Dopamine from the substantia nigra modulates both pathways. Dysfunction causes Parkinson's disease (dopamine deficiency) and Huntington's disease.

**CC-015 — Cerebellum**
The "little brain" containing more neurons than the rest of the brain combined (~69 billion), housed in the posterior fossa. Responsible for motor coordination, fine-tuning of movement, balance, and motor learning (adaptation). Receives input from the cortex (via pons) and sensory systems; output via deep cerebellar nuclei to the thalamus and motor cortex. Also implicated in timing and some cognitive functions.

**CC-016 — Thalamus**
A bilateral diencephalic structure serving as the principal relay station for sensory and motor signals to the cortex. Specific relay nuclei (LGN for vision, MGN for audition, VPL/VPM for somatosensory) project to primary sensory cortices. Mediodorsal nucleus connects to prefrontal cortex. The thalamus is also critical for arousal and consciousness (thalamocortical loops).

**CC-017 — Hypothalamus**
A small but critical diencephalic structure controlling homeostasis: body temperature, hunger, thirst, sleep-wake cycles, circadian rhythms, sexual behavior, and stress response (via the HPA axis). Contains the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the master circadian clock. Controls the pituitary gland (master endocrine gland) via releasing hormones.

**CC-018 — Prefrontal Cortex (PFC)**
The anterior portion of the frontal lobe supporting executive functions: working memory, planning, decision-making, inhibitory control, social cognition, and personality. Divided into dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC, cognitive control), ventromedial PFC (vmPFC, value-based decisions, emotional regulation), and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC, reward valuation). Not fully matured until ~age 25.

**CC-019 — Dopaminergic Systems**
Two major dopaminergic pathways: mesolimbic (VTA → nucleus accumbens, amygdala, hippocampus) — reward prediction error signaling, motivation, addiction; mesocortical (VTA → prefrontal cortex) — working memory, executive function. Nigrostriatal (substantia nigra → striatum): motor control; degeneration causes Parkinson's. Tuberoinfundibular (hypothalamus → pituitary): prolactin regulation.

**CC-020 — Serotonergic System**
Serotonin (5-HT) is produced primarily in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem and projects widely throughout the brain. Modulates mood, appetite, sleep, nausea, and cognition. 14 receptor subtypes (5-HT1A–7). SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) are the first-line pharmacological treatment for major depression and anxiety disorders.

**CC-021 — Noradrenergic System**
Norepinephrine (NE) is produced in the locus coeruleus (LC) and projects broadly to the cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, and spinal cord. Regulates arousal, attention, stress response (fight-or-flight), and memory consolidation for emotionally significant events. The LC-NE system is the primary ascending arousal system alongside acetylcholine and orexin.

**CC-022 — Cholinergic System**
Acetylcholine (ACh) is produced in the basal forebrain (Meynert nucleus → cortex and hippocampus) and brainstem (pedunculopontine nucleus). Critical for attention, learning, memory, and REM sleep generation. Degeneration of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons is a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease. Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil) partially compensate.

**CC-023 — Neuroplasticity**
The brain's ability to reorganize structure, function, and connections in response to experience, learning, injury, or disease. Includes synaptic plasticity (LTP/LTD), structural plasticity (dendritic spine growth/retraction), and functional reorganization (cortical remapping after sensory deprivation or injury). Neuroplasticity is greatest in critical periods of development but persists throughout life.

**CC-024 — Adult Neurogenesis**
The production of new neurons in the adult brain. Definitively established in two regions: the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the hippocampal dentate gyrus and the subventricular zone (SVZ, feeding the olfactory bulb). Regulated by exercise, stress, antidepressants, and environmental enrichment. The extent of adult neurogenesis in the human hippocampus remains actively debated.

**CC-025 — Memory Systems**
Multiple distinct memory systems: Declarative (explicit): episodic (personal events, hippocampus-dependent) and semantic (general knowledge, neocortex-dependent). Nondeclarative (implicit): procedural/skill learning (striatum, cerebellum), priming (neocortex), classical conditioning (amygdala, cerebellum). Working memory: temporary online maintenance (PFC, parietal cortex).

**CC-026 — Sleep and Memory Consolidation**
Sleep is essential for memory consolidation. Slow-wave sleep (SWS/NREM3): hippocampal sharp-wave ripples replay memories, transferring them to neocortical long-term storage (systems consolidation). REM sleep: associated with emotional memory processing and procedural learning. Sleep deprivation impairs LTP induction and significantly reduces next-day memory encoding.

**CC-027 — Pain and Nociception**
Nociceptors (free nerve endings) detect tissue-damaging stimuli. Two main pathways: fast sharp pain via myelinated Aδ fibers; slow burning pain via unmyelinated C fibers. Signals travel via the spinothalamic tract to the thalamus and somatosensory cortex, and to the anterior cingulate cortex (emotional component). Gate control theory (Melzack & Wall, 1965) explains descending modulation. Endogenous opioids (endorphins, enkephalins) modulate pain via μ, δ, κ opioid receptors.

**CC-028 — Visual System**
Retinal photoreceptors (rods: low-light, no color; cones: color, high acuity) transduce light into electrical signals. Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) project via the optic nerve → optic chiasm → LGN of the thalamus → primary visual cortex (V1, occipital lobe). Parallel processing streams: dorsal ("where/how" stream to parietal cortex), ventral ("what" stream to temporal cortex). V1 is retinotopically organized; higher areas process motion, color, faces, objects.

**CC-029 — Auditory System**
Hair cells in the cochlea's organ of Corti transduce sound waves via mechanosensitive ion channels (prestin, stereocilia deflection). Tonotopic organization (frequency mapping) preserved from cochlea → cochlear nucleus → inferior colliculus → medial geniculate nucleus (MGN) → primary auditory cortex (A1, Heschl's gyrus). Binaural processing (ITD, ILD) enables sound localization.

**CC-030 — Motor System**
Voluntary movement is commanded by upper motor neurons in the primary motor cortex (M1, precentral gyrus), with contributions from premotor and supplementary motor areas. Corticospinal tract (pyramidal tract): the dominant pathway for fine voluntary movement. Lower motor neurons in the spinal cord innervate muscles. The cerebellum and basal ganglia modulate motor planning and execution without directly commanding muscles.

**CC-031 — Autonomic Nervous System (ANS)**
The ANS controls involuntary functions: heart rate, digestion, respiration, pupillary response, urination, sexual arousal. Sympathetic division: "fight-or-flight" (preganglionic from thoracolumbar spinal cord, NE as main neurotransmitter at targets). Parasympathetic division: "rest-and-digest" (preganglionic from craniosacral regions, ACh at targets). Enteric nervous system: semi-autonomous gut nervous system with ~500 million neurons.

**CC-032 — HPA Axis and Stress**
The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is the neuroendocrine stress response: hypothalamus releases CRH → pituitary releases ACTH → adrenal cortex releases cortisol. Cortisol mobilizes energy, modulates immune function, and provides negative feedback to the hypothalamus. Chronic HPA activation causes hippocampal neuronal damage (cortisol is neurotoxic at high chronic levels), contributing to stress-related psychiatric disorders.

**CC-033 — Consciousness and Neural Correlates**
Consciousness remains scientifically unresolved. Major theories: Global Workspace Theory (GWT, Baars/Dehaene): widespread cortical broadcasting of information underlies conscious access; Integrated Information Theory (IIT, Tononi): consciousness correlates with integrated information (Φ); Higher-Order Theories. Neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) include thalamocortical dynamics, P300 ERP components, and gamma oscillations (~40 Hz).

**CC-034 — Neurodegenerative Diseases**
Progressive loss of neurons: Alzheimer's disease (amyloid plaques, tau tangles, cholinergic/memory neurons); Parkinson's disease (α-synuclein Lewy bodies, nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons, motor control); Huntington's disease (CAG repeat expansion in HTT, striatal neuron loss, movement/cognition); ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, upper and lower motor neuron loss, motor function). All share proteotoxic aggregation and neuroinflammation as mechanisms.

**CC-035 — Psychiatric Disorders and Neuroscience**
Major depression: monoamine deficiency hypothesis (oversimplified); structural changes in hippocampus, PFC; HPA hyperactivation. Schizophrenia: dopamine hypothesis (excess D2 in mesolimbic, deficit in mesocortical); glutamate (NMDA hypofunction) hypothesis; cortical thinning, white matter changes. PTSD: amygdala hyperreactivity, hippocampal volume reduction, impaired vmPFC regulation of fear. Bipolar disorder: circadian rhythm dysregulation, altered neurotrophic signaling.

**CC-036 — Brain-Computer Interface (BCI)**
A direct communication pathway between the brain and an external device. Invasive BCIs (ECoG, intracortical arrays — Utah Array, Neuropixels): high spatial and temporal resolution; require surgery. Non-invasive BCIs (EEG, fNIRS): lower resolution but safer. Applications: motor prosthetics (Braingate), speech restoration (Chang lab, UCSF 2021), neurofeedback, locked-in syndrome communication. BrainGate demonstrated computer cursor control via single-unit recordings in M1.

**CC-037 — Neuroimaging Methods**
fMRI (functional MRI): BOLD signal (blood-oxygenation-level-dependent), ~1–3 mm spatial / ~2 s temporal resolution, infers neural activity from hemodynamic response. EEG: electrical potential, ~1 ms temporal resolution, poor spatial resolution; good for event-related potentials (ERPs). MEG: magnetic fields, millisecond timing, better spatial resolution than EEG. PET: radiotracer-based, quantifies neurotransmitter receptor density, metabolic activity. DTI: maps white matter tract connectivity.

**CC-038 — Optogenetics**
A technique using light to control genetically defined neurons expressing channelrhodopsins (ChR2: blue light activates) or halorhodopsins/archaerhodopsins (yellow/green light silences). Developed by Deisseroth and Boyden (2005); revolutionized causal circuit analysis in systems neuroscience. Allows millisecond-precision activation or silencing of specific cell types in vivo with genetic specificity unachievable by electrical stimulation.

## 5. Patterns

**P-001 — Hierarchical Processing**
Sensory systems process information in hierarchical stages, each extracting increasingly abstract features. V1 detects edges and orientations; V2/V4 integrate color and form; IT cortex represents objects and faces. This feed-forward hierarchy is ubiquitous in visual, auditory, and somatosensory systems and inspired deep convolutional neural networks.

**P-002 — Parallel Processing Streams**
The brain processes information along parallel pathways specializing in different features. The visual system's dorsal and ventral streams, the auditory system's "what" and "where" pathways, and the motor system's direct and indirect basal ganglia pathways all exemplify this architectural principle.

**P-003 — Topographic Organization**
Sensory and motor cortical areas maintain spatial maps of the body surface (somatotopy), retinal surface (retinotopy), or cochlear frequency axis (tonotopy). These maps are distorted by behavioral relevance: the somatosensory homunculus over-represents the hands and lips relative to the trunk.

**P-004 — Oscillatory Dynamics and Neural Synchrony**
Neural populations coordinate through synchronized oscillations at different frequencies: delta (0.5–4 Hz, deep sleep), theta (4–8 Hz, hippocampal memory encoding), alpha (8–12 Hz, cortical inhibition/idling), beta (13–30 Hz, motor control, cognitive maintenance), gamma (30–100 Hz, local information binding). Phase synchrony across regions is hypothesized to coordinate long-range communication.

**P-005 — Predictive Coding**
The brain continuously generates predictions about incoming sensory input and updates internal models based on prediction errors. Only the difference between prediction and input (prediction error) is propagated up the hierarchy. Proposed to explain perception, attention, hallucinations, and psychiatric symptoms (Friston Free Energy Principle).

**P-006 — Hebbian Learning**
"Neurons that fire together wire together" (Hebb, 1949). Co-activation strengthens synaptic connections; this principle underlies LTP and associative learning. The formal version (Bienenstock-Cooper-Munro, BCM) includes a sliding threshold preventing unbounded potentiation.

**P-007 — Neuromodulation of State**
Neuromodulatory systems (dopamine, acetylcholine, norepinephrine, serotonin) shift global brain states rather than transmitting point-to-point information. They set the gain, signal-to-noise, and plasticity of cortical and subcortical circuits, enabling flexible adaptation to behavioral context (arousal, fear, reward, sleep).

**P-008 — Redundancy and Distributed Representation**
Information is represented across distributed populations of neurons rather than single "grandmother cells" (except in very high-level regions like the hippocampus where sparse, selective representations exist). Distributed codes are robust to single-neuron loss, which is critical for a system operating under constant neuronal turnover and injury.

**P-009 — Critical Periods and Sensitive Windows**
Neural circuits exhibit heightened plasticity during specific developmental windows. Visual critical period: closure of one eye during this period (amblyopia) permanently degrades acuity in that eye. Language acquisition: most efficient before puberty. Disrupting sensory experience or social input during critical periods has lasting structural consequences.

**P-010 — Synaptic Pruning**
During development, an initial overproduction of synapses (synaptogenesis) is followed by activity-dependent elimination of weaker connections. Complement proteins (C1q, C3) tag weak synapses for microglial phagocytosis. Aberrant pruning has been linked to schizophrenia (excess pruning of prefrontal synapses in adolescence) and autism (insufficient pruning).

**P-011 — Default Mode Network (DMN)**
A set of brain regions (medial PFC, posterior cingulate cortex, angular gyrus, hippocampus) that are active during rest, self-referential thought, mind-wandering, and future planning, and deactivated during demanding cognitive tasks. DMN disruption is implicated in Alzheimer's disease, depression, and schizophrenia.

**P-012 — Reentry and Recurrent Connectivity**
Corticocortical and thalamocortical circuits are massively recurrent; lower areas receive feedback projections from higher areas (feedback exceeds feedforward in many cortical systems). Reentrant signaling is proposed as a mechanism for binding distributed representations into unified percepts and sustaining working memory.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP-001 — The 10% Brain Myth**
The claim that humans only use 10% of their brain is entirely false. Neuroimaging shows that virtually all brain regions are active at some point; no large-scale silent regions exist. The myth likely originated from misinterpretations of early neurological studies and William James's motivational writings.

**AP-002 — Left Brain / Right Brain Personality Typology**
The claim that people are "left-brained" (logical) or "right-brained" (creative) as fixed personality types is a pop-science oversimplification. While hemispheric specialization is real (language left-lateralized in most right-handers), individuals do not preferentially use one hemisphere; both hemispheres are active and integrated during virtually all cognitive tasks.

**AP-003 — Equating Brain Region Activation with Function**
Reverse inference: inferring a specific mental state from fMRI activation of a region (e.g., "the amygdala activated, so the subject was afraid") is logically flawed. Virtually all brain regions are activated by multiple tasks. Valid inference requires meta-analytic priors (NeuroSynth) and convergent methods.

**AP-004 — Confusing Correlation with Causation in Neuroimaging**
fMRI correlates neural activity with behavior but cannot establish causal relationships. Only methods with causal leverage (TMS/tDCS perturbation, optogenetics, lesion studies) can test causal necessity. Over-interpreting correlational neuroimaging as causal is a systematic error in popular neuroscience communication.

**AP-005 — Single-Neurotransmitter Disease Theories**
The "serotonin imbalance causes depression" narrative is an oversimplification unsupported by the full evidence base. Psychiatric disorders involve complex interactions among multiple neurotransmitter systems, neural circuits, genetics, and environment. Reducing them to single-molecule deficits misguides both research and patient expectations.

**AP-006 — Treating the Developing Brain Like an Adult Brain**
Adolescent brains differ structurally and functionally from adult brains: the PFC is not fully myelinated until ~25 years, dopaminergic mesolimbic reward circuitry is more reactive relative to cortical control, and sleep architecture differs. Policies, clinical interventions, and drug dosing based on adult brain models applied to developing brains cause systematic errors.

**AP-007 — Assuming Neuroplasticity Is Unlimited**
Neuroplasticity is real but constrained. Adult neurogenesis is limited to specific regions; critical periods close; many forms of plasticity require specific molecular conditions. The self-help narrative of "rewire your brain" without qualification overpromises plasticity in ways that mislead patients and practitioners.

**AP-008 — Oversimplifying Neurological Disorders as Binary**
Neurological and psychiatric conditions exist on continuous spectra influenced by genetic, developmental, and environmental factors. Treating diagnoses (autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, bipolar disorder) as discrete binary categories ignores dimensional variation and comorbidity, leading to under- or over-diagnosis and mismatched treatment.

## 7. Facts

**F-001** — The human brain contains approximately 86 billion neurons (Azevedo et al., 2009, via isotropic fractionation) and approximately the same number of glial cells (~85 billion).

**F-002** — The cerebral cortex, if unfolded, would have a surface area of approximately 2,500 cm² (about the size of a page of a broadsheet newspaper).

**F-003** — The brain represents approximately 2% of body weight but consumes approximately 20% of the body's oxygen and glucose at rest.

**F-004** — The fastest conducting nerve fibers (Aα myelinated motor efferents to muscle spindles) conduct at up to 120 m/s (432 km/h); the slowest unmyelinated pain fibers (C fibers) conduct at 0.5–2 m/s.

**F-005** — Resting membrane potential of a typical neuron is approximately −70 mV, established primarily by the sodium-potassium ATPase pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per cycle).

**F-006** — A single neuron can receive input from thousands of synapses; Purkinje cells of the cerebellum receive input from up to 200,000 parallel fiber synapses.

**F-007** — The synaptic cleft is approximately 20–40 nm wide; neurotransmitter diffusion across it takes approximately 0.1–0.2 ms.

**F-008** — NMDA receptors are called "coincidence detectors" because they require simultaneous presynaptic glutamate release AND postsynaptic depolarization (to relieve the Mg²⁺ block) to activate — the cellular basis for associative learning.

**F-009** — Patient H.M. (Henry Molaison, 1926–2008) was the most studied individual in neuroscience history; his bilateral medial temporal lobe resection (1953) established the hippocampus as necessary for new episodic memory formation.

**F-010** — Place cells in the hippocampus (O'Keefe, 1971) and grid cells in the entorhinal cortex (Moser & Moser, 2005) constitute an internal GPS system; O'Keefe and the Mosers shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

**F-011** — The cerebellum contains approximately 69 billion neurons — more than the rest of the brain combined — mostly granule cells; Purkinje cells, though far fewer (~15 million), provide the sole output of the cerebellar cortex.

**F-012** — Broca's area (left inferior frontal gyrus, BA 44/45) is critical for speech production; Wernicke's area (left superior temporal gyrus, BA 22) is critical for language comprehension; damage to the arcuate fasciculus connecting them causes conduction aphasia.

**F-013** — The human brain's white matter, if unraveled, would stretch approximately 150,000 km — enough to circle Earth about 4 times.

**F-014** — LTP was first described by Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo in 1973 in the rabbit hippocampus; it remains the dominant cellular model of associative learning 50+ years later.

**F-015** — Cortisol at high chronic levels causes apical dendrite retraction in CA3 hippocampal pyramidal neurons, reducing synaptic input and hippocampal volume — a structural correlate of stress-induced memory impairment.

**F-016** — The blood-brain barrier is formed by tight junctions (claudin-5, occludin, ZO-1) between brain endothelial cells; lipophilicity and molecular weight <400–600 Da predict CNS penetrance for small molecules.

**F-017** — fMRI BOLD signal reflects changes in deoxyhemoglobin concentration (paramagnetic) vs. oxyhemoglobin (diamagnetic); the hemodynamic response peaks 5–6 seconds after neural activity, introducing temporal lag.

**F-018** — The adult human hippocampus produces approximately 700 new neurons per day in the dentate gyrus (Spalding et al., 2013 via ¹⁴C birth-dating) — though the magnitude and functional significance of adult human neurogenesis remain debated.

**F-019** — Alzheimer's disease affects approximately 50 million people globally (2023 estimate); amyloid plaques (extracellular Aβ42 aggregates) and neurofibrillary tangles (intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau) are the neuropathological hallmarks.

**F-020** — Parkinson's disease involves loss of >60% of substantia nigra pars compacta dopaminergic neurons before motor symptoms (bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor) become apparent.

**F-021** — The substantia nigra pars compacta produces neuromelanin (a byproduct of dopamine synthesis), giving it a visibly dark pigmentation in the brainstem — clearly reduced in Parkinson's disease tissue.

**F-022** — Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus dramatically reduces motor symptoms in Parkinson's disease; the mechanism is thought to involve disruption of pathological beta oscillations in the basal ganglia-thalamic circuit.

**F-023** — The mirror neuron system, discovered in macaque premotor cortex (F5 area) by Rizzolatti et al. (1992), fires both when a monkey performs an action AND when it observes another performing the same action; evidence for a homologous system in humans comes from neuroimaging.

**F-024** — Optogenetics was developed by Deisseroth and Boyden (2005); channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2), a naturally occurring algal light-gated ion channel, was the first tool; the field has since expanded to hundreds of opsin variants.

**F-025** — The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), approximately 20,000 neurons in the anterior hypothalamus, is the master circadian clock. Bilateral SCN lesions eliminate circadian rhythms; transplanted SCN restores rhythms with donor's period.

**F-026** — Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) remains the most effective treatment for severe, treatment-resistant depression; it induces a brief generalized seizure and has a response rate of 70–90% in this population.

**F-027** — Ketamine (NMDA receptor antagonist) produces rapid antidepressant effects within hours, compared to 2–6 weeks for SSRIs; esketamine (Spravato) received FDA approval in 2019 for treatment-resistant depression.

**F-028** — The glymphatic system (Nedergaard lab, 2012) is a perivascular waste-clearance system that flushes amyloid beta and other metabolic waste from the brain primarily during sleep via aquaporin-4 water channels on astrocyte end-feet.

**F-029** — Pain catastrophizing (the tendency to ruminate on pain and feel helpless) is a stronger predictor of chronic pain disability than the degree of tissue damage, demonstrating the primacy of top-down central processing over peripheral injury.

**F-030** — The vestibular system (utricle, saccule: linear acceleration; semicircular canals: rotational acceleration) provides the brain with inertial navigation input, integrated with visual and proprioceptive signals in the vestibular nuclei and cerebellum for balance and spatial orientation.

**F-031** — Phantom limb pain, experienced by ~70–80% of amputees, arises from maladaptive cortical reorganization and spontaneous activity in deafferented cortical regions; mirror therapy and virtual reality body illusions provide evidence-based relief.

**F-032** — Adult cortical thickness averages ~2.5 mm; gray matter volume peaks in early adulthood and declines at approximately 0.5% per year in normal aging, accelerating in neurodegenerative disease.

**F-033** — The human genome contains approximately 20,000 genes; roughly half are expressed in the brain, making the brain the most transcriptionally complex organ in the body.

**F-034** — Braingate clinical trials demonstrated that a tetraplegic patient could control a robotic arm with sufficient dexterity to drink coffee unaided, using intracortical microelectrode arrays recording M1 population activity.

**F-035** — Neuralink's first human implant (January 2024, patient Noland Arbaugh) demonstrated control of a computer cursor and games via a 1,024-electrode intracortical array implanted in motor cortex.

**F-036** — The P300 ERP component (a positive voltage deflection ~300 ms post-stimulus in EEG) is elicited by rare, task-relevant stimuli and is the basis of "oddball" BCI communication paradigms for locked-in patients.

**F-037** — Sleep deprivation for 17–19 hours produces cognitive impairment equivalent to a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05%; 24-hour sleep deprivation is equivalent to 0.10% BAC (Williamson & Feyer, 2000).

**F-038** — The thalamus contains approximately 20 specific relay nuclei; the pulvinar is the largest, receiving multimodal input and projecting to association cortices; it is implicated in attention and consciousness.

**F-039** — GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) is the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in the adult brain; during fetal development, GABA is depolarizing (excitatory) because the KCC2 chloride transporter is not yet expressed.

**F-040** — Benzodiazepines (diazepam, lorazepam) are positive allosteric modulators of GABA-A receptors, increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening in response to GABA, producing anxiolytic, sedative, anticonvulsant, and amnestic effects.
