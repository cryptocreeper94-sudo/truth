## 4. Core Concepts

**C01 — Earth's Internal Structure**
Definition: Earth is differentiated into four major layers: inner core (solid iron-nickel alloy, ~1,220 km radius, ~5,100–6,370 km depth), outer core (liquid iron-nickel, ~2,250 km thick, generates magnetic field), mantle (silicate rock, ~2,900 km thick, convecting), and crust (5–70 km thick, oceanic and continental types).
Key relationships: Seismic wave velocity profiles (P and S waves), MOHO discontinuity, Gutenberg discontinuity, Lehmann discontinuity.

**C02 — Plate Tectonics**
Definition: The theory that Earth's lithosphere is divided into ~15 major and several minor tectonic plates that move relative to each other, driven by mantle convection, slab pull, and ridge push. Plate boundaries are divergent (spreading), convergent (subduction or collision), or transform (lateral slip).
Key relationships: Wilson cycle, supercontinent cycle, seafloor spreading, subduction zones, orogenic belts.

**C03 — Lithosphere and Asthenosphere**
Definition: The lithosphere is Earth's rigid outer layer (crust + uppermost mantle, ~100 km thick for oceanic, ~200 km for continental). It overlies the asthenosphere — a weak, partially molten mantle zone (~100–350 km depth) that permits viscous flow on geological timescales.
Key relationships: Isostasy (Airy, Pratt, Vening-Meinesz), elastic rebound, thermal subsidence.

**C04 — Seafloor Spreading**
Definition: At mid-ocean ridges, mantle upwelling creates new oceanic crust through volcanic activity and intrusion. Magnetic anomaly stripes on either side of ridges record reversals of Earth's magnetic field, providing evidence for spreading rates (1–20 cm/yr).
Key relationships: Paleomagnetism, Vine-Matthews-Morley hypothesis, transform faults, age-depth relationship of oceanic crust.

**C05 — Subduction**
Definition: The process by which a denser tectonic plate descends beneath a less dense plate into the mantle. Oceanic lithosphere (denser) subducts under oceanic or continental lithosphere. Generates volcanic arcs, deep-focus earthquakes, and accretionary wedges.
Key relationships: Benioff zone (Wadati-Benioff), volcanic arc (Cascade Range, Andes), back-arc basin, slab rollback, blueschist metamorphism.

**C06 — Continental Collision**
Definition: Convergence of two continental plates (both buoyant, neither subducts) produces orogenic (mountain-building) belts through crustal thickening, folding, and faulting. Example: India-Eurasia collision producing the Himalayas (~50 Ma to present).
Key relationships: Suture zone, ophiolite, fold-thrust belt, crustal root, isostatic rebound.

**C07 — Transform Faults**
Definition: Plate boundaries where plates slide laterally past each other with no creation or destruction of crust. Perpendicular to spreading ridges (oceanic transforms); on continents, produce strike-slip fault systems.
Key relationships: San Andreas Fault (North American-Pacific), Dead Sea Transform, seismicity, right-lateral vs. left-lateral sense.

**C08 — The Rock Cycle**
Definition: The continuous set of processes by which rocks transform among the three major types: igneous (crystallization from magma), sedimentary (deposition and lithification of particles), and metamorphic (recrystallization under heat and pressure). No rock type is permanent.
Key relationships: Weathering, erosion, deposition, burial, melting, uplift, crystallization.

**C09 — Igneous Rocks**
Definition: Rocks formed by the cooling and crystallization of magma (underground) or lava (surface). Intrusive (plutonic) rocks cool slowly → coarse-grained (granite, gabbro). Extrusive (volcanic) rocks cool rapidly → fine-grained or glassy (basalt, rhyolite, obsidian).
Key relationships: Bowen's reaction series, magma differentiation, SiO₂ content classification (mafic, intermediate, felsic), texture vs. composition.

**C10 — Bowen's Reaction Series**
Definition: Describes the order in which minerals crystallize from cooling magma. Discontinuous series: olivine → pyroxene → amphibole → biotite. Continuous series: Ca-plagioclase → Na-plagioclase. Both converge at orthoclase, muscovite, quartz.
Key relationships: Magmatic differentiation, assimilation, cumulate rocks, mineral stability.

**C11 — Sedimentary Rocks**
Definition: Rocks formed by the accumulation, compaction, and cementation of sediment (clastic), precipitation from solution (chemical), or accumulation of organic material (biochemical). Record depositional environments, climate, and biological history.
Key relationships: Grain size (Wentworth scale), sorting, bedding, sedimentary structures (cross-bedding, ripple marks, graded bedding), diagenesis.

**C12 — Metamorphic Rocks**
Definition: Rocks transformed by heat, pressure, or chemically active fluids without melting. Foliated types (slate, phyllite, schist, gneiss) show aligned minerals. Non-foliated types (marble, quartzite, hornfels) lack preferred orientation.
Key relationships: Metamorphic facies (zeolite, blueschist, greenschist, amphibolite, granulite, eclogite), P-T paths, index minerals (chlorite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite/sillimanite/andalusite).

**C13 — Mineralogy**
Definition: The study of minerals — naturally occurring, inorganic, crystalline solids with definite chemical composition and ordered atomic structure. ~5,900 mineral species recognized. Classified by anion group: silicates (most abundant), carbonates, oxides, sulfides, halides, phosphates, sulfates.
Key relationships: Crystal system (cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, hexagonal/trigonal), Mohs hardness scale, cleavage, fracture, luster.

**C14 — Silicate Minerals**
Definition: The dominant mineral class, comprising ~90% of Earth's crust by volume. Built on SiO₄ tetrahedra linked in various configurations: nesosilicates (olivine), sorosilicates, cyclosilicates, inosilicates (pyroxene, amphibole), phyllosilicates (micas, clays), tectosilicates (feldspar, quartz).
Key relationships: Substitution and solid solution, Si:O ratio, chemical weathering susceptibility (Goldich stability series).

**C15 — Seismology**
Definition: The study of seismic waves (elastic waves propagating through Earth) generated by earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, explosions, or other sources. P-waves (compressional, travel through solids and liquids), S-waves (shear, solids only), surface waves (Love, Rayleigh).
Key relationships: Seismograph, seismogram, epicenter, hypocenter (focus), seismic tomography, shadow zone (outer core).

**C16 — Earthquake Mechanics**
Definition: Earthquakes occur when accumulated elastic strain energy on a fault is released suddenly as slip. The seismic moment M₀ = μ × A × D (shear modulus × rupture area × average slip). Moment magnitude M_w = (2/3) log(M₀) − 10.7.
Key relationships: Elastic rebound theory (Reid), fault types (normal, reverse, strike-slip), focal mechanism, aftershock sequences, seismic hazard.

**C17 — Fault Classification**
Definition: Faults classified by relative motion: Normal fault (hanging wall down, extensional); Reverse fault (hanging wall up, compressional; thrust fault if dip <30°); Strike-slip fault (lateral motion, left-lateral or right-lateral); Oblique-slip combines components.
Key relationships: Horst and graben (normal faults), thrust sheets, fold-thrust belts, seismogenic zone depth.

**C18 — Volcanology**
Definition: Study of volcanoes, magma, eruption mechanisms, and volcanic products. Eruption style depends on magma viscosity and volatile content: effusive (low-viscosity basaltic lava flows) vs. explosive (high-viscosity, high-silica, volatile-rich magma producing tephra and pyroclastic flows).
Key relationships: Volcanic explosivity index (VEI), pyroclastic density currents, lahar, caldera formation, shield vs. stratovolcano morphology.

**C19 — Magma Types and Composition**
Definition: Magma classified by SiO₂ content: basaltic (~50% SiO₂, low viscosity, mafic minerals), andesitic (~60%, intermediate), rhyolitic (~70%, high viscosity, felsic, explosive). Generated by decompression melting (ridges), flux melting (subduction zones), or heat transfer (hot spots).
Key relationships: Mantle melting fraction, volatile solubility, degassing, eruption column dynamics.

**C20 — Hot Spots and Mantle Plumes**
Definition: Intraplate volcanic centers attributed to anomalously hot mantle upwellings (plumes) that create chains of volcanic islands/seamounts as the plate moves over them.
Key relationships: Hawaiian-Emperor chain, Yellowstone caldera, Iceland (ridge-centered plume), plume head and conduit, Large Igneous Provinces (LIPs).

**C21 — Stratigraphy**
Definition: The study of rock layers (strata) and their temporal and spatial relationships. Lithostratigraphy (physical character), biostratigraphy (fossil content), chronostratigraphy (time), and magnetostratigraphy (magnetic reversals) provide complementary correlation tools.
Key relationships: Law of superposition (Steno), lateral continuity, original horizontality, index fossils, unconformities.

**C22 — Geochronology**
Definition: The science of determining the age of rocks, minerals, and geological events. Radiometric dating uses radioactive decay with known half-lives: U-Pb (~4.5 Ga half-life, suitable for ancient rocks), K-Ar (igneous), Ar-Ar (improved precision), Rb-Sr, Sm-Nd, Re-Os, ¹⁴C (organic material <50,000 yr).
Key relationships: Concordia diagram (U-Pb), isochron method, closure temperature, discordance, geologic time scale.

**C23 — Geologic Time Scale**
Definition: The hierarchical division of Earth's 4.54 Ga history: Eons (Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic, Phanerozoic) → Eras (Paleozoic, Mesozoic, Cenozoic) → Periods (e.g., Cambrian, Jurassic, Cretaceous) → Epochs → Ages. Boundaries defined by stratigraphic events and calibrated radiometrically.
Key relationships: GSSP (Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point), mass extinctions, biozone markers.

**C24 — Paleontology**
Definition: The study of prehistoric life through fossil evidence — preserved remains (hard parts, molds, casts, tracks, chemical traces). Documents the history of life, evolution, extinction events, and past environments.
Key relationships: Taphonomy, preservation potential, index fossils, biostratigraphy, mass extinctions (Big Five).

**C25 — Mass Extinctions**
Definition: Events in which >75% of species go extinct in a geologically short interval. The Big Five: end-Ordovician (~443 Ma, glaciation), Late Devonian (~375 Ma, anoxia), end-Permian (~252 Ma, "The Great Dying," ~96% species lost, Siberian Traps), end-Triassic (~201 Ma), end-Cretaceous (~66 Ma, Chicxulub impactor + Deccan Traps).
Key relationships: Benton's kill mechanisms, recovery intervals, evolutionary diversification following extinction.

**C26 — Geomorphology**
Definition: The study of landform origin, classification, and evolution. Fluvial (river erosion/deposition), glacial, aeolian (wind), coastal, karst (dissolution), tectonic, and volcanic geomorphic systems each produce characteristic landform assemblages.
Key relationships: Base level, grade, equilibrium, erosion rate, hillslope processes, drainage basin, watershed divide.

**C27 — Hydrology**
Definition: The study of water distribution, movement, and properties in the hydrological cycle. Surface hydrology (streamflow, flooding, drainage basins); hydrogeology (groundwater, aquifers, recharge, porosity, permeability); watershed management.
Key relationships: Darcy's law (groundwater flow), water table, potentiometric surface, aquifer types (confined, unconfined, artesian), hydrograph.

**C28 — Glaciology**
Definition: Study of ice in all forms: glaciers, ice sheets, sea ice, permafrost. Glaciers move by internal deformation (creep) and basal sliding; erode through plucking and abrasion; deposit moraines, drumlins, eskers, and outwash plains.
Key relationships: Glacial cycles (Milankovitch cycles), ice cores (paleoclimate archives), isostatic rebound, glacial flour, fjords.

**C29 — Economic Geology**
Definition: Application of geological principles to discovery and extraction of mineral resources. Ore deposits classified by origin: magmatic (chromite, PGE), hydrothermal (gold, copper, porphyry, VMS), sedimentary (iron formations, evaporites), placer (gold, diamonds), weathering residual (bauxite, laterite).
Key relationships: Grade and tonnage, metallogenic provinces, ore genesis, exploration geochemistry.

**C30 — Petroleum Geology**
Definition: The geology of oil and gas accumulations. Requires: source rock (organic-rich, thermally mature), reservoir rock (porous, permeable), cap rock (seal), and structural or stratigraphic trap. Hydrocarbons generated during catagenesis (oil window: 60–120°C; gas window: >120°C).
Key relationships: Total organic carbon (TOC), vitrinite reflectance (maturity indicator), porosity, permeability, trap types (anticline, fault, salt dome, stratigraphic).

**C31 — Geochemistry**
Definition: Study of the distribution and cycling of chemical elements and isotopes in Earth systems. Major subfields: isotope geochemistry (radiogenic and stable isotopes), organic geochemistry, cosmochemistry, low-temperature geochemistry.
Key relationships: Goldschmidt classification (lithophile, siderophile, chalcophile, atmophile), partition coefficients, stable isotope fractionation (δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O, δD).

**C32 — Earth's Magnetic Field (Geomagnetism)**
Definition: Earth's magnetic field is generated by convective motion of the liquid outer core (geodynamo). The field undergoes secular variation, polarity excursions, and full reversals (geomagnetic reversals) recorded in magnetized rocks — the basis of magnetostratigraphy.
Key relationships: Dipole and non-dipole components, virtual geomagnetic pole (VGP), paleomagnetic inclination (paleolatitude), geomagnetic polarity time scale (GPTS).

**C33 — Soil Science (Pedology)**
Definition: The study of soil formation (pedogenesis), classification, and spatial distribution. Soil forms through weathering of parent material, influenced by climate, organisms, topography, and time. USDA Soil Taxonomy and WRB classify soils into 12 orders.
Key relationships: Soil horizons (O, A, B, C, R), soil pH, cation exchange capacity (CEC), soil texture (sand, silt, clay), weathering and leaching profiles.

**C34 — Isostasy**
Definition: The gravitational equilibrium between Earth's lithosphere and the underlying asthenosphere. Crustal columns of equal pressure exist at a compensation depth. Thicker/less dense crust stands higher; thinner/denser crust stands lower. Perturbations (loading, erosion, glacial melting) drive isostatic adjustment.
Key relationships: Airy isostasy (crustal root depth variation), Pratt isostasy (density variation), glacio-isostatic rebound (Scandinavia, Hudson Bay).

**C35 — Natural Hazard Geology**
Definition: Assessment and mitigation of geological hazards: earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis, landslides, subsidence, flooding, and coastal erosion. Probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA) quantifies ground-shaking probability at a site.
Key relationships: Return period, PGA (peak ground acceleration), liquefaction, lahars, pyroclastic density currents, landslide triggering factors.

## 5. Patterns

**P01 — Stratigraphic Correlation**
Description: Use lithostratigraphic (rock type), biostratigraphic (index fossils), or chemostratigraphic (isotope excursions) markers to correlate rock units between geographically separated sections.
When to use: Reconstructing depositional environments, establishing relative age relationships, basin analysis.
Example: The Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary iridium anomaly and shocked quartz worldwide enables global correlation of the K-Pg mass extinction horizon.

**P02 — Actualism (Uniformitarianism)**
Description: Interpret ancient geological features using processes observable today operating at similar rates, unless evidence supports catastrophic events.
When to use: Environmental reconstruction from ancient strata; interpreting sedimentary structures.
Example: Interpret cross-bedded sandstone as dune migration using modern desert analog; interpret graded beds (Bouma sequences) using modern turbidite systems.

**P03 — Radiometric Age Dating**
Description: Select appropriate isotope system for the target age range and material: U-Pb for zircon (any age); K-Ar for volcanic minerals (>100 ka); ¹⁴C for organic material (<50 ka); Rb-Sr for metamorphic rocks; apply isochron method to correct for initial ratio variation.
When to use: Establishing absolute ages for geological events and rock formations.
Example: U-Pb zircon dating of igneous intrusions cross-cutting faults constrains fault age.

**P04 — Geophysical Survey Design**
Description: Select appropriate geophysical method (seismic reflection/refraction, gravity, magnetics, electromagnetic, ground-penetrating radar) based on target depth, contrast, and resolution requirements.
When to use: Subsurface imaging for resource exploration, hazard characterization, structural mapping.
Example: Seismic reflection for petroleum reservoir imaging; ground-penetrating radar for near-surface void detection.

**P05 — Fault Kinematic Analysis**
Description: Measure striation orientations (slickenlines) on fault surfaces and use fault-slip data inversion to determine the paleostress tensor (principal stress axes σ₁, σ₂, σ₃).
When to use: Tectonic reconstruction, understanding structural history of a region.
Example: Anderson's theory of faulting: normal faults form when σ₁ is vertical; reverse faults when σ₃ is vertical; strike-slip when σ₂ is vertical.

**P06 — Mass Balance Calculation**
Description: Track conservation of mass through a geological process (erosion, chemical weathering, metamorphism) to quantify material transfer and transformation.
When to use: Weathering profile interpretation, ore grade estimation, basin sediment budget.
Example: Isocon analysis (Grant method) quantifies mass gains and losses during hydrothermal alteration using immobile element ratios.

**P07 — Mineral Identification Procedure**
Description: Systematically apply physical properties (hardness, cleavage, fracture, luster, color, streak, specific gravity, crystal form) and optical properties (in thin section: birefringence, extinction angle, pleochroism) to identify unknown minerals.
When to use: Petrographic analysis, ore mineral identification, soil mineral characterization.
Example: Identify olivine: high birefringence, no cleavage, orthrhombic system, alteration to serpentine.

**P08 — Sedimentary Facies Analysis**
Description: Interpret depositional environment from the suite of sedimentary structures, grain size, sorting, fossil content, and stratigraphic context (facies associations).
When to use: Paleoenvironmental reconstruction, reservoir characterization.
Example: Coarsening-upward cycles indicate shallowing (deltaic progradation); fining-upward cycles indicate deepening or channel migration.

**P09 — Geomorphic Process-Form Coupling**
Description: Interpret landform morphology (slope angle, drainage density, valley shape) to infer the dominant geomorphic process and its rate relative to tectonic forcing.
When to use: Landscape evolution modeling, hazard assessment, erosion rate estimation.
Example: High stream power index (SPI) and steepness index in channels indicate active tectonic uplift; knickpoints mark transient response to base level change.

**P10 — Volcanic Hazard Zonation**
Description: Map probabilistic hazard zones around volcanoes based on eruptive history, DEM analysis, and flow modeling for lava, pyroclastic, lahar, and tephra hazards.
When to use: Land use planning around active volcanoes, emergency response planning.
Example: Lahar hazard mapping around Merapi (Indonesia) using paleochannel mapping and flow model outputs.

**P11 — Hydrogeological Pump Test Analysis**
Description: Conduct aquifer pump tests (constant-rate or step-drawdown) and analyze time-drawdown curves using Theis, Cooper-Jacob, or other analytical solutions to determine transmissivity (T) and storage coefficient (S).
When to use: Groundwater resource assessment, aquifer characterization.
Example: Theis equation: s = (Q/4πT) × W(u), where u = r²S/4Tt; drawdown s at distance r, time t.

**P12 — Provenance Analysis**
Description: Determine the source terrane of sediment using mineral assemblages, heavy mineral suites, detrital zircon U-Pb ages, geochemical signatures, and paleocurrent indicators.
When to use: Basin analysis, reconstructing paleogeography, correlating subsurface reservoirs.
Example: Detrital zircon age spectrum from a sandstone compared to potential source terrane age peaks identifies sediment routing.

**P13 — Geotechnical Site Investigation**
Description: Characterize subsurface conditions (soil type, strength, groundwater, bearing capacity) for engineering purposes using borehole logging, SPT tests, CPT, geophysics, and laboratory testing.
When to use: Foundation design, slope stability analysis, liquefaction assessment.
Example: Standard Penetration Test (SPT) N-values used in Seed-Idriss method for earthquake liquefaction potential.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP01 — Applying Uniformitarianism Rigidly to Catastrophic Events**
Why wrong: Some geological events (large bolide impacts, LIP eruptions, rapid glacial lake outburst floods) produce features that have no modern analog and operated at rates orders of magnitude faster than typical processes.
What to do instead: Apply the principle of actualism (processes are the same) but allow rates and magnitudes to vary; recognize event-driven stratigraphy (impact layers, flood basalts).

**AP02 — Treating Rock Units as Time-Equivalent Across Basins**
Why wrong: Similar-looking rock types can form at very different times in different basins. Lithostratigraphic correlation without biostratigraphic or geochronological control frequently leads to miscorrelation.
What to do instead: Use index fossils, radiometric dates, or chemostratigraphic markers to establish time equivalence before lithological correlation.

**AP03 — Ignoring Structural Complexity in Subsurface Interpretations**
Why wrong: Seismic reflection profiles show time, not depth; velocity-depth conversion introduces uncertainty. Folds, faults, and unconformities can create "phantom" structures.
What to do instead: Apply depth migration using accurate velocity models; calibrate with well data; consider multiple structural interpretations consistent with the data.

**AP04 — Conflating Geologic Age with Stratigraphic Position in Deformed Terranes**
Why wrong: In thrust belts, fold nappes, or zones of tectonic inversion, older rocks can structurally overlie younger rocks (inverted stratigraphy). Superposition does not apply without first establishing structural history.
What to do instead: Map structures, determine kinematics (way-up criteria, cross-cutting relationships), and reconstruct palinspastic position before applying stratigraphic principles.

**AP05 — Using Single-Element Geochemistry for Tectonic Discrimination**
Why wrong: Single major or trace elements are controlled by many factors (fractional crystallization, crustal contamination, source heterogeneity) and cannot uniquely identify tectonic setting.
What to do instead: Use multi-element discrimination diagrams (Ti-Zr-Y, Th-Hf-Nb) and immobile element ratios; validate with multiple independent lines of evidence.

**AP06 — Treating Earthquake Recurrence as Perfectly Periodic**
Why wrong: Earthquake recurrence intervals are statistical, not clockwork. Stress interactions (Coulomb stress transfer), seismic clustering, and fault complexity produce highly variable inter-event times.
What to do instead: Use probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA) with appropriate recurrence models (Poisson, BPT, time-dependent); account for uncertainty in all parameters.

**AP07 — Ignoring Secondary Permeability in Hydrogeology**
Why wrong: Many aquifers derive their hydraulic conductivity from fractures, dissolution cavities, or bedding planes, not matrix porosity alone. Matrix-only analysis underestimates permeability dramatically in carbonate or fractured crystalline rock aquifers.
What to do instead: Characterize fracture density, orientation, aperture, and connectivity using borehole imaging, tracer tests, and pumping test derivative analysis.
