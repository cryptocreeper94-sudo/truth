## 4. Core Concepts

**C01 — Phonetics**
Definition: The study of the physical properties of speech sounds — their production (articulatory phonetics), acoustic properties (acoustic phonetics), and perception (auditory phonetics). The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) provides a universal notation system for all human speech sounds.
Key relationships: Place of articulation (bilabial, alveolar, velar, glottal), manner of articulation (stop, fricative, affricate, nasal, lateral, approximant), voicing, vowel height and backness, formants.

**C02 — Phonology**
Definition: The study of how sounds function as a system within a language. Phonemes are the abstract contrastive units; allophones are their phonetic realizations in context. Minimal pairs demonstrate phonemic contrast (e.g., /p/ vs. /b/: "pat" vs. "bat").
Key relationships: Phoneme, allophone, minimal pair, distinctive features (Jakobson), syllable structure (onset-nucleus-coda), stress, tone, intonation.

**C03 — Morphology**
Definition: The study of the internal structure of words. Morphemes are the minimal meaningful units. Free morphemes stand alone (e.g., "cat"); bound morphemes require attachment (e.g., "-ing," "un-"). Inflectional morphology marks grammatical categories (tense, number, case); derivational morphology creates new words.
Key relationships: Root, stem, affix (prefix, suffix, infix, circumfix), inflection vs. derivation, compounding, clitics, morphological typology (isolating, agglutinative, fusional, polysynthetic).

**C04 — Syntax**
Definition: The study of sentence structure — the rules governing how words combine into phrases and sentences. Constituent structure (phrase structure rules) and dependency structure represent grammatical relationships differently but both describe the same structural relations.
Key relationships: Phrase structure grammar (X-bar theory), transformational grammar (Chomsky), dependency grammar, tree diagram, grammatical functions (subject, object, predicate), agreement, case marking.

**C05 — X-Bar Theory**
Definition: A theory of phrase structure in which every phrase shares a common hierarchical structure: Specifier – Head – Complement, with the head determining the category (NP, VP, AP, PP, CP). Provides a universal template for phrase structure across languages.
Key relationships: NP (noun phrase), VP (verb phrase), AP (adjective phrase), PP (prepositional phrase), CP (complementizer phrase), DP (determiner phrase), head movement, specifier position.

**C06 — Generative Grammar and Universal Grammar**
Definition: Chomsky's hypothesis that humans possess an innate Universal Grammar (UG) — a set of formal principles and parameters shared by all human languages — that constrains the possible forms of human language and explains the poverty of the stimulus in language acquisition.
Key relationships: Principles and Parameters (GB theory), Minimalist Program, merge operation, language acquisition device (LAD), poverty of the stimulus argument, I-language vs. E-language.

**C07 — Semantics**
Definition: The study of meaning in language. Compositional semantics: the meaning of complex expressions derives systematically from the meanings of their parts (principle of compositionality, Frege). Formal semantics uses logical tools (model theory, possible worlds) to represent meaning.
Key relationships: Sense vs. reference (Frege), truth conditions, entailment, presupposition, implicature, lexical semantics (synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy, polysemy, homonymy).

**C08 — Pragmatics**
Definition: The study of how context affects language interpretation — what speakers mean beyond what words literally say. Grice's Cooperative Principle: speakers are expected to be truthful, informative, relevant, and clear. Violations of these maxims generate conversational implicatures.
Key relationships: Grice's maxims (quantity, quality, relation, manner), conversational implicature, speech act theory (Austin, Searle: locutionary, illocutionary, perlocutionary acts), deixis, presupposition, politeness theory (Brown & Levinson).

**C09 — Speech Act Theory**
Definition: Language is used to perform actions (Austin's How to Do Things with Words, 1962). Locutionary act: the utterance itself. Illocutionary act: the social action performed (promise, request, assertion, warning). Perlocutionary act: the effect on the hearer.
Key relationships: Indirect speech acts, performatives vs. constatives, felicity conditions (for a speech act to succeed), Austin, Searle.

**C10 — Language Typology**
Definition: The cross-linguistic study of structural variation and universals. Basic word orders (SOV most common ~45%, SVO ~42%, VSO ~9%); head-initial vs. head-final; nominative-accusative vs. ergative-absolutive case systems; analytic vs. synthetic vs. polysynthetic morphology.
Key relationships: Greenberg's universals (implicational: if X then Y), SOV, SVO, VSO order, case alignment, morphological typology, phonological universals (all languages have consonants and vowels).

**C11 — Language Families**
Definition: Groupings of languages descended from a common ancestor (proto-language). Major families: Indo-European (~3 billion speakers, 445 languages: Romance, Germanic, Slavic, Indo-Iranian, etc.), Sino-Tibetan (~1.5 billion), Afro-Asiatic (~400 million), Austronesian (~380 million, most geographically widespread), Niger-Congo (~400 million), Dravidian (~250 million), Japonic, Turkic.
Key relationships: Proto-Indo-European (PIE), comparative method, cognates, sound correspondences, Swadesh list (basic vocabulary), language isolates (Basque, Sumerian).

**C12 — Historical Linguistics and Sound Change**
Definition: Languages change systematically over time. Sound change is regular (Grimm's Law: PIE stops shifted systematically in Proto-Germanic). The comparative method reconstructs proto-languages from regular correspondences among descendant languages.
Key relationships: Grimm's Law, Verner's Law, the Neogrammarian Hypothesis (sound laws have no exceptions), internal reconstruction, proto-language reconstruction, lexical diffusion.

**C13 — Sociolinguistics**
Definition: The study of relationships between language and society: how social factors (class, gender, ethnicity, age, region) influence language variation and change. Labov's studies of New York City English demonstrated systematic social stratification of linguistic variables.
Key relationships: Dialect, sociolect, idiolect, register, code-switching, diglossia (Ferguson: two varieties used in different social contexts), language contact, pidgin, creole, prestige dialect.

**C14 — Language Change and Contact**
Definition: Languages change through internal mechanisms (analogy, reanalysis, phonological drift) and through contact with other languages (borrowing, code-switching, convergence). Language contact can produce pidgins (contact languages with reduced grammar) and creoles (fully developed first languages with pidgin origins).
Key relationships: Substratum influence, linguistic area (Sprachbund), calque (loan translation), loanword, Creole formation theories (universalist, substrate, superstrate), language shift vs. language maintenance.

**C15 — Language Endangerment and Documentation**
Definition: Approximately 7,000 languages are spoken today; ~40% are endangered (fewer than 1,000 speakers or no child speakers). Language documentation records endangered languages for preservation: audio/video recordings, grammatical descriptions, lexicons, text corpora.
Key relationships: Language vitality metrics (EGIDS scale), language revitalization, UNESCO Atlas of World Languages in Danger, language death, ethnologue, ELDP (Endangered Languages Documentation Programme).

**C16 — Writing Systems**
Definition: Visual representation of language. Types: logographic (Chinese characters — each character represents a morpheme), syllabic (Japanese kana, Cherokee syllabary), alphabetic (letters represent phonemes: Latin, Cyrillic, Arabic, Devanagari), abjad (consonant-only: Arabic, Hebrew), abugida (consonant + inherent vowel: Devanagari, Ethiopic).
Key relationships: Orthography, phonemic vs. morphophonemic spelling, directionality (LTR, RTL, boustrophedon), Unicode standard (covers 154 scripts), historical development of writing (Sumerian cuneiform ~3200 BCE, earliest writing).

**C17 — Language Acquisition (First Language)**
Definition: Children acquire their native language without explicit instruction, typically completing basic grammar by age 5. Milestones: babbling (~6 months), first words (~12 months), two-word stage (~18–24 months), multiword utterances (~24–36 months), complex grammar (3–5 years).
Key relationships: Critical period hypothesis (Lenneberg), language acquisition device (Chomsky), usage-based approach (Tomasello), Nicaraguan Sign Language (evidence for innate grammaticalization), input-output relationships.

**C18 — Second Language Acquisition (SLA)**
Definition: The process by which people learn additional languages after early childhood. Key factors: age (critical period: earlier acquisition → more native-like attainment), input quantity and quality, motivation, interaction, and individual differences.
Key relationships: Interlanguage (Selinker: systematic stages of L2 grammar), transfer (L1 influence on L2), fossilization, comprehensible input (Krashen's Input Hypothesis, i+1), interaction hypothesis (Long), output hypothesis (Swain).

**C19 — Computational Linguistics and NLP Fundamentals**
Definition: Computational modeling of human language for analysis and generation. Core tasks: tokenization, part-of-speech (POS) tagging, parsing, named entity recognition (NER), coreference resolution, sentiment analysis, machine translation, question answering, text summarization.
Key relationships: Rule-based vs. statistical vs. neural NLP approaches, pipeline architecture, end-to-end models, annotation and corpora (Penn Treebank, Universal Dependencies, OntoNotes).

**C20 — Language Models**
Definition: Probabilistic models that assign probabilities to sequences of words or tokens. N-gram models (traditional): P(w_n | w_1...w_{n-1}). Neural language models (LSTM, Transformer-based): learn dense representations. Large Language Models (LLMs, e.g., GPT-4, LLaMA): trained on web-scale text, capable of fluent text generation.
Key relationships: Perplexity (evaluation metric), tokenization (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece), attention mechanism, self-supervised pretraining, fine-tuning, hallucination, grounding.

**C21 — Transformer Architecture and Attention**
Definition: The Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) replaced recurrent networks for NLP tasks using self-attention: each token attends to all others simultaneously. BERT (bidirectional, masked LM pretraining) and GPT (autoregressive, causal LM) are two main architectural directions.
Key relationships: Scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention, positional encoding, encoder-decoder architecture, BERT (classification, NLU), GPT (generation), encoder-only vs. decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder.

**C22 — Machine Translation**
Definition: Automated translation between human languages. Evolution: rule-based MT (RBMT) → statistical MT (SMT, phrase-based) → neural MT (NMT, sequence-to-sequence with attention) → LLM-based translation. Google Translate and DeepL use NMT; ChatGPT-class models perform competitively on translation.
Key relationships: BLEU score (bilingual evaluation understudy — precision-based translation metric), post-editing, zero-shot translation, multilingual models (mBERT, mT5, NLLB), code-switching in translation.

**C23 — Discourse Analysis**
Definition: The study of language above the sentence level — how sentences connect to form coherent texts and conversations. Cohesion: formal connections (reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, lexical cohesion). Coherence: logical and thematic organization (rhetorical structure theory, RST).
Key relationships: Genre, register, topic and comment, given vs. new information, discourse markers ("however," "therefore," "in contrast"), conversational analysis (turn-taking, adjacency pairs).

**C24 — Phonological Processes**
Definition: Systematic modifications to sounds in context: assimilation (sounds become more alike: "impossible" from "in+possible"), dissimilation (sounds become less alike), deletion, insertion (epenthesis), metathesis (sounds swap), reduplication (common in Austronesian languages).
Key relationships: Sandhi (sound changes at word boundaries), vowel harmony (Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian: vowels in a word agree in backness/roundness), consonant mutation (Celtic languages), phonological rules.

**C25 — Deixis and Reference**
Definition: Expressions whose interpretation depends on context: person deixis (I, you, they), spatial deixis (here, there, this, that), temporal deixis (now, then, yesterday), discourse deixis (the following argument). Anaphora: expressions referring to prior discourse elements (pronouns, definite noun phrases).
Key relationships: Reference tracking, coreference, pronoun binding, indexicality (Peirce), Fregean sense vs. reference, presupposition of existence.

**C26 — Language and Thought (Linguistic Relativity)**
Definition: The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis proposes that language shapes or influences cognition and thought. Strong version (determinism: discredited); weak version (linguistic relativity): moderate effects found experimentally in color perception, spatial cognition, and number.
Key relationships: Whorfian effects (Boroditsky's work on time, color, and space), Pirahã language controversy, color terminology and categorical perception, grammatical gender and object conceptualization, numeral systems and quantity perception.

**C27 — Signed Languages**
Definition: Fully natural human languages in the visual-gestural modality, not derived from or a version of spoken language. ASL (American Sign Language), BSL, ISL are distinct languages with their own phonology (handshape, location, movement, orientation), morphology, and syntax. Demonstrate that language is modality-independent.
Key relationships: Iconicity vs. arbitrariness in sign, spatial grammar, classifier constructions, non-manual markers (facial grammar), Nicaraguan Sign Language (spontaneous language creation).

**C28 — Morphological Typology**
Definition: Languages classified by how they encode grammatical information: Isolating/Analytic (minimal morphology, each word = one morpheme: Mandarin, Vietnamese, English tends toward this); Agglutinative (morphemes stack with clear boundaries: Turkish, Finnish, Swahili); Fusional/Synthetic (single morpheme encodes multiple categories: Latin, Spanish); Polysynthetic (entire clause in one word: Inuktitut).
Key relationships: Synthesis index (morphemes per word), fusion index, word complexity, head-marking vs. dependent-marking.

**C29 — Information Structure**
Definition: How sentences package information for the hearer given what is known and new. Topic (what the sentence is about) vs. Comment (what is said about the topic). Focus (new or contrastive information) vs. Background. Different languages use word order, prosody, morphology, or particles to mark these distinctions.
Key relationships: Given vs. new information, topic-prominent languages (Mandarin, Japanese) vs. subject-prominent (English), focus-fronting, contrastive stress, question-answer congruence.

**C30 — Language Policy and Planning**
Definition: Governmental and institutional decisions about which languages are used in official domains (education, law, media, administration), how languages are standardized (corpus planning), and efforts to promote or protect languages (status planning). Affects language vitality and speaker rights.
Key relationships: Official language, national language, co-official status, medium of instruction policy, language revitalization (Welsh, Hawaiian, Māori, Hebrew), language rights, linguistic imperialism.

## 5. Patterns

**P01 — Phonemic Analysis**
Description: Identify the phoneme inventory of a language by collecting minimal pairs (pairs differing in one sound only, with different meanings), determining which sounds are in complementary distribution (= allophones), and establishing phonological rules for allophonic variation.
When to use: Language documentation, linguistic fieldwork, phonological typology.
Example: English /p/ and /pʰ/ (aspirated): in complementary distribution (aspirated word-initially, unaspirated after /s/); therefore allophones of one phoneme, not distinct phonemes.

**P02 — Syntactic Tree Construction (Phrase Structure)**
Description: Parse a sentence into immediate constituents (NP, VP, PP, etc.) using phrase structure rules; construct a labeled bracketed representation or tree diagram showing hierarchical constituent structure.
When to use: Grammatical analysis, NLP parsing, theoretical syntax.
Example: [S [NP The cat] [VP [V chased] [NP the mouse]]] — demonstrates subject-verb-object structure with NP and VP constituents.

**P03 — Comparative Method (Historical Reconstruction)**
Description: Compare cognate words (same origin) across related languages; establish systematic sound correspondences; reconstruct proto-language forms (with asterisk notation *); use evidence across branches to validate reconstruction.
When to use: Historical linguistics, language family research.
Example: English "father," German "Vater," Latin "pater," Sanskrit "pitṛ" all cognate; PIE root reconstructed as *ph₂tér-.

**P04 — Ethnographic Interview for Language Documentation**
Description: Elicit language data through structured interviews: word lists (Swadesh list), narrative elicitation (picture description, personal narratives), grammatical elicitation (translation tasks), and natural conversation recording; transcribe and analyze with community speakers.
When to use: Endangered language documentation fieldwork.
Example: ELDP documentary fieldwork protocol: record 40+ hours of naturalistic speech, elicit 1,000-item vocabulary, produce grammatical sketch and text corpus.

**P05 — Corpus Analysis**
Description: Use large, searchable text collections (corpora) to study language patterns: frequency analysis, collocations, concordances, n-gram analysis; compare varieties, registers, or time periods using corpus comparison tools.
When to use: Lexicographic research, language change study, NLP model training, discourse analysis.
Example: British National Corpus (BNC) + Google Ngram Viewer analysis of word frequency changes over time to track semantic change ("gay" meaning shift).

**P06 — Formal Semantic Analysis**
Description: Translate natural language sentences into logical form (predicate logic, λ-calculus, possible worlds semantics); verify entailment and contradiction relations; model context-dependent meaning (presuppositions, implicatures).
When to use: Formal semantics research; NLP semantic parsing; dialogue system development.
Example: "Every student passed" → ∀x(student(x) → passed(x)); this entails "some students passed" but not "the teacher passed."

**P07 — NLP Pipeline Construction**
Description: Build sequential text processing: (1) tokenization → (2) sentence splitting → (3) POS tagging → (4) lemmatization → (5) dependency parsing → (6) NER → (7) coreference resolution → (8) semantic role labeling, using pre-trained models (spaCy, Stanza, HuggingFace Transformers).
When to use: Text analytics applications, information extraction, question answering systems.
Example: spaCy pipeline processes "Barack Obama visited Berlin" → tokens, POS (PROPN, VERB, PROPN), NER (PERSON, GPE), dependency (nsubj, dobj).

**P08 — Implicature Cancellation Test**
Description: Test whether an inference is a conversational implicature (cancellable without contradiction) or an entailment/presupposition (not cancellable): add "...but not P" and evaluate acceptability.
When to use: Semantic/pragmatic boundary testing; lexical semantics research.
Example: "Some students passed, in fact all of them" (implicature "not all" is cancellable) vs. "#The king of France is bald, but France has no king" (presupposition failure, infelicitous).

**P09 — Sociolinguistic Survey Design**
Description: Design stratified sample of speakers across social variables (age, gender, class, ethnicity, geographic location); collect naturalistic speech through sociolinguistic interviews (vernacular elicitation via danger-of-death question); code linguistic variables; analyze using Goldvarb/Rbrul (variable rule analysis).
When to use: Dialect survey, language variation study, language change research.
Example: Labov's New York City department store study: rapid anonymous observation of /r/ pronunciation across socioeconomic strata in Saks, Macy's, and S. Klein stores.

**P10 — Language Revitalization Program Design**
Description: Assess language vitality (EGIDS scale); identify transmission breakdown points; design interventions (master-apprentice program, language nests for children, documentation, community classes, digital media in the language); evaluate impact on intergenerational transmission.
When to use: Endangered language communities; indigenous language policy.
Example: Welsh language revitalization: mandatory Welsh education, Welsh TV channel (S4C, 1982), Welsh Language Act (1993) — Welsh speakers increased from 19% (1991) to 29% (2011).

**P11 — Cross-Linguistic Typological Survey**
Description: Sample languages from diverse families and geographic areas using stratified sampling (WALS sample); code for presence/absence of structural features; test implicational universals (if X then Y); control for genealogical relatedness (Galton's problem).
When to use: Typological research; testing linguistic universals; language family studies.
Example: World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS, Dryer & Haspelmath): 2,662 languages coded on 192 features; major database for typological research.

**P12 — Error Analysis in SLA**
Description: Collect learner production data; identify systematic errors; classify as L1 transfer (interference), overgeneralization of L2 rule, or development-stage error; use error patterns to infer interlanguage grammar; target instruction at persistent systematic errors.
When to use: SLA research; language teaching materials development; learner assessment.
Example: Spanish learners of English omit "do" in questions (*"Where you went?") → L2 inversion rule not yet acquired; not L1 transfer (Spanish doesn't use "do").

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP01 — Conflating Prescriptive and Descriptive Grammar**
Why wrong: Linguistics is descriptive (documents how language is actually used) not prescriptive (how it "should" be used). Rules like "don't split infinitives" or "never end a sentence with a preposition" are style conventions, not grammatical facts. Non-standard dialects (AAVE, Scottish English) are not deficient — they are complete grammatical systems.
What to do instead: Describe language as it is used by actual speakers; distinguish descriptive norms from prescriptive conventions; recognize all dialects as rule-governed systems.

**AP02 — Treating Writing as Primary Language**
Why wrong: Writing is a secondary representation of spoken language, developed only ~5,200 years ago; all the world's languages existed long before writing. Many linguistic universals, language acquisition findings, and phonological generalizations apply to spoken (or signed) language — not writing.
What to do instead: Treat spoken language as primary; analyze writing systems as distinct from spoken language; acknowledge that ~3,500 of 7,000 languages have no writing system.

**AP03 — Equating Language Complexity**
Why wrong: All human languages are equally complex in aggregate — simplicity in one domain (morphology) is compensated by complexity in another (syntax or pragmatics). No language is "primitive," "simple," or "undeveloped."
What to do instead: Analyze complexity domain by domain; avoid value judgments about language complexity; recognize that complexity varies by subsystem and reflects communicative needs of the community.

**AP04 — Assuming Language Models Understand Language**
Why wrong: LLMs (GPT, etc.) are extremely powerful statistical models of token sequences; they generate fluent text by predicting likely continuations, not by semantic understanding, reference, or grounding in the world. They confabulate, hallucinate, and fail on reasoning that requires real-world grounding.
What to do instead: Use LLMs for fluency/form-related tasks; verify factual outputs; supplement with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) or structured knowledge bases for factual accuracy; never treat LLM output as equivalent to human understanding.

**AP05 — Using BLEU Score as the Only MT Evaluation**
Why wrong: BLEU measures n-gram overlap with reference translations; it correlates poorly with human judgment on many language pairs, especially morphologically rich languages; it penalizes valid paraphrases; modern systems score higher than humans on BLEU while having clear quality issues.
What to do instead: Combine BLEU with human evaluation (direct assessment), MQM error annotation, CometScore (neural reference-based), BLEURT, and fluency/adequacy ratings; use multiple metrics for a complete picture.

**AP06 — Ignoring Language Transfer in SLA Pedagogy**
Why wrong: Learners inevitably draw on their L1 knowledge; ignoring L1 in instruction fails to target predictable difficulty and misses opportunities for positive transfer (cognates, shared structures).
What to do instead: Use contrastive analysis to predict transfer errors; explicitly address L1-L2 differences; leverage positive transfer (shared cognates, similar structures); provide L1 scaffolding where appropriate.

**AP07 — Treating Linguistic Relativity as Linguistic Determinism**
Why wrong: The strong Whorfian hypothesis — that language determines thought, making certain concepts unthinkable in some languages — has been thoroughly empirically refuted. All languages can express any concept given sufficient context; speakers of all languages can perceive color, time, space equally.
What to do instead: Evaluate weak relativity claims empirically (specific linguistic features may have measurable effects on specific cognitive tasks); maintain the distinction between linguistic encoding habits and cognitive capacity.
