## 4. Core Concepts

**C01 — The Levels of War**
Definition: The hierarchical framework for understanding military operations. Strategic level: national objectives, resource allocation, political-military direction. Operational level: theater campaigns linking tactical actions to strategic objectives. Tactical level: engagements and battles. Each level has its own logic and planning processes.
Key relationships: Grand strategy (national security policy), military strategy, campaign planning, mission orders, operational design.

**C02 — Strategy**
Definition: The art of using military means to achieve political ends. Clausewitz: war is the continuation of politics by other means. Strategy aligns ends (objectives), ways (courses of action), and means (resources). Grand strategy encompasses all elements of national power: diplomatic, informational, military, economic (DIME).
Key relationships: Clausewitz (friction, fog of war, trinity), Sun Tzu (deception, intelligence), Jomini (interior lines, decisive point), political objective vs. military objective.

**C03 — Clausewitz's Theory of War**
Definition: Carl von Clausewitz (On War, 1832): war is a continuation of politics by other means; characterized by friction (unexpected difficulties), the fog of war (uncertainty), and the remarkable trinity (people's will, military forces, and government). Centers of gravity are sources of power that if neutralized can produce enemy collapse.
Key relationships: Center of gravity, critical vulnerability, culmination point, moral forces, absolute war vs. limited war, war as policy instrument.

**C04 — Sun Tzu's Strategic Principles**
Definition: Sun Tzu (The Art of War, ~500 BCE): supreme excellence is defeating the enemy without fighting; know yourself and your enemy; use deception and intelligence; attack weakness, avoid strength; win by strategy before combat. Emphasizes psychological and informational dimensions over attrition.
Key relationships: Shaping operations, deception (MILDEC), intelligence superiority, maneuver, strategic patience, asymmetric approach.

**C05 — Operational Art**
Definition: The use of military forces to achieve strategic objectives through campaigns and major operations. Operational art links tactics to strategy: the operational artist designs campaigns to create the conditions for decisive action. Key concepts: operational design, lines of effort, phase planning, culmination management.
Key relationships: Campaign design, lines of operation, phased operations, culminating point, operational reach, center of gravity at operational level.

**C06 — Maneuver Warfare**
Definition: A warfighting philosophy emphasizing rapid movement, surprise, disorientation, and exploitation of enemy vulnerabilities rather than attrition. Seeks to collapse enemy will and cohesion by striking at critical nodes and avoiding strengths. Associated with German Auftragstaktik (mission tactics) and John Boyd's OODA loop.
Key relationships: Blitzkrieg (WWII German operational method), OODA loop (Observe-Orient-Decide-Act, Boyd), Auftragstaktik, initiative, tempo, exploitation.

**C07 — Attrition Warfare**
Definition: A strategy of wearing down the enemy through sustained application of firepower and combat power, destroying or depleting personnel, equipment, and will. Contrasted with maneuver warfare. Characterized WWI Western Front, Soviet doctrine in WWII (especially early), and many grinding campaigns.
Key relationships: Attritional strategy (WWI), war of exhaustion, war of annihilation, mass, firepower, strategic bombing (strategic attrition of industry and will), cost-exchange ratios.

**C08 — Deterrence Theory**
Definition: Preventing an adversary from taking an undesired action by convincing them the costs will outweigh the benefits. Nuclear deterrence: second-strike capability (assured destruction) deters first strike. Conventional deterrence: demonstrated capability and will to defend.
Key relationships: Assured destruction, second-strike capability, MAD (Mutual Assured Destruction), extended deterrence (NATO Article 5), credibility, capability, resolve, ICBM triad, SLBM, strategic bomber.

**C09 — Nuclear Strategy**
Definition: The theory and practice of nuclear weapons employment or threatened employment. TRIAD: land-based ICBMs (prompt global strike, survivability through hardening), SLBMs (most survivable, assured second strike), strategic bombers (flexible, recallable). Arms control: SALT, START I/II/New START treaties limiting strategic warheads.
Key relationships: First strike vs. second strike, counterforce (targeting enemy nuclear forces) vs. countervalue (targeting cities), escalation control, No First Use (NFU) policy, proliferation, NPT (Non-Proliferation Treaty, 1968).

**C10 — Intelligence Cycle**
Definition: The systematic process of producing actionable intelligence: Planning and Direction (requirements) → Collection → Processing → Analysis and Production → Dissemination → Feedback. All-source intelligence integrates HUMINT, SIGINT, IMINT, MASINT, OSINT.
Key relationships: HUMINT (human intelligence), SIGINT (signals: COMINT + ELINT), IMINT (imagery), MASINT (measurement and signature), OSINT (open source), counterintelligence, intelligence community, finished intelligence products.

**C11 — HUMINT and Counterintelligence**
Definition: Human Intelligence (HUMINT): information gathered from human sources through clandestine officers (case officers), debriefing of travelers, and liaison with foreign services. Counterintelligence (CI): identifying, assessing, neutralizing, and exploiting foreign intelligence services' activities against one's own forces and secrets.
Key relationships: Asset handling, tradecraft, dead drop, cover story, double agent, mole, penetration, deception operation.

**C12 — Logistics and Sustainment**
Definition: The science of planning and executing the movement and maintenance of forces. "Amateurs talk strategy, professionals talk logistics" (attributed to various). Logistics comprises supply (Class I–IX), transportation, maintenance, medical, and infrastructure support. Logistical culmination is a greater cause of operational failure than tactical defeat.
Key relationships: Lines of communication (LOC), forward support battalion (FSB), supply chain, logistics reach, consumption rates, supply class system (I: food, III: fuel, V: ammunition, VIII: medical, IX: repair parts).

**C13 — Joint Operations**
Definition: Military operations conducted by forces from two or more military departments (Army, Navy, Air Force, Marine Corps, Space Force, Coast Guard) under a single joint force commander. Jointness requires common doctrine, interoperability, and unified command.
Key relationships: Joint Force Commander (JFC), CCMD (Combatant Commands), JOPP (Joint Operational Planning Process), effects-based operations, joint fires, joint logistics, C4ISR.

**C14 — Combined Arms Operations**
Definition: The synchronized employment of multiple arms and branches (infantry, armor, artillery, aviation, engineers, air defense) to exploit each other's capabilities and neutralize each other's weaknesses. Successful combined arms overwhelms enemy decision-making by presenting multiple simultaneous threats.
Key relationships: Infantry-armor teamwork, fire and movement, covering fire, close air support (CAS), artillery-maneuver synchronization, engineer obstacle breaching.

**C15 — Command and Control (C2)**
Definition: The exercise of authority and direction by a commander over assigned forces. Modern C2 systems (C4ISR: Command, Control, Communications, Computers, Intelligence, Surveillance, Reconnaissance) enable real-time situational awareness and precision strike. Mission command (Auftragstaktik): subordinate leaders act within commander's intent without detailed orders.
Key relationships: Commander's Intent, battle rhythm, OPORD (Operations Order), OODA loop, network-centric warfare, command post, decision cycles.

**C16 — Military History — Ancient Warfare**
Definition: Ancient military history encompasses the emergence of organized warfare with the rise of states (~3000 BCE), development of standing armies, siege warfare, naval combat, and military theory. Key conflicts and innovations: Egyptian empire, Persian-Greek wars (Marathon 490 BCE, Thermopylae/Salamis 480 BCE), Macedonian phalanx (Alexander the Great), Roman legion system.
Key relationships: Phalanx, legion (manipular and cohort system), combined arms (cavalry + infantry + peltasts), logistics of ancient armies, siege warfare (catapults, battering rams, siege towers).

**C17 — Military History — World War I**
Definition: WWI (1914–1918): characterized by trench warfare on the Western Front, massive artillery bombardments, early use of tanks (Cambrai, 1917), poison gas (Ypres, 1915), airpower, and submarine warfare. Industrial-scale attrition produced ~20 million deaths. Strategic stalemate broken by combined arms tactics (storm troopers) and Allied counteroffensive (1918).
Key relationships: Schlieffen Plan, trench warfare, Verdun (1916, ~700,000 casualties), Somme (1916, ~1.2 million casualties), Gallipoli, Jutland naval battle, unrestricted submarine warfare, Bruchmüller artillery methods.

**C18 — Military History — World War II**
Definition: WWII (1939–1945): global conflict engaging all major powers. Key operational innovations: German Blitzkrieg (combined arms, radio, initiative), Soviet deep battle doctrine, Allied amphibious warfare (D-Day, Pacific island-hopping), strategic bombing campaigns, nuclear weapons. ~70–85 million deaths total.
Key relationships: Operation Barbarossa, Stalingrad, El Alamein, D-Day (Normandy, June 6, 1944), Pacific theater (Midway — decisive naval air battle), Operation Overlord, Manhattan Project, Hiroshima/Nagasaki.

**C19 — Counterinsurgency (COIN)**
Definition: Military and civilian efforts to defeat an insurgency — an organized armed struggle by a non-state group to overthrow a government or occupy territory. COIN requires: security, governance, and economic development simultaneously. Population-centric COIN (FM 3-24/MCWP 3-33.5) emphasizes winning population support over body count.
Key relationships: Mao's three-phase insurgency (guerrilla to mobile to conventional war), Galula COIN theory, population-centric vs. enemy-centric approach, clear-hold-build, Afghanistan/Iraq COIN experience.

**C20 — Irregular Warfare**
Definition: Violent struggle among state and non-state actors for legitimacy and influence over relevant populations. Encompasses guerrilla warfare, terrorism, insurgency, counterinsurgency, foreign internal defense (FID), unconventional warfare (UW), information warfare, and civil affairs.
Key relationships: Asymmetric warfare, terrorism (political violence against civilians to coerce), special operations forces (SOF), proxy war, gray zone operations.

**C21 — Special Operations Forces (SOF)**
Definition: Military forces trained for unconventional, clandestine, or politically sensitive operations: direct action (raids), special reconnaissance, counterterrorism, unconventional warfare (working with indigenous forces), PSYOP, civil affairs. Examples: US Army Special Forces (Green Berets), Delta Force, Navy SEALs, UK SAS/SBS.
Key relationships: Direct action vs. indirect approach, foreign internal defense (FID), counterterrorism (CT), strategic reconnaissance, E&E (escape and evasion), JSOC (Joint Special Operations Command).

**C22 — Airpower Theory**
Definition: The theory governing the strategic employment of air forces. Douhet (1921): airpower could win wars independently by destroying enemy cities and will. Mitchell: strategic bombardment could defeat enemy industry and morale. Warden's Five Rings model (1988): attack leadership, system essentials, infrastructure, population, and fielded military forces — in that order.
Key relationships: Strategic bombing, interdiction, close air support (CAS), air superiority, Douhet, Mitchell, Warden's Five Rings, precision guided munitions (PGMs), effects-based operations.

**C23 — Naval Strategy**
Definition: The theory and practice of naval power. Mahan (1890): sea power — including merchant shipping, navy, and naval bases — determines national greatness; command of the sea enables commerce and power projection. Maritime strategy encompasses sea control, sea denial, power projection, and maritime interdiction.
Key relationships: Mahanian sea power theory, command of the sea, sea control vs. sea denial, SLOC (sea lines of communication), aircraft carrier battle group, submarine warfare (ASW, SSN, SSBN), amphibious operations.

**C24 — Law of Armed Conflict (LOAC) / IHL**
Definition: The body of international law governing the conduct of armed conflict. Rooted in the Hague Conventions (1899, 1907) and Geneva Conventions (1949, Additional Protocols 1977). Key principles: distinction (combatants vs. civilians), proportionality (civilian harm not excessive to military advantage), military necessity, and humanity.
Key relationships: Geneva Conventions, POW status, protected persons, war crimes (ICC jurisdiction), perfidy, unnecessary suffering prohibition, non-international armed conflict (NIAC), Additional Protocol I/II.

**C25 — Cyber Warfare**
Definition: Military operations in cyberspace: offensive (disrupting/destroying enemy C4ISR, infrastructure, or weapons systems) and defensive (protecting own networks and systems). Stuxnet (2010) — first confirmed cyber weapon destroying physical infrastructure — marks the beginning of sophisticated state cyber operations.
Key relationships: Cyberspace operations (OCO, DCO, DODIN), CNE (computer network exploitation), CNA (computer network attack), APT (Advanced Persistent Threat), zero-day exploits, cyber deterrence, USCYBERCOM.

**C26 — Anti-Access/Area Denial (A2/AD)**
Definition: Strategies by which a state prevents or degrades enemy access to contested areas using long-range precision fires (missiles), sea mines, air defense systems, and electronic warfare. A2: preventing entry into a region; AD: preventing freedom of action within a region. China's A2/AD challenges US power projection in the Pacific.
Key relationships: ASBM (Anti-Ship Ballistic Missiles), IADS (Integrated Air Defense System), DF-21D, S-400, sea mines, AUV, electronic warfare (EW), distributed maritime operations (US counter-strategy).

**C27 — Hybrid Warfare**
Definition: The use of a combination of conventional forces, irregular tactics, information operations, cyber attacks, economic coercion, and political subversion below the threshold of formal war. Russia's Gerasimov Doctrine (debated) and operations in Ukraine and Georgia exemplify hybrid approaches.
Key relationships: Gray zone operations, information operations, disinformation, proxy forces, little green men (Russia-Crimea 2014), lawfare, economic warfare, resilience.

**C28 — Space as a Military Domain**
Definition: Space is the fifth military domain (alongside land, sea, air, cyberspace). Satellites provide critical military capabilities: GPS (navigation, timing, targeting), satellite communications (SATCOM), intelligence gathering (IMINT, SIGINT), missile warning. Counter-space capabilities: ASAT (anti-satellite weapons), jamming, dazzling, cyber attacks on satellites.
Key relationships: Space Control, ISR satellites, GPS spoofing and jamming, China's ASAT test (2007, debris cloud), US Space Force (2019), Starlink military use (Ukraine), Space Situational Awareness (SSA).

**C29 — Military Decision-Making Process (MDMP)**
Definition: The US Army's structured process for planning military operations: Receipt of Mission → Mission Analysis → Course of Action (COA) Development → COA Analysis (War Game) → COA Comparison → COA Approval → Orders Production. Produces the Operations Order (OPORD).
Key relationships: Commander's intent, mission statement, METT-TC (Mission, Enemy, Terrain/Weather, Troops, Time, Civil Considerations), warning order, fragmentary order (FRAGO), parallel planning.

**C30 — Civil-Military Relations**
Definition: The relationship between military institutions and civilian political authority. Huntington's "objective civilian control" (military professionalism, apolitical): armed forces carry out civilian orders within their sphere of competence. Feaver's "principal-agent" model: civilians monitor military agents to ensure compliance. Coup-proofing: deliberate measures by authoritarian leaders to prevent military takeover.
Key relationships: Samuel Huntington, Goldwater-Nichols Act (1986), SECARDEF authority, chain of command, military professionalism, democratic civil-military relations.

## 5. Patterns

**P01 — Mission Analysis (MDMP Step 2)**
Description: Analyze the higher commander's order and intent; conduct IPB (Intelligence Preparation of the Battlefield — terrain, weather, enemy, civil analysis); identify specified/implied/essential tasks; formulate restated mission; determine decision points and time available.
When to use: All military planning processes; the foundation of sound operational design.
Example: Given an order to "seize the bridge at grid ####," mission analysis identifies: specified task (seize), purpose (enable follow-on forces), constraints (rules of engagement), implied tasks (clear enemy on approaches, establish security), and risks (enemy reinforcement).

**P02 — Intelligence Preparation of the Battlefield (IPB)**
Description: Continuous, four-step process: (1) Define the operational environment; (2) Describe environmental effects (terrain, weather, civil); (3) Evaluate the threat (enemy capabilities, doctrine, courses of action); (4) Determine threat courses of action (Most Likely and Most Dangerous).
When to use: All military planning; intelligence community analysis; targeting cycle.
Example: IPB for river crossing operation: identifies crossable points, fordable locations, enemy observation positions, likely defensive positions, and two enemy COAs (defend in place vs. delay).

**P03 — OODA Loop Application**
Description: Boyd's Observe-Orient-Decide-Act cycle: gather information → analyze and contextualize → decide on action → act. The strategically decisive insight: getting inside the enemy's OODA loop (cycling faster than they can react) paralyzes enemy decision-making.
When to use: Tactical and operational tempo management; electronic warfare targeting; strategic planning.
Example: German Blitzkrieg operations succeeded by cycling OODA faster than French command could respond: German penetrations appeared faster than French commanders could reorient and issue counterorders.

**P04 — Targeting Cycle (F3EAD)**
Description: Find-Fix-Finish-Exploit-Analyze-Disseminate: Find target (intelligence); Fix target location precisely; Finish target (kinetic/non-kinetic action); Exploit the site (collect intelligence from the scene); Analyze intelligence gathered; Disseminate to drive next targeting cycle.
When to use: Direct action operations; high-value target (HVT) pursuit; CT operations.
Example: Operation Neptune Spear (bin Laden, 2011): Find (years of intelligence); Fix (Abbottabad compound identified); Finish (SEAL Team 6 assault); Exploit (extensive collection of hard drives, documents); Analyze (yielded intelligence for 30+ subsequent operations).

**P05 — Logistics Estimation**
Description: Calculate daily consumption rates by unit type (Classes I, III, V primarily); multiply by operation duration and force size; add 20–30% planning factor for friction/uncertainty; plan transportation assets to move supplies at required rate; identify logistics culminating point.
When to use: Campaign planning; operational reach assessment; force sizing.
Example: A division conducting intensive operations consumes ~1,400 tons/day (food, fuel, ammunition); a 300-km supply line requires continuous truck convoy operations; logistics determines the tempo of advance.

**P06 — Defensive Planning (Area Defense)**
Description: Assign battle positions providing mutual support and interlocking fires; identify and cover likely avenues of approach; plan engagement areas (EAs) with obstacle systems channeling enemy into kill zones; position reserves for counterattack; integrate indirect fires throughout.
When to use: Tactical defensive operations; hasty defense establishment; prepared defensive positions.
Example: US Army doctrinal area defense: battalion defensive sector with three rifle companies in main battle area, one company in reserve, artillery fire plan coordinated with obstacle plan, air defense integrated.

**P07 — Staff Estimate Process**
Description: Each staff section (S2 intelligence, S3 operations, S4 logistics, S5 civil affairs, S6 communications) produces a functional estimate analyzing the situation from their perspective; estimates are synthesized in the commander's estimate to develop and select courses of action.
When to use: Course of action analysis (MDMP Step 4); operational planning.
Example: S4 logistics estimate identifies that COA 1 (requiring 800 tons/day supply) exceeds current logistics capability (600 tons/day); recommends building logistics nodes before execution or selecting COA 2 (300 tons/day).

**P08 — Counterinsurgency Population Analysis (COIN)**
Description: Map population distribution, ethnicity/tribe, social networks, economic drivers, grievances, and loyalties; identify population centers of gravity (key influencers, essential services); design operations to protect, not just control, population; measure effectiveness by population confidence, violence levels, essential services delivery.
When to use: COIN campaign planning; stability operations; foreign internal defense.
Example: Anbar Awakening (2006–2007): US commanders engaged Sunni tribal leaders directly, addressing their economic and security grievances; shifted tribal allegiance from AQI to US-backed security forces, dramatically reducing violence.

**P09 — Intelligence Cycle Execution**
Description: Establish Priority Intelligence Requirements (PIRs) driving collection; assign collection assets by type (HUMINT, SIGINT, IMINT, OSINT); establish tasking, collection, processing, exploitation, and dissemination (TCPED) pipeline; ensure analysis reaches decision-makers in time to act.
When to use: All military operations; crisis response; contingency planning.
Example: Before an airborne operation, PIRs focus on: enemy air defense positions (IMINT/SIGINT), enemy command nodes (HUMINT/SIGINT), weather at H-hour (meteorological), and alternate drop zones.

**P10 — Escalation Management**
Description: Identify the escalation ladder (rungs from conventional skirmish to nuclear exchange); establish thresholds for each escalation level; design operations to achieve objectives below acceptable risk thresholds; plan off-ramps (de-escalation options) at each level.
When to use: Crisis management; nuclear planning; confrontation with near-peer adversary.
Example: Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): Kennedy administration managed escalation by choosing naval quarantine (blockade) over airstrike — allowing Soviet face-saving exit; Khrushchev could withdraw missiles without triggering US invasion of Cuba.

**P11 — Red Team Analysis**
Description: Dedicated team assumes adversary perspective (role, culture, decision calculus) to challenge friendly plans, assumptions, and estimates; produces adversary COA assessments, wargames friendly COAs from adversary perspective, and identifies vulnerabilities in friendly plans.
When to use: COA analysis wargaming; plan stress-testing; strategic assessments.
Example: Red team analyzing invasion plan from defender's perspective identifies that the "decisive" avenue of approach is predictable and easily defended; recommends deception operation and alternate main effort.

**P12 — Information Operations (IO) Planning**
Description: Synchronize military information support operations (MISO), military deception (MILDEC), electronic warfare (EW), cyber operations, and operations security (OPSEC) to influence adversary decision-making and protect friendly information while degrading adversary information systems.
When to use: All operations requiring shaping of information environment; COIN and irregular warfare especially.
Example: D-Day (Operation Overlord) preceded by Operation Bodyguard: deception campaign (fictitious FUSAG army group, double agents, false radio traffic) convincing Germans the main landing would be at Pas-de-Calais, not Normandy.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP01 — Conflating Strategy with Tactics**
Why wrong: Tactical success does not automatically produce strategic victory. Vietnam: US military consistently won tactical engagements while losing the strategic war. Tactical superiority without strategic clarity produces military victory and political defeat.
What to do instead: Ensure clear connection between tactical actions, operational objectives, and strategic ends; ask "what political objective does this tactical action serve?"; align military operations with political strategy.

**AP02 — Ignoring Logistics in Operational Planning**
Why wrong: Military history is filled with operations defeated by logistics: Napoleon in Russia (supply lines too long), Rommel in North Africa (fuel shortages), Market Garden (bridges one bridge too far from supply lines). "An army travels on its stomach" (attributed to Napoleon).
What to do instead: Conduct logistics estimation early in planning; identify culminating point before it becomes a crisis; plan logistics nodes, transportation assets, and consumption rates as a planning constraint, not an afterthought.

**AP03 — Underestimating Moral Forces**
Why wrong: Clausewitz emphasized that moral forces (will, cohesion, leadership, morale) are often more decisive than physical forces. Technologically superior armies have repeatedly lost to less-equipped opponents with higher motivation and better strategy (US in Vietnam, Soviet Union in Afghanistan).
What to do instead: Assess adversary will and cohesion as carefully as their material capabilities; consider population support for adversary; assess own force morale and political will; design operations to erode adversary will, not just destroy equipment.

**AP04 — Mirror Imaging in Intelligence Analysis**
Why wrong: Assuming adversaries think, plan, and value what we would value in their position produces systematically wrong predictions. US intelligence consistently underestimated adversary resolve (Korea, Vietnam, Iraq) by assuming adversary leaders would respond "rationally" in Western terms.
What to do instead: Red team using adversary's own doctrine, culture, decision-making frameworks, and leadership psychology; avoid projecting own values onto adversary decision-making; consult cultural advisors and regional experts.

**AP05 — Seeking Decisive Battle as the Universal Solution**
Why wrong: The Western military tradition obsesses with the decisive engagement destroying the enemy army. Many conflicts, however — insurgencies, protracted wars, gray zone competition — are not won by decisive battle. Attrition of an adversary's legitimacy, support base, or will may be more decisive than battlefield victory.
What to do instead: Match strategy to the nature of the conflict; assess whether victory can be achieved through battle, or whether it requires political, economic, or legitimacy-focused approaches; avoid the "ready for battle" planning assumption when the enemy won't stand and fight.

**AP06 — Ignoring Law of Armed Conflict (LOAC) as a Constraint**
Why wrong: Violations of LOAC (laws of war) destroy strategic legitimacy, alienate local populations, provide adversary propaganda victories, enable war crimes prosecutions, and undermine domestic political support for military operations. Abu Ghraib prison abuse significantly damaged US counterinsurgency efforts in Iraq.
What to do instead: Integrate LOAC compliance into planning, rules of engagement (ROE), training, and battle damage assessment; treat LOAC not as an obstacle but as a strategic asset — legitimacy is a center of gravity.

**AP07 — Preparing to Fight the Last War**
Why wrong: Military organizations naturally optimize for the threat they just faced. WWI armies entered WWII structured for trench warfare; Cold War militaries entered post-Cold War conflicts optimized for peer-vs-peer conventional warfare. The nature of the next conflict is rarely identical to the last.
What to do instead: Continuously assess the evolving threat environment; conduct joint and red team wargames against anticipated future adversaries (not current); invest in flexibility and adaptability; study emerging technologies and doctrinal changes in potential adversaries.
