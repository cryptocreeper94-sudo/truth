## 4. Core Concepts

**CC-001 — Attention**
The selective allocation of cognitive resources to a subset of available information. Selective attention: filtering relevant from irrelevant stimuli. Divided attention: processing multiple streams simultaneously (limited). Sustained attention (vigilance): maintaining focus over extended periods (degrades after 20–30 minutes). Attentional bottleneck: the single channel in information processing where serial processing limits throughput. The cocktail party effect: detecting one's own name in a competing channel despite attending elsewhere (Cherry, 1953).

**CC-002 — Inattentional Blindness**
The failure to notice an unexpected but salient object or event when attention is directed elsewhere. The gorilla experiment (Simons & Chabris, 1999): approximately 50% of observers watching a basketball video while counting passes failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. Change blindness: failure to detect changes across scenes or visual interruptions. Both phenomena demonstrate that perception is active and selective, not a passive recording of visual reality.

**CC-003 — Working Memory**
A limited-capacity system for temporary active maintenance and manipulation of information. Baddeley's multicomponent model: Central Executive (attentional control) + Phonological Loop (verbal/acoustic, 2 seconds duration) + Visuospatial Sketchpad (visual information) + Episodic Buffer (integrates information from other components with long-term memory). Working memory capacity (WMC): approximately 4 ± 1 chunks in the focus of attention (Cowan's core estimate, vs. Miller's original 7 ± 2 which conflated rehearsal). WMC strongly predicts fluid intelligence and academic achievement.

**CC-004 — Long-Term Memory Architecture**
Explicit (declarative): Episodic (personal events, context-specific) and Semantic (general knowledge, world facts, context-free). Implicit (non-declarative): Procedural/skills (motor sequences, cognitive procedures), Priming (prior exposure facilitates subsequent processing), Classical conditioning (fear associations), and Habituation. Long-term memory has no known capacity limit; forgetting reflects retrieval failure, interference, or reconsolidation modification rather than storage loss.

**CC-005 — The Encoding Specificity Principle**
Memory retrieval is most effective when the conditions at retrieval match the conditions at encoding (Tulving & Thomson, 1973). Context-dependent memory: information learned in context A is better recalled in context A than in context B. State-dependent memory: information encoded in a particular physiological or emotional state is better recalled in the same state. Transfer-appropriate processing: the test that best matches encoding conditions produces the best performance.

**CC-006 — Forgetting: Ebbinghaus and Interference**
Ebbinghaus (1885): the forgetting curve describes exponential decay of retention over time, with most forgetting occurring in the first hours/days; spaced repetition exploits this curve to optimize long-term retention. Proactive interference (PI): prior learning interferes with new learning (old language habits interfere with new language acquisition). Retroactive interference (RI): new learning interferes with old memories. Tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon: a feeling of imminent retrieval that is temporarily inaccessible — evidence that memory is not all-or-none.

**CC-007 — Schema Theory and Top-Down Processing**
Schemas are organized knowledge structures representing general knowledge about objects, events, roles, and situations. They guide perception, comprehension, and memory by providing top-down expectations that fill gaps and resolve ambiguity. Schemas speed processing but introduce systematic distortions: schema-inconsistent information is either ignored or reinterpreted to fit the schema; schema-consistent information is more likely to be falsely remembered. Bartlett's "War of the Ghosts" study (1932) demonstrated that recall is reconstructive, not reproductive.

**CC-008 — Dual Process Theory (System 1 / System 2)**
Kahneman's (2011) influential framework: System 1 (Type 1 processes): fast, automatic, intuitive, low-effort, parallel, emotion-associated, heuristic-driven; System 2 (Type 2 processes): slow, deliberate, rule-following, high-effort, serial, logic-associated. Most everyday decisions are System 1; System 2 is recruited for novel, complex, or high-stakes decisions. System 1 is more error-prone in systematic ways (cognitive biases); System 2 can override System 1 but is limited by cognitive capacity and motivation.

**CC-009 — Heuristics and Their Dual Role**
Heuristics are mental shortcuts reducing cognitive effort. They are often adaptive (accurate in typical environments) but systematically produce errors in atypical conditions. Availability heuristic: judging frequency/probability by ease of retrieval (plane crashes judged more probable after media coverage despite base rates). Representativeness heuristic: judging probability by resemblance to a prototype (base rate neglect, conjunction fallacy). Anchoring and adjustment: initial value (anchor) disproportionately influences final judgments even when the anchor is arbitrary.

**CC-010 — Prospect Theory's Judgment Mechanisms**
Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory (1979) describes three key psychological mechanisms: (1) Reference-point dependency: outcomes evaluated as gains or losses relative to a reference point, not as absolute levels of wealth; (2) Diminishing sensitivity: both gains and losses show diminishing marginal impact (value function concave for gains, convex for losses); (3) Loss aversion: losses are weighted approximately 2–2.5× more than equivalent gains. These mechanisms collectively explain the endowment effect, status quo bias, and framing effects.

**CC-011 — Framing Effects**
Logically equivalent problems produce different choices depending on how they are presented. Tversky and Kahneman's Asian Disease Problem (1981): "200 people saved" (gain frame) vs. "400 people die" (loss frame) — identical outcomes but different majority preferences (risk-averse in gain frame, risk-seeking in loss frame). Medical decision-making: patients prefer treatments described as "90% survival rate" vs. "10% mortality rate" — logically equivalent but psychologically different.

**CC-012 — Overconfidence**
People systematically overestimate the accuracy of their judgments and knowledge (calibration overconfidence), the probability of their predictions being correct, and their ability to perform tasks (planning fallacy). The 90% confidence interval task: asked to estimate ranges for factual questions such that there is a 90% chance the true value falls within the range, most people achieve only ~50–60% hit rates. Expert calibration is often better within their domain of expertise but deteriorates outside it.

**CC-013 — Anchoring and Adjustment**
An initial value (anchor) disproportionately influences subsequent judgments. Adjustment from the anchor is typically insufficient (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Critical feature: the anchor biases judgment even when it is demonstrably arbitrary (random number spinning wheels, irrelevant starting values). Applications: initial salary offers anchor salary negotiations; first asking prices anchor real estate transactions; initial diagnoses anchor subsequent medical evaluations.

**CC-014 — Cognitive Load Theory**
Cognitive load (Sweller, 1988): the total working memory demand placed on a learner. Intrinsic load: determined by element interactivity of the material (complex material = high intrinsic load). Extraneous load: caused by poor instructional design (redundant text and narration, split attention between diagrams and text). Germane load: processing devoted to schema formation. Effective instruction minimizes extraneous load, manages intrinsic load through sequencing, and optimizes germane load for learning. Working memory limitation is the primary constraint on learning rate.

**CC-015 — Expertise and Chunking**
Expert performance relies on the ability to recognize meaningful patterns in domain-relevant information (Chase & Simon, 1973 chess experiments). Chunks: meaningful units of information stored as single units in long-term memory; experts have approximately 50,000–100,000 chunks in their domain (Simon's estimate for chess grandmasters). Expert chess masters reconstruct a mid-game board from memory after brief exposure; they show no advantage on random piece configurations — expertise is pattern-based, not a general memory advantage.

**CC-016 — Mental Models**
Internal representations of how external systems work — causal, functional, mechanical models constructed from prior experience, reasoning, and instruction. Mental models allow people to simulate system behavior, troubleshoot problems, and make predictions. People's mental models of physical systems (electricity, probability, ecological dynamics) are often incorrect in predictable ways: electricity is often conceptualized as "water flowing through pipes," which misrepresents voltage/current relationships.

**CC-017 — Reasoning: Deductive, Inductive, Abductive**
Deductive reasoning: conclusions follow necessarily from premises (valid if structure preserves truth). Humans perform poorly on abstract deductive tasks (Wason selection task: ~10% correct) but much better on pragmatic, socially-embedded versions of the same logical structure. Inductive reasoning: generalizing from instances to general rules; susceptible to confirmation bias. Abductive reasoning (inference to the best explanation): the form of reasoning used in diagnosis, troubleshooting, and scientific theory selection — psychologically natural but epistemically defeasible.

**CC-018 — Confirmation Bias**
The tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information in a way that confirms pre-existing beliefs or hypotheses, and to underweight or avoid contradictory information. One of the most robust and consequential cognitive biases. In medicine: physicians anchoring on an initial diagnosis selectively seek confirming evidence (diagnostic momentum). In science: researchers design studies favorable to their hypotheses, over-interpret null results, and resist falsifying evidence. Disconfirmation bias: opposing bias — applying heightened skepticism to disconfirming evidence.

**CC-019 — Decision Fatigue**
Decision quality degrades following a long sequence of decisions. An Israeli study of parole board decisions (Danziger et al., 2011) found favorable ruling probability dropped from approximately 65% at the start of a session to nearly 0% just before a break, then recovered after breaks — apparently reflecting depletion of cognitive resources rather than case characteristics. Medical decisions, dietary choices, and judicial rulings show consistent quality degradation after extended decision-making periods.

**CC-020 — Motivated Reasoning**
The tendency to reason toward a desired conclusion rather than toward the truth. People evaluate evidence more critically when it challenges beliefs they are motivated to protect (disconfirmation) and less critically when it supports them. Kunda (1990): motivation directs cognition, but reasoning must pass a minimal plausibility threshold — people cannot believe anything, but they can find justification for preferred conclusions within wide limits. Motivated reasoning is distinct from simple bias — it recruits deliberate reasoning in the service of motivated goals.

**CC-021 — The Wason Selection Task**
Wason (1966): participants shown 4 cards (E, K, 4, 7) asked which to flip to test "If a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other." Most choose E and 4; correct answer is E and 7 (modus tollens). Only ~10% solve the abstract version; performance rises dramatically to ~75% on identical logic in a social contract version (checking compliance with a rule like "If a person drinks beer, they must be over 21"). Demonstrates reasoning is domain-specific and context-sensitive, not purely abstract.

**CC-022 — Satisficing vs. Maximizing**
Simon's (1956) bounded rationality: human decision-makers are not utility maximizers (as assumed in classical economics) but satisficers — they search for the first option that meets a satisfactory threshold rather than exhaustively comparing all options. Satisficing is adaptive given cognitive limitations and information costs. Schwartz's "Paradox of Choice": maximizers (who attempt to identify the best option) experience more regret, less satisfaction, and lower well-being than satisficers, despite often making objectively better choices.

**CC-023 — Sunk Cost Fallacy**
Continuing to invest in a failing course of action because of prior irrecoverable investment, even when future expected value is negative. Concorde fallacy: continued development of the Concorde despite mounting cost overruns because billions were already invested. Cognitive mechanism: loss aversion — abandoning the project means acknowledging losses; continuing avoids that psychological cost. Rational decision-making requires considering only future costs and benefits; sunk costs should be ignored.

**CC-024 — Base Rate Neglect**
The tendency to underweight statistical base rate information in favor of specific case information when making probabilistic judgments. Tversky and Kahneman's taxicab problem: 85% green cabs, 15% blue; witness identifies blue cab with 80% accuracy; most people give the posterior probability as ~80% (ignoring base rates) when the correct Bayesian answer is ~41%. Medical diagnosis: failing to weight the prior probability of disease (base rate) when interpreting test results produces systematic overdiagnosis of rare conditions and underdiagnosis of common ones.

**CC-025 — Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) Model**
Klein's naturalistic decision-making model (1989): expert decision-makers in high-stakes, time-pressured environments (firefighters, ICU nurses, military commanders) rarely generate and compare options; they recognize the situation as a familiar type, mentally simulate the first option that comes to mind, and implement it if it seems satisfactory, or cycle to the next recognized type. RPD explains expert decision speed and quality in dynamic environments where classical decision-analytic approaches are impractical.

**CC-026 — Emotional Influences on Cognition**
Affect infusion model (Forgas, 1995): affective states influence cognitive processes — mood-congruent recall (happy moods → recall of positive memories), risk perception elevation during fear or anxiety, and moral judgment modulation by disgust (Haidt's moral dumbfounding). Incidental affect: emotions triggered by factors irrelevant to a decision (sunny weather, background music) influence subsequent unrelated judgments. The James-Lange theory (emotion follows physiological response) and Cannon-Bard (simultaneous cognition and physiology) have both been modified by appraisal theories.

**CC-027 — Flow and Optimal Cognitive Performance**
Csikszentmihalyi's flow state (1990): a state of intense, effortless concentration in which self-consciousness recedes and time perception distorts. Conditions: clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge-skill balance. Neurologically: transient hypofrontality (reduced PFC activity), increased alpha/theta EEG activity, and dopaminergic/norepinephrinergic reward. Cognitive performance during flow combines high accuracy, speed, and creativity; it is the phenomenological counterpart of expert automaticity.

**CC-028 — Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT)**
Frederick (2005): a 3-item test requiring suppression of an immediate System 1 response in favor of correct reflective System 2 analysis. Classic item: "A bat and a ball together cost $1.10. The bat costs $1 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?" Intuitive answer: $0.10; correct answer: $0.05. CRT scores predict performance on judgment and decision-making tasks, probabilistic reasoning, economic rationality, and resistance to cognitive biases. CRT scores are correlated with but distinct from intelligence (g).

**CC-029 — Nudge Architecture and Default Effects**
Thaler and Sunstein (2008): changing the default option dramatically changes behavior without restricting freedom of choice. Organ donation opt-out countries have ~90% donation registration vs. ~10–20% in opt-in countries — identical infrastructure, different defaults. 401(k) automatic enrollment: participation rates jump from ~50% to ~90% with no change in contribution rate or plan features. Cafeteria arrangement: placing healthier food at eye level and front-of-counter position increases healthy food selection by 25–30%. Defaults work because of status quo bias, inertia, and implicit endorsement interpretation.

**CC-030 — Metacognition**
Thinking about one's own thinking — monitoring and controlling cognitive processes. Metacognitive accuracy (calibration): the degree to which confidence in one's performance matches actual performance. Feeling-of-knowing (FOK) judgments: predicting memory before attempting retrieval. Judgment of learning (JOL): assessing learning during or after study. Metacognitive illusions: the fluency effect (processing fluency creates an illusion of knowing — smooth reading feels like understanding), the illusion of explanatory depth (people overestimate how well they understand complex systems).

**CC-031 — Diagnostic Reasoning and Clinical Decision-Making**
Medical diagnosis is a cognitive process subject to all biases described above. Errors: premature closure (accepting the first diagnosis that fits and stopping search — most common error), anchoring on initial presentation, availability bias (recently seen diseases judged more probable), commission bias (preferring action over watchful waiting), omission bias (opposite), framing effects (symptoms presented negatively vs. positively). Dual-process diagnostic errors: System 1 pattern recognition fails in atypical presentations; System 2 analytical reasoning fails under time pressure and cognitive overload.

**CC-032 — Group Decision-Making**
Groups make different decisions than individuals: Groupthink (Janis, 1972): in cohesive groups, pressure for consensus suppresses critical evaluation, leading to defective decision-making (Bay of Pigs, Challenger decision). Group polarization: group discussion moves members toward more extreme positions in the direction of their initial lean. Brainstorming generates fewer ideas in groups than individuals working alone (production blocking, evaluation apprehension) — but nominal group technique and electronic brainstorming mitigate this. The common information effect: groups focus discussion on information shared by all members, neglecting unique information held by only some.

**CC-033 — Decision Under Uncertainty: Risk, Knightian Uncertainty, and Ambiguity Aversion**
Risk: probability distribution over outcomes is known. Knightian uncertainty (Knight, 1921): probability distribution itself is unknown (deep uncertainty, radical uncertainty). Ambiguity aversion (Ellsberg Paradox, 1961): people prefer known probability risks over unknown probability risks even when expected value is identical — revealed as a violation of expected utility theory that is not explained by risk aversion alone. Ambiguity aversion drives excessive conservatism in policy, medicine, and investment.

**CC-034 — Expertise and Decision Calibration**
Expert calibration varies significantly by domain: weather forecasters are among the best-calibrated of any professional group (decades of feedback on probabilistic predictions); clinical psychologists predicting violent recidivism are no better than actuarial formulas; economic forecasters perform little better than chance for 12-month predictions; superforecasters (Tetlock's Good Judgment Project) achieve significantly better calibration than government intelligence analysts through structured training in probabilistic reasoning, self-monitoring, and belief updating.

## 5. Patterns

**P-001 — The Generation Effect**
Information is better remembered when it is actively generated than when it is passively read (Slamecka & Graf, 1978). Multiple mechanisms: deeper encoding, distinctive memory traces, and greater elaboration. Educational applications: retrieval practice (testing, free recall, flash cards) significantly outperforms passive re-reading for long-term retention; the "testing effect" is one of the most robust findings in applied cognitive psychology.

**P-002 — Spacing Effect**
Distributing practice across multiple sessions with delays between them produces better long-term retention than massing the same amount of practice in a single session (Ebbinghaus, 1885). Optimal spacing intervals increase as the retention interval increases (expanding retrieval intervals). Spaced repetition software (Anki, SuperMemo) implements spacing algorithms that schedule reviews at optimal intervals, dramatically improving learning efficiency.

**P-003 — Interleaving**
Practicing different types of problems or skills in an interleaved order (ABCBCA) rather than blocked order (AAABBBCCC) produces better long-term retention and transfer despite feeling more difficult during practice. Interleaving forces discrimination between problem types, strengthens the retrieval cue for the appropriate strategy, and prevents the illusion of learning that blocked practice produces. This is an instance of the "desirable difficulties" principle (Bjork).

**P-004 — Pre-Mortem Analysis**
A prospective hindsight technique (Klein, 2007): before implementing a decision, imagine the project has failed and work backward to identify potential causes. This overcomes the planning fallacy (optimism bias), reduces groupthink, encourages surfacing of dissenting information, and provides concrete risk mitigation targets. More effective than asking "what might go wrong?" (which is blocked by motivated reasoning) because the failure is stipulated, reducing psychological threat.

**P-005 — Structured Analytic Techniques (SATs)**
Formal methods for improving judgment and group decision quality in intelligence analysis, strategic planning, and organizational decision-making. Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH): systematically evaluate evidence against all hypotheses simultaneously, weighting by contradicting vs. confirming evidence. Red team analysis: assign a group to argue against the favored position. Devil's advocacy: institutionalized role of challenging group consensus. Key assumptions check: explicitly list and scrutinize the assumptions underlying an analysis.

**P-006 — Reference Class Forecasting**
Kahneman and Tversky's outside-view method (Flyvbjerg): make predictions by identifying a reference class of similar past projects and using the distribution of outcomes from that class as the base rate forecast, before adjusting for specific inside-view information. Systematically reduces the planning fallacy and optimism bias: infrastructure projects actually cost 20–50% more than inside-view estimates on average; reference class adjustment produces better-calibrated forecasts.

**P-007 — Decision Rules and Checklists**
Complex decisions with multiple criteria are made more consistently and accurately by simple linear decision rules and checklists than by unstructured expert judgment (Meehl, 1954; Dawes, 1979). A unit-weight model (equal weighting of predictors) often outperforms actuarial and clinical judgment in prediction tasks. Atul Gawande's checklist manifesto: surgical checklists reduced post-operative complications by 35% and deaths by 47% in a WHO multi-country trial; aviation checklists have been foundational to flight safety for 80+ years.

**P-008 — Cognitive Load Management in Interface Design**
User interface design must minimize extraneous cognitive load to support accurate and efficient task completion. Principles: recognition over recall (menus, autocomplete — don't require users to remember options); chunking (group related information to reduce visual scanning); progressive disclosure (reveal information only when needed); consistent mapping (same actions produce same results); proximity and alignment (Gestalt grouping principles guide attention). Violation of these principles produces user errors, frustration, and increased task completion time.

**P-009 — Debiasing Strategies**
Systematic methods for reducing the impact of cognitive biases on decision quality: Consider the Opposite (generate reasons why an initial judgment might be wrong — reduces overconfidence and anchoring); reference class forecasting (reduces planning fallacy); structured protocols (IDEO design thinking, military MDMP — reduce availability bias by requiring systematic information consideration); Bayesian training (improves probabilistic calibration); red teaming and pre-mortems (reduce confirmation bias); separation of information collection from evaluation (reduces anchoring).

**P-010 — The Hot Hand and Gambler's Fallacy**
Two related but opposite statistical errors: Gambler's Fallacy: believing that a random sequence "must" reverse — after 5 heads, a tail is "due." Hot Hand Fallacy: believing recent successes indicate continued success in a non-stationary random process. Both errors misrepresent randomness. Importantly, some "hot hand" phenomena are real (recent evidence suggests some skill domains, like basketball three-point shooting, exhibit genuine serial correlation beyond chance) — distinguishing genuine serial correlation from the illusion is the statistical challenge.

**P-011 — Selective Attention and Perceptual Load Theory**
Lavie's (1995) perceptual load theory: distractor processing is automatic only when perceptual load of the primary task is low; under high perceptual load, all processing resources are consumed by the primary task, leaving none for distractors. This explains why demanding visual tasks (reading fine print) make us temporarily deaf (auditory distractors ignored) and why low-load tasks (monitoring a single instrument) allow full distractor processing. Load theory predicts when attention will and will not fail.

**P-012 — Bayesian Updating in Expert Judgment**
Well-calibrated experts update their beliefs incrementally in proportion to the strength of new evidence (Bayesian). Failures: insufficient updating (anchoring, conservatism bias), excessive updating (recency bias, overreaction to vivid cases), failure to update at all (belief perseverance). Training in Bayesian reasoning, probabilistic thinking, and explicit tracking of accuracy records over time (the superforecaster approach) produces measurable improvements in forecast calibration.

**P-013 — Cognitive Diversity in Team Decision-Making**
Teams with diverse cognitive styles, information sets, mental models, and domain expertise systematically outperform homogeneous expert groups on complex problems that require integrating multiple perspectives. Diversity benefits are largest for non-routine, complex tasks and when team processes (psychological safety, structured information sharing, explicit norm of dissent) enable diversity to contribute. Diversity alone is insufficient; processes for extracting and integrating diverse perspectives are essential.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP-001 — Outcome Bias**
Evaluating the quality of a decision by its outcome rather than by the quality of the process and information available at the time of the decision. A surgeon who makes a correct decision with available information but the patient dies due to an unforeseeable complication should be evaluated on process; a surgeon who makes a poor decision that happens to produce a good outcome should be critiqued on process. Outcome bias corrupts performance evaluation and reinforces gambling behavior (lucky bad decisions are rewarded).

**AP-002 — Hindsight Bias**
The tendency, after learning an outcome, to believe that one would have predicted that outcome (Fischhoff, 1975). "I knew it all along." Creates overconfidence in forecasting ability, interferes with accurate assessment of decision quality, and impairs learning from past decisions (if the outcome seemed inevitable, there was no real failure to learn from). Hindsight bias is nearly impossible to eliminate without explicit debiasing and documentation of pre-outcome predictions.

**AP-003 — The Planning Fallacy**
Systematic underestimation of time, cost, and risks of future projects while overestimating benefits (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). The inside view (focusing on this specific project's details) dominates the outside view (the track record of similar projects). Almost all major infrastructure projects (The Sydney Opera House: estimated AU$7M, actual AU$102M; Denver Airport: estimated $1.7B, actual $4.8B) dramatically exceed initial estimates. Reference class forecasting is the evidence-based corrective.

**AP-004 — Illusion of Understanding (Illusion of Explanatory Depth)**
People systematically overestimate how well they understand how complex systems work (Rozenblit & Kelman, 2002). When asked to explain a toilet flush mechanism, bicycle gear shifting, or zipper in detail, most people quickly discover they understand far less than they believed. This illusion drives overconfident policy advocacy, poor system design decisions, and resistance to expert guidance. The illusion collapses under the demand for specific, detailed explanation.

**AP-005 — Belief Perseverance**
The tendency to maintain beliefs even after the evidence supporting them has been discredited or shown to be false. Anderson et al. (1980): correcting false information from a study reduced but did not eliminate its influence on subsequent judgment. The continued influence effect: even when explicitly told that a news story was false, people's reasoning continues to be influenced by it. Belief perseverance is strongest for personally significant beliefs and beliefs integrated into causal explanatory frameworks.

**AP-006 — Groupthink**
Janis (1972): Irving Janis identified groupthink from analysis of high-profile decision fiascoes (Bay of Pigs, Pearl Harbor, Challenger). Symptoms: illusion of invulnerability, collective rationalization, belief in inherent morality of the group, stereotyped views of outgroups, self-censorship of doubts, illusion of unanimity, direct pressure on dissenters, self-appointed mindguards. Structural solutions: assign devil's advocate role, invite external experts, discuss with trusted outsiders before final decision, hold second-chance meeting.

**AP-007 — Availability Cascade**
A self-reinforcing process by which collective beliefs are amplified by media coverage and social communication — independent of their accuracy. Media coverage of an event (shark attacks, plane crashes, terrorism) makes it more cognitively available, increasing perceived probability, generating public demand for regulation, which generates further coverage, which further amplifies availability. Slovic and Kahneman: the availability cascade can produce societal overreaction to small risks and underreaction to large but less dramatic risks.

**AP-008 — Scope Insensitivity**
People's willingness to pay or donate to prevent harm does not scale proportionally with the magnitude of harm. Kahneman et al.: willingness to pay to save 2,000, 20,000, or 200,000 migrating birds from oil ponds was approximately the same ($80, $78, $88) — sensitivity to quantity is absent. This is because harm is evaluated by the emotional image of a single suffering bird (affect heuristic) rather than by statistical magnitude. Scope insensitivity undermines rational cost-benefit allocation of charitable resources.

**AP-009 — Attribution Errors: FAE and SAB**
Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE, Ross, 1977): overattributing others' behavior to stable dispositional factors (character, attitude) and underattributing it to situational factors (context, pressure). Actor-Observer Asymmetry (AOA): we explain our own behavior situationally, others' dispositionally. Self-Serving Bias (SSB): attributing successes to internal factors (ability, effort) and failures to external factors (bad luck, unfair circumstances). These attribution biases systematically distort interpersonal judgment, organizational performance evaluation, and political perception.

**AP-010 — False Consensus Effect**
People overestimate the extent to which others share their beliefs, preferences, attitudes, and behaviors (Ross et al., 1977). A Republican overestimates how many Americans are Republican; a vegetarian overestimates how many are vegetarian; a person who would accept ransom terms overestimates how many others would. The false consensus effect produces poor market research (projecting one's own preferences onto consumers), poor negotiation (assuming the other party shares your assessment of fairness), and political polarization misperception.

## 7. Facts

**F-001** — George Miller's "magical number seven, plus or minus two" (1956, Psychological Review) proposed that short-term memory capacity is approximately 7 ± 2 "chunks"; later work by Cowan (2001) and Luck & Vogel (1997) revised this estimate to approximately 3–4 ± 1 items when rehearsal is prevented, with the discrepancy explained by Miller's inclusion of rehearsal-extended items.

**F-002** — Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized thousands of nonsense syllables to study his own memory; his 1885 book "Über das Gedächtnis" established the forgetting curve, the spacing effect, the learning curve, and the concepts of savings (relearning costs less than original learning) — laying the empirical foundation for memory research.

**F-003** — The Stroop task (Stroop, 1935): naming the ink color of color words (red printed in blue ink) takes longer than naming the color of neutral shapes — because the automatic word-reading process interferes with the controlled color-naming process. Stroop interference is one of the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology and is used to assess executive function and selective attention.

**F-004** — The cocktail party problem (Cherry, 1953) and dichotic listening paradigms showed that unattended channels are largely blocked at a semantic level; Moray (1959) showed that one's name breaks through the attentional filter approximately 33% of the time even in unattended channels.

**F-005** — Cognitive psychology emerged as a discipline with Miller, Galanter & Pribram's "Plans and the Structure of Behavior" (1960) and the founding of the Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior (1962); it replaced behaviorism's stimulus-response paradigm with information-processing models of the mind.

**F-006** — The Müller-Lyer illusion (two lines of equal length, one with inward-pointing arrowheads, one with outward-pointing) persists even when one knows the lines are equal; perception and belief can dissociate — top-down knowledge does not override bottom-up perceptual processing, demonstrating modularity of perception.

**F-007** — Inattentional blindness was demonstrated by Simons and Chabris (1999) in the "invisible gorilla" study; approximately 50% of observers missed the gorilla; the study has been replicated across many variants and has become the most widely cited demonstration of the limits of attention in popular and scientific discourse.

**F-008** — Baddeley's multicomponent working memory model has been the dominant framework since 1974 (Baddeley & Hitch); the phonological loop is the most studied component — its capacity is approximately 2 seconds of speech in duration, explaining the articulatory loop for rehearsal and the word-length effect (fewer long words than short words are retained).

**F-009** — Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch (1974) originally proposed the working memory model to challenge Atkinson and Shiffrin's (1968) modal model (sensory store → short-term store → long-term store); subsequent neuropsychological evidence (e.g., patient KF who had impaired verbal STM but intact LTM) supported fractionation of working memory.

**F-010** — The testing effect (retrieval practice effect): students who study material and then take tests on it recall 50–80% more on a final test than students who study and re-study without testing, even though re-studying feels more effective during practice (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006, Psychological Science).

**F-011** — Kahneman and Tversky's collaboration began in the early 1970s at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem; their 1974 Science paper "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" has been cited over 40,000 times and initiated the behavioral revolution in economics, law, medicine, and policy.

**F-012** — Daniel Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002, the first psychologist to do so; Amos Tversky had died in 1996 (Nobel Prizes are not awarded posthumously); the Nobel Committee stated the prize was for "having integrated insights from psychological research into economic science."

**F-013** — Richard Thaler won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2017 for his contributions to behavioral economics, specifically mental accounting, the endowment effect, and the nudge framework.

**F-014** — The Iowa Gambling Task (Bechara et al., 1994): participants choose cards from four decks; two yield small consistent gains, two yield large gains but larger occasional losses (net negative). Patients with ventromedial PFC damage fail to shift to advantageous decks; normal subjects shift before they can verbally articulate why — demonstrating somatic markers (emotional gut feelings) guide decision-making before conscious reasoning.

**F-015** — The endowment effect (Thaler, 1980): people demand more to give up an object they own than they would pay to acquire the same object; in Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler's (1990) mug experiment, mug owners demanded ~$7 on average; non-owners offered ~$3.50 — approximately twice the gap. The endowment effect is driven by loss aversion.

**F-016** — Dunning and Kruger (1999) showed that people with poor performance in a domain systematically overestimate their relative performance, and people with high performance systematically underestimate their relative performance — the "Kruger-Dunning effect." The finding has been partly reinterpreted as a statistical artifact (regression to the mean), but genuine metacognitive calibration differences do exist between low- and high-skilled performers.

**F-017** — Tetlock's "Expert Political Judgment" (2005) tracked 82,361 forecasts by 284 experts over 20 years; expert predictions were barely better than chance for medium-term political forecasts, and marginally better than dilettantes; specialists were often worse than generalists (foxes who know many things outperform hedgehogs who know one big thing).

**F-018** — The Good Judgment Project (Tetlock & Mellers): superforecasters — self-selected from a large pool via training and track record — achieved calibration approximately 60% better than intelligence analysts with access to classified information, demonstrating that structured probabilistic thinking can dramatically improve forecast accuracy.

**F-019** — The planning fallacy is particularly severe in infrastructure: a Flyvbjerg meta-analysis (2003) of 258 transportation infrastructure projects found cost overruns in 90% of cases, with an average overrun of 28% for roads and 45% for rail projects; Sydney Opera House (1,400% cost overrun) and Boston Big Dig (190% overrun) are iconic examples.

**F-020** — Chunking: chess grandmasters can reconstruct a mid-game position from memory after 5 seconds of exposure with ~90% accuracy; they show no advantage over novices for random piece configurations — confirming Chase and Simon's (1973) conclusion that grandmaster memory is pattern-based (template recall), not a general photographic memory.

**F-021** — The Stroop task has been administered to approximately 700+ published studies; the interference effect is approximately 100–200 ms (naming ink color of congruent color word takes ~300–400 ms; incongruent ~400–600 ms); age, anxiety, ADHD, schizophrenia, and traumatic brain injury all modulate the Stroop interference effect.

**F-022** — The availability heuristic was demonstrated by Tversky and Kahneman (1973): when asked whether there are more English words beginning with 'R' or having 'R' as the third letter, most people say 'R-first' — because such words come to mind more easily, though there are in fact more words with 'R' in third position.

**F-023** — Milgram's obedience experiments (1963): approximately 65% of participants administered what they believed was a 450-volt shock to a learner (confederate) when instructed by an authority figure, even as the "learner" screamed in protest. Replicated across cultures (though rates vary), the study remains the most famous demonstration of situational influences on behavior overriding individual moral judgment.

**F-024** — Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory (1957): holding two inconsistent cognitions creates psychological tension (dissonance) that motivates attitude or behavior change to restore consistency. Classic study: paid $1 or $20 to tell the next subject a boring task was interesting; $1-paid subjects later rated the task as more interesting (dissonance reduction — insufficient external justification requires attitude change); $20-paid subjects did not (sufficient external justification).

**F-025** — The anchoring effect was demonstrated in Tversky and Kahneman (1974): participants spun a wheel (rigged to land on 10 or 65) then estimated the percentage of African countries in the UN; those who landed on 65 gave significantly higher estimates (~45%) than those who landed on 10 (~25%) — though the wheel spin was demonstrably random and irrelevant.

**F-026** — Damasio's Somatic Marker Hypothesis (1994): decision-making in complex personal and social situations requires emotional signals (somatic markers — body-based gut feelings) to pre-filter options before rational deliberation. Without somatic markers (as in vmPFC damage), patients become paralyzed by endless deliberation and make consistently poor real-life decisions despite intact intelligence.

**F-027** — Fast and Frugal Heuristics (Gigerenzen & Todd, 1999): simple heuristics that use a minimum of information (Take the Best: decide on the one best cue, ignore the rest) match or exceed the accuracy of complex optimization models across a wide range of real-world prediction tasks, especially in small-sample, noisy environments where model overfitting is a problem.

**F-028** — Flow was first described by Csikszentmihalyi in "Beyond Boredom and Anxiety" (1975) based on studies of chess players, rock climbers, surgeons, and composers; subsequent research has identified flow in virtually every domain of skilled human activity and across cultures.

**F-029** — Herbert Simon coined the term "bounded rationality" in "A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice" (1955) and "satisficing" from the portmanteau of "satisfy" and "suffice"; Simon won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978 "for his pioneering research into the decision-making process within economic organizations."

**F-030** — Priming effects: exposure to the word "doctor" makes the word "nurse" recognized faster (semantic priming); exposure to elderly-related words (Florida, gray, wrinkle) caused participants to walk more slowly to the elevator (behavioral priming — Bargh et al., 1996). The behavioral priming effects have been among the most difficult to replicate in the replication crisis; semantic priming is extremely robust.

**F-031** — The replication crisis in psychology: the Reproducibility Project (Open Science Collaboration, 2015) attempted to replicate 100 published psychology studies; only approximately 39% of effects replicated with p<0.05; average effect sizes in replications were approximately 50% of the original. Social priming and ego depletion studies have been particularly difficult to replicate; basic cognitive effects (Stroop, spacing, testing) replicate reliably.

**F-032** — Decision fatigue empirical data: in the Israeli parole board study (Danziger et al., 2011), judges granted approximately 65% of paroles in the first decision block of the day, dropping to nearly 0% just before food breaks and recovering after them; this was interpreted as decision fatigue, but alternative explanations (sequential contrast effects, session timing of case types) have been proposed.

**F-033** — Working memory training (Cogmed, Lumosity): commercial programs training working memory capacity show improvements on trained tasks (near transfer) but poor evidence for transfer to untrained tasks, IQ, or academic outcomes (far transfer). A rigorous meta-analysis by Melby-Lervåg and Hulme (2013) found specific near-transfer effects decayed after months and no meaningful far-transfer effects.

**F-034** — The Affect Heuristic (Slovic et al., 2002): people evaluate risks and benefits of activities (nuclear power, chemical plants, food additives) according to their affective response (how good or bad it feels) rather than independent judgment of risk and benefit; activities perceived as good are judged as having high benefit AND low risk; perceived-as-bad activities are judged as having low benefit AND high risk — producing a systematic inverse correlation between perceived risk and benefit that does not reflect reality.

**F-035** — The ACT-R (Adaptive Control of Thought-Rational) cognitive architecture (Anderson, 1983/2007) provides a unified computational model of human cognition — specifying in millisecond detail how declarative memory retrieval, procedural rule firing, visual attention, and motor planning interact to produce complex task performance. It is the most implemented cognitive architecture for modeling human experimental data.

**F-036** — Intrinsic motivation and extrinsic rewards: adding extrinsic rewards (money) to activities that are intrinsically motivating can reduce intrinsic motivation (overjustification effect, Deci & Ryan, 1971). Cognitive evaluation theory: controlling extrinsic rewards undermine autonomy and competence needs; informational rewards (positive feedback on competence) can enhance intrinsic motivation. Highly relevant to gamification design and employee motivation systems.

**F-037** — Mental imagery: the same cortical regions active during visual perception (including primary visual cortex in some conditions) are active during visual mental imagery, suggesting imagery uses top-down perceptual simulation rather than a separate representational system (Kosslyn's imagery debates with Pylyshyn's propositional representation theory, spanning 1973–2003).

**F-038** — Stereotype threat (Steele & Aronson, 1995): the awareness that one could confirm a negative stereotype about one's social group impairs performance on the stereotyped domain. African-American college students performed significantly worse on a verbal test when told it measured intellectual ability (invoking the racial stereotype) than when told it was a problem-solving study. Stereotype threat is a situational factor that contributes to observed group performance differences independently of ability.

**F-039** — The 10,000-hour rule (Gladwell's popularization of Ericsson's research): Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) found that deliberate practice accumulated over approximately 10,000 hours distinguished expert from non-expert violinists; Gladwell's popularization was an oversimplification — the original research emphasized quality of deliberate practice over raw hours; subsequent work shows large individual variation and domain-specific patterns.

**F-040** — Kahneman's "What You See Is All There Is" (WYSIATI): System 1 constructs the best possible story from available information without representing missing information; it is systematically unaware of what it does not know. This produces overconfidence, narrative fallacies, and failure to seek disconfirming evidence — because the constructed story is evaluated for coherence, not comprehensiveness.

**F-041** — Psychological distance (Construal Level Theory, Trope & Liberman, 2010): the psychological distance of an event (temporal, spatial, social, or hypothetical) determines its level of construal. Distant events are represented abstractly ("why" level); proximate events concretely ("how" level). Distant risks are underweighted relative to proximate ones; distant people are perceived more abstractly and with less empathy. CLT explains temporal discounting, why climate change is psychologically distant, and why people behave differently when imagining vs. actually in situations.

**F-042** — The correlation between IQ (general cognitive ability, g) and decision-making task performance is approximately r = 0.20–0.40 (moderate); CRT scores correlate with decision quality at approximately r = 0.30–0.45; neither IQ nor CRT are sufficient predictors of real-world decision quality, which also depends on domain knowledge, motivation, and decision process quality.

**F-043** — The Sunk Cost fallacy is most pronounced when: (1) the sunk cost is large and salient; (2) the person made the original commitment; (3) there is no external accountability; (4) there is no obvious opportunity cost. Managers consistently continue projects with negative expected return because of prior investment; investors hold losing stocks longer than winners (disposition effect); relationships continue despite clear dysfunction partly because of past investment.

**F-044** — Multi-attribute utility theory (MAUT) and the Simple Multi-Attribute Rating Technique (SMART) provide structured methods for complex multi-criteria decisions: identify attributes, weight them by importance, score each option on each attribute, compute weighted sums. These structured approaches consistently outperform unstructured expert judgment in prediction tasks (Dawes, 1979) — even unit-weight models (equal weights) typically beat unstructured clinical judgment.

**F-045** — The conjunction fallacy (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983): the "Linda problem" — Linda is a 31-year-old outspoken feminist; which is more probable? (A) Linda is a bank teller; (B) Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement? Most people choose B, violating the basic probability rule that P(A ∩ B) ≤ P(A). The representativeness of the description overrides probabilistic reasoning even among statistically trained subjects.

**F-046** — Mirror neurons (Rizzolatti, 1992) in macaques and presumed equivalent systems in humans may contribute to observational learning and imitation; the "broken mirror" hypothesis of autism (deficient mirror neuron function) is now considered an oversimplification — the empirical evidence for ASD-specific mirror neuron dysfunction is mixed and contested.

**F-047** — Emotional regulation strategies: cognitive reappraisal (reinterpreting the emotional meaning of a situation — "this stressful interview is an exciting challenge") is more effective than expressive suppression (hiding emotional expression) for both emotional experience reduction and long-term well-being; suppression reduces emotional expression but increases physiological arousal and is cognitively taxing.

**F-048** — The Von Restorff effect (isolation effect, 1933): a distinctive item in a series is more likely to be remembered than other items. The practical application: in interfaces, important information should be visually distinctive; in educational materials, key concepts should be highlighted, formatted differently, or presented as exceptions to a pattern — isolation from surrounding context boosts memorability.
