## 4. Core Concepts

**CC-001 — Jurisprudence: Natural Law vs. Legal Positivism**
Natural law theory: law derives from or must conform to a higher moral order (divine law, universal reason); unjust law is not law in the fullest sense ("lex iniusta non est lex" — St. Thomas Aquinas); revived by Lon Fuller (procedural natural law: "inner morality of law" — 8 principles including generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity) and Ronald Dworkin (rights as trumps; principles and policies beyond rules). Legal positivism: law is a social fact, not a moral fact; the existence of law and its merit are separate questions (Hart: the "separability thesis"); the law is what the sovereign commands (Austin) or what officials recognize as valid law (Hart's rule of recognition).

**CC-002 — Common Law vs. Civil Law Systems**
Common law (UK origin): judge-made law through precedent (stare decisis); adversarial litigation; rules developed case-by-case; equity as a separate body of principles; statutory law overlaid on common law base. Adopted in: UK, US, Canada, Australia, India, most former British colonies. Civil law (Roman law/Justinian Code origin): comprehensive statutory codes (civil code, commercial code, penal code); inquisitorial procedure; doctrine secondary to code; judges apply codes rather than making law. Adopted in: France, Germany, Spain, Latin America, most of continental Europe and non-British Asia/Africa. Mixed systems: South Africa, Scotland, Quebec, Louisiana (civil law code + common law procedure).

**CC-003 — Constitutional Law: US Framework**
The US Constitution (1787, effective 1789) establishes: separation of powers (legislative, executive, judicial) with checks and balances; federalism (divided sovereignty between national and state governments); individual rights (Bill of Rights — first 10 amendments; 14th Amendment — due process, equal protection). Judicial review (power to declare laws unconstitutional): not explicit in the Constitution; established by Chief Justice Marshall in Marbury v. Madison (1803). Constitutional interpretation: originalism (interpret by original public meaning or framer intent) vs. living constitutionalism (interpret in light of evolving standards and societal needs) — a central debate in US constitutional law.

**CC-004 — Contract Law: Formation**
A valid contract requires: offer (proposal to enter into legally binding agreement, with definite terms); acceptance (unconditional assent to all terms — mirror image rule; or in UCC: battle of forms); consideration (each party must give something of legal value — a bargained-for exchange; past consideration is not valid consideration; moral obligation is not sufficient under majority rule); capacity (parties must have legal capacity: adults, mental competence); legality (subject matter must be legal). Mutual assent must be objective (outward manifestation of assent, not purely subjective intent). Promissory estoppel: equitable doctrine enforcing promises without consideration when reasonable reliance and injustice would otherwise result (Restatement Second §90).

**CC-005 — Contract Law: Breach and Remedies**
Material breach: so significant it excuses the other party's performance (vs. minor breach: excused but damages available). Anticipatory repudiation: clear indication before performance is due that the party will not perform — immediately actionable. Expectation damages: put the non-breaching party in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed (benefit of the bargain). Reliance damages: reimburse expenses incurred in reliance on the contract. Restitution: prevent unjust enrichment. Specific performance (equitable remedy): ordered when money damages are inadequate (unique goods, real property). Limitations: duty to mitigate damages; foreseeability (Hadley v. Baxendale: consequential damages limited to reasonably foreseeable losses at time of contracting).

**CC-006 — Tort Law: Negligence**
The four elements of negligence: (1) Duty: defendant owed a legal duty of care to the plaintiff (reasonable person standard; Palsgraf: duty owed only to foreseeable plaintiffs); (2) Breach: failure to conform to the required standard of care; (3) Causation: actual cause (but-for causation; substantial factor test for multiple causes) and proximate cause (foreseeability limitation on liability; intervening acts); (4) Damages: actual harm (nominal damages generally not available for negligence). Contributory negligence (minority rule): plaintiff's negligence bars all recovery. Comparative negligence (majority rule): plaintiff's recovery reduced proportionally to fault. Assumption of risk: voluntary encountering of a known risk.

**CC-007 — Tort Law: Strict Liability and Products Liability**
Strict liability imposes liability without fault for abnormally dangerous activities (Rylands v. Fletcher; Restatement Second §520) and for wild animals. Products liability: manufacturer's strict liability for defective products causing harm (Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, 1963 — California; Restatement Second §402A). Three types of product defects: manufacturing defects (product deviates from intended design); design defects (reasonable alternative design test [RAD] or consumer expectations test); failure to warn (inadequate warnings of non-obvious risks). Learned intermediary doctrine: in pharmaceutical cases, manufacturer's duty to warn runs to prescribing physician, not patient.

**CC-008 — Criminal Law: Elements of Crimes**
Most crimes require: actus reus (guilty act — voluntary act or omission where there is a duty to act) and mens rea (guilty mind). Four levels of mens rea under Model Penal Code (MPC): Purpose (conscious object to engage in conduct or cause result), Knowledge (aware that conduct is of that nature or that result will occur with substantial certainty), Recklessness (conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk), Negligence (failure to perceive a substantial and unjustifiable risk that a reasonable person would perceive). Strict liability crimes: no mens rea required (most regulatory offenses, statutory rape in many jurisdictions). Causation: proximate and actual cause required for result crimes.

**CC-009 — Criminal Defenses**
Justification defenses: the act was right (or at least permissible) under the circumstances. Self-defense: reasonable belief that imminent harm is threatened; force proportional to the threat; no duty to retreat in many US jurisdictions ("Stand Your Ground" laws, ~30 states). Necessity: choice between two evils, lesser evil chosen. Excuse defenses: the act was wrong but the actor is not fully responsible. Insanity (McNaughten test: did not know nature of act or that it was wrong; MPC test: lacks substantial capacity to appreciate wrongfulness or conform conduct to law). Duress: coerced by immediate threat of serious harm. Mistake of fact: negates mens rea if it would make the act innocent if facts were as believed. Entrapment: government induced a person to commit a crime they were not predisposed to commit.

**CC-010 — Constitutional Criminal Procedure**
Fourth Amendment: prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures; warrant requirement (probable cause, particularity); exceptions: exigent circumstances, plain view, automobile, consent, search incident to arrest, Terry stops. Exclusionary rule: evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is inadmissible (Mapp v. Ohio, 1961 — applied to states). Fifth Amendment: protection against self-incrimination (privilege against compelled self-incrimination; Miranda rights required before custodial interrogation); double jeopardy (not twice tried for the same offense). Sixth Amendment: right to counsel (Gideon v. Wainwright, 1963); right to speedy trial, public trial, jury trial, confrontation of witnesses. Fourteenth Amendment: Due Process and Equal Protection applied to states; incorporated most of the Bill of Rights.

**CC-011 — Evidence Law**
Relevance: evidence must make a fact of consequence more or less probable (FRE 401); relevant evidence generally admissible (FRE 402); may be excluded if prejudicial effect substantially outweighs probative value (FRE 403). Hearsay: out-of-court statement offered for the truth of the matter asserted; generally inadmissible (FRE 802); numerous exceptions (dying declarations, excited utterances, business records, admissions by party-opponent, etc.). Character evidence: generally not admissible to prove conduct on a particular occasion (FRE 404(a)); exceptions: criminal defendant's character, victim's character, prior crimes/acts for non-character purposes (MIMIC). Expert testimony: Daubert standard (FRE 702) — trial judge as gatekeeper; reliable methodology; relevant.

**CC-012 — Civil Procedure**
Jurisdiction: personal jurisdiction (minimum contacts with the forum — International Shoe, 1945) and subject matter jurisdiction (federal question or diversity of citizenship ≥ $75,000). Erie doctrine: federal courts in diversity cases apply state substantive law, federal procedural law. FRCP: discovery (interrogatories, depositions, requests for production, requests for admission, expert disclosure); pleading (notice pleading post-Iqbal/Twombly — plausible on its face, not conclusory); summary judgment (no genuine dispute of material fact — Rule 56). Res judicata (claim preclusion): final judgment bars relitigation of claims that were or could have been raised. Collateral estoppel (issue preclusion): a litigated and decided issue is binding in subsequent proceedings.

**CC-013 — Administrative Law**
Federal agencies derive authority from statutes (enabling legislation); act through rulemaking (notice-and-comment: APA §553), adjudication (APA §554–557), and enforcement. Chevron deference (Chevron USA v. NRDC, 1984 — now overruled by Loper Bright, 2024): courts previously deferred to agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes; Loper Bright overruled Chevron, requiring courts to exercise independent judgment on statutory interpretation. Arbitrary-and-capricious review: agency actions set aside if the agency failed to consider relevant factors, relied on irrelevant factors, or made a clear error (Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Assn v. State Farm, 1983). Due process in agency adjudication: Mathews v. Eldridge balancing test: private interest, risk of erroneous deprivation, and government interest.

**CC-014 — International Law**
Sources of international law (ICJ Statute Art. 38): international treaties (binding on parties), customary international law (CIL — general practice + opinio juris, binding on all states except persistent objectors), general principles of law recognized by civilized nations, judicial decisions and teachings (subsidiary). State sovereignty: states have exclusive jurisdiction within their territory; jus cogens (peremptory norms): non-derogable norms (prohibition on genocide, torture, slavery, aggression) binding on all states regardless of consent. International enforcement: no global police force; enforcement through: sanctions, retorsion, countermeasures, ICJ jurisdiction (contentious and advisory), ICC (criminal accountability for individuals — genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, aggression).

**CC-015 — Patent Law**
A patent grants the patentee an exclusive right to exclude others from making, using, selling, or importing the patented invention for 20 years from filing. Patentability requirements (US): patent-eligible subject matter (35 USC §101 — processes, machines, manufactures, compositions of matter; excluded: laws of nature, natural phenomena, abstract ideas — Alice/Mayo framework); novelty (35 USC §102 — not anticipated by prior art); non-obviousness (35 USC §103 — not obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the art, PHOSITA, considering Graham v. John Deere factors); enablement and written description (35 USC §112). The patent document: claims (define the legal scope), specification (enable the invention), and drawings.

**CC-016 — Copyright Law**
Copyright protects original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium (17 USC §102). Protected works: literary, musical, dramatic, choreographic, pictorial/graphic, audiovisual, sound recordings, architectural works. Protection arises automatically upon creation (registration not required for existence but required for statutory damages/attorneys' fees in the US). Duration: life + 70 years (individual); 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation (corporate/work for hire) (Sonny Bono CTEA). Copyright protects expression, not ideas (idea-expression dichotomy, 17 USC §102(b)). Fair use (17 USC §107): four-factor test — purpose and character of use (transformative?), nature of copyrighted work, amount used, effect on market.

**CC-017 — Trademark Law**
A trademark is a word, phrase, symbol, design, or combination that identifies and distinguishes the source of goods/services. Rights acquired through use (in US) or registration (internationally via Madrid Protocol). Distinctiveness spectrum: generic (never protectable), descriptive (protectable with secondary meaning), suggestive (inherently distinctive), arbitrary, fanciful (strongest marks). Infringement: likelihood of confusion test (Polaroid factors in 2nd Circuit; DuPont factors in TMEP). Dilution: blurring or tarnishment of famous marks regardless of confusion (Lanham Act §43(c); FTDA/TDRA — requires famous nationwide mark). Geographic indications: Champagne, Scotch Whisky, Parmigiano-Reggiano — origin-specific designations protected under TRIPS.

**CC-018 — Trade Secret Law**
A trade secret is information (formula, pattern, compilation, program, device, method, technique) that: has independent economic value from secrecy, and is the subject of reasonable measures to maintain its secrecy. DTSA (Defend Trade Secrets Act, 2016 — federal civil cause of action); prior state protection via Uniform Trade Secrets Act. Misappropriation: acquisition by improper means or disclosure/use without consent. Unlike patents, trade secrets have no fixed term (can last indefinitely — Coca-Cola formula alleged to have been secret since 1886) but are lost if the secret is independently discovered or disclosed. Non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) and reasonable security measures are the primary protection mechanisms.

**CC-019 — Corporate Law**
The corporation: a legal entity separate from its shareholders (Dartmouth College, 1819). Characteristics: separate legal personality, limited liability (shareholders' liability limited to investment), perpetual existence, centralized management (board of directors), transferable ownership. Directors' duties: duty of care (business judgment rule — courts defer to directors' decisions if informed, good faith, honest belief it's in the corporation's best interest) and duty of loyalty (no self-dealing, usurping corporate opportunities). Piercing the corporate veil: holding shareholders personally liable when the corporate form is used to perpetrate fraud or there is inadequate capitalization plus failure to observe formalities.

**CC-020 — Contract Law: UCC Article 2 (Goods)**
The Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) Article 2 governs contracts for the sale of goods (personal property, not real estate, services, or securities). Merchant rules differ from common law: merchants (people regularly dealing in goods of the kind) are held to higher standards. Statute of frauds (UCC 2-201): sale of goods ≥ $500 requires a writing (or electronic record) sufficient to indicate a contract. Implied warranties: warranty of merchantability (goods are fit for ordinary purpose — UCC 2-314), warranty of fitness for a particular purpose (UCC 2-315). Perfect tender rule (common law) modified: UCC 2-601 (right to reject if goods don't conform); cure (UCC 2-508); substantial performance doctrine does not apply.

**CC-021 — Securities Law**
Securities Act of 1933: requires registration of securities offerings (Form S-1) or reliance on exemptions (Reg D private placements, Reg A+, Rule 144A, Reg CF crowdfunding); disclosure document (prospectus). Securities Exchange Act of 1934: regulates secondary market trading; created SEC; anti-fraud provisions (Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 — prohibit material misstatements, omissions, or deceptive practices in connection with securities transactions). Insider trading: trading on material non-public information (MNPI) in violation of a duty; a violation of Rule 10b-5; the "misappropriation theory" extends liability to outsiders who misappropriate information from their principal.

**CC-022 — Labor and Employment Law**
Employment at will (US common law default): employer may terminate for any reason or no reason, not just cause; exceptions: anti-discrimination laws, public policy exceptions, implied contracts, good faith implied covenant (minority). Title VII (Civil Rights Act 1964): prohibits employment discrimination on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, national origin; disparate treatment (intentional) and disparate impact (facially neutral policy with discriminatory effect — Griggs v. Duke Power). NLRA (National Labor Relations Act 1935): protects employees' right to organize, bargain collectively; unfair labor practices. FMLA (1993): 12 weeks unpaid leave for qualifying family/medical reasons. At-will employment contrasts with "just cause" employment common in Europe and Canada.

**CC-023 — Property Law**
Property rights: a bundle of rights (use, exclusion, alienation). Real property (land and fixtures): fee simple absolute (fullest ownership), life estate, leaseholds. Adverse possession: open, notorious, exclusive, hostile, continuous possession for the statutory period (typically 10–20 years) transfers title. Recording acts: bona fide purchaser protection (race, notice, race-notice statutes protect subsequent purchasers without notice against prior unrecorded interests). The Rule Against Perpetuities (RAP): no future interest is valid unless it must vest within a life in being at creation of the interest plus 21 years (criticized for complexity; many states have abolished or modified it).

**CC-024 — Constitutional Law: First Amendment**
Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances. Content-neutral restrictions: intermediate scrutiny — narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest and leaves open alternative channels. Content-based restrictions: strict scrutiny — compelling government interest, narrowly tailored. Unprotected speech categories: incitement to imminent lawless action (Brandenburg v. Ohio), obscenity (Miller test), true threats, defamation, fraud. Commercial speech: intermediate scrutiny (Central Hudson test).

**CC-025 — Constitutional Law: Equal Protection and Suspect Classifications**
14th Amendment Equal Protection Clause: no state shall deny to any person the equal protection of the laws. Three tiers of scrutiny: Rational basis (economic regulations, age, disability): government action upheld if rationally related to a legitimate interest (extreme deference). Intermediate scrutiny (sex, illegitimacy): substantially related to an important government interest. Strict scrutiny (race, national origin, alienage; fundamental rights): necessary to achieve a compelling government interest, narrowly tailored (most laws fail). Affirmative action in higher education admissions: SFFA v. Harvard/UNC (2023) — SCOTUS held race-conscious admissions programs at Harvard and UNC violate Equal Protection.

**CC-026 — Antitrust Law**
Sherman Act §1: prohibits contracts, combinations, and conspiracies in restraint of trade (horizontal price-fixing, bid-rigging, market allocation per se illegal; vertical restraints analyzed under rule of reason). Sherman Act §2: prohibits monopolization and attempted monopolization; requires: monopoly power (>70% market share typical threshold) in a relevant market + willful acquisition or maintenance of that power (as opposed to growth or development as a consequence of a superior product, business acumen, or historical accident). Clayton Act §7: prohibits mergers and acquisitions whose effect may be substantially to lessen competition (HSR Act: pre-merger notification for large transactions). FTC Act §5: prohibits unfair methods of competition.

**CC-027 — Environmental Law**
Clean Air Act (1970): NAAQS (National Ambient Air Quality Standards) for criteria pollutants; NSPS (New Source Performance Standards) for new sources; MACT for HAPs; PSD (prevention of significant deterioration) permitting; Title V operating permits; EPA regulates greenhouse gases (Massachusetts v. EPA, 2007 — GHGs are air pollutants). Clean Water Act (1972): NPDES permits for point source discharges; §404 dredge-and-fill permits (Army Corps + EPA); water quality standards and TMDLs; "waters of the United States" (WOTUS) definition is a contested regulatory boundary. CERCLA (1980): strict, joint-and-several, retroactive liability for hazardous substance cleanup; NPL superfund sites.

**CC-028 — Data Privacy Law**
GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation, EU 2018): applies to processing of personal data of EU residents by any organization globally; rights: access, rectification, erasure ("right to be forgotten"), portability, restriction, object; lawful bases for processing (consent, contract, legitimate interest, etc.); privacy by design; mandatory breach notification within 72 hours; fines up to 4% of global annual revenue or €20M. CCPA/CPRA (California Consumer Privacy Act/California Privacy Rights Act): California state equivalent; opt-out of sale of personal information; right to know, delete, correct. HIPAA: health information privacy in US (covered entities + business associates); minimum necessary standard; PHI security.

**CC-029 — International Arbitration**
International commercial arbitration: private dispute resolution outside national courts; finality and enforceability governed by the New York Convention (1958, ~170 state parties): arbitral awards recognized and enforced in all contracting states. Leading arbitral institutions: ICC (Paris), AAA/ICDR (New York), LCIA (London), SIAC (Singapore), CIETAC (Beijing). ISDS (Investor-State Dispute Settlement): mechanisms in BITs (bilateral investment treaties) and multilateral treaties (NAFTA/USMCA, ECT) allowing foreign investors to sue host states for violation of investment protections (fair and equitable treatment, expropriation). ICSID (World Bank): specialized tribunal for investment disputes.

**CC-030 — Regulatory Law: Agency Rulemaking**
Notice-and-comment rulemaking (APA §553): agency publishes proposed rule in Federal Register → public comment period (60+ days for major rules) → agency responds to significant comments → final rule published → delayed effective date. Major rules: OIRA (OMB's Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs) review for significant regulatory actions (>$100M economic impact) under Executive Order 12866/13563. Final rules judicially reviewed for: procedural compliance with APA, statutory authority (post-Loper Bright: courts decide independently), factual basis (substantial evidence in formal rulemaking; arbitrary and capricious in informal). Congressional Review Act (CRA): Congress may disapprove a final rule by joint resolution signed by the President.

**CC-031 — Criminal Procedure: Trial and Sentencing**
The US felony trial process: indictment (grand jury for federal felonies; 5th Amendment right) or information (waivable) → arraignment → discovery (Brady material: exculpatory evidence must be disclosed) → pretrial motions (suppression) → jury selection (voir dire; Batson challenges to discriminatory peremptory strikes) → trial (burden of proof: beyond a reasonable doubt) → verdict (must be unanimous in federal and most state courts) → sentencing. Federal Sentencing Guidelines (advisory post-Booker, 2005): grid of offense level and criminal history category → recommended range. Mandatory minimums: congressional floors limiting judicial discretion (3 Strikes laws, drug minimums).

**CC-032 — Legal Ethics and Professional Responsibility**
Model Rules of Professional Conduct (MRPC, ABA): govern attorney behavior. Confidentiality (Rule 1.6): attorney must not reveal client information without client consent; exceptions include preventing future crime likely to result in death or substantial bodily harm. Conflict of interest (Rule 1.7): attorney may not represent a client if the representation involves a concurrent conflict of interest unless both clients give informed written consent. Candor to the tribunal (Rule 3.3): attorney must not make false statements of law or fact to the court. Competence (Rule 1.1): must provide competent representation including legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation. Privilege: attorney-client privilege (protects confidential communications for legal advice) vs. work product doctrine (protects materials prepared in anticipation of litigation).

**CC-033 — Family Law**
Marriage: civil contract governed by state law; common-law marriage recognized in ~10 states. Divorce: no-fault divorce available in all states (irreconcilable differences); fault-based grounds still relevant in some states for property distribution and alimony. Equitable distribution (majority) vs. community property (9 states: California, Texas, etc.) for marital assets. Child custody: best interests of the child standard (factors: parent-child relationships, stability, history of abuse, child's preference depending on age). Child support: state guidelines based on income shares or percentage-of-income models; modifiable upon substantial change in circumstances. Adoption: terminates parental rights of birth parents; creates parent-child relationship; open vs. closed adoption.

**CC-034 — Legal Reasoning and Statutory Interpretation**
Canons of statutory interpretation: (1) plain meaning — if the statutory text is clear, courts apply it as written (textualism); (2) legislative history — use committee reports, floor statements to discern intent (intentionalism); (3) purposivism — interpret statutes in light of their broader purpose. Specific canons: expressio unius (expression of one thing excludes others); ejusdem generis (general term limited to items of same category as specific terms); noscitur a sociis (word known by its company); rule against surplusage (every word has meaning); the rule of lenity (criminal statutes construed narrowly in defendant's favor). Stare decisis: courts follow precedent; SCOTUS may overrule its own precedent (Dobbs overruled Roe; Loper Bright overruled Chevron).

## 5. Patterns

**P-001 — IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion)**
The universal framework for legal analysis: Issue (what is the legal question?), Rule (what law governs this issue — statute, regulation, common law, constitution?), Application (how does the rule apply to the specific facts?), Conclusion (what is the answer given the rule applied to the facts?). IRAC structures all legal writing (memos, briefs, exam answers). Variations: CREAC (Conclusion, Rule, Explanation, Application, Conclusion) used in brief writing; MIRAT (material facts, issue, rule, argument, conclusion) in some jurisdictions.

**P-002 — Distinguishing Binding and Persuasive Authority**
Courts must follow binding (mandatory) precedent from higher courts in the same jurisdiction; they may consider but are not bound by persuasive authority (same-level court decisions, other jurisdictions' decisions, law review articles, Restatements). When a precedent is inconvenient, counsel distinguishes it: argue the facts of the present case are materially different from the precedent case in a way that makes the precedent's holding inapplicable. A lawyer's primary skill is applying, distinguishing, and arguing for extension or limitation of precedent.

**P-003 — Strict vs. Broad vs. Narrow Construction**
Strict construction: limit the law's scope to its precise terms (especially in criminal law — rule of lenity; in derogation of common law). Broad/liberal construction: interpret generously in light of purpose (remedial statutes; civil rights laws). Narrow construction: limit to core application. The choice of interpretive method is not neutral — it often determines the outcome. Courts signal their interpretive approach through textual choices and by citing or ignoring legislative history and purpose.

**P-004 — The "Parade of Horribles" Argument**
A common reductio ad absurdum in legal argument: if this legal rule is adopted, it will lead to extreme and unacceptable outcomes. Used to attack broad rules and expansive interpretations. Counter-strategy: identify limiting principles that prevent the parade of horribles while permitting the desired outcome; argue that the limiting principle is workable and the parade is speculative. Courts frequently articulate limiting principles in their opinions precisely to foreclose the parade of horribles in future cases.

**P-005 — Balancing Tests in Constitutional Law**
Many constitutional questions are resolved through multi-factor balancing tests. Mathews v. Eldridge (procedural due process): balance private interest, risk of erroneous deprivation, and governmental interest. First Amendment balancing: government interest vs. speech restriction. Fourth Amendment reasonableness: totality-of-the-circumstances balancing. Intermediate scrutiny (sex discrimination): substantially related to important interest. Balancing tests provide flexibility at the cost of predictability; categorical rules provide clarity at the cost of occasional injustice.

**P-006 — Elements Analysis in Civil and Criminal Claims**
Every claim (civil or criminal) is broken into elements, each of which must be established to the applicable standard of proof. Civil: preponderance of evidence (more likely than not, > 50%). Criminal: beyond a reasonable doubt. Intermediate: clear and convincing evidence (civil commitment, fraud, parental rights termination). When analyzing whether a claim is established, go element-by-element: if any element is missing, the claim fails entirely (unless there are alternative theories). Reversing a jury verdict requires finding that no reasonable jury could have found an element established — a very high bar.

**P-007 — Purposive vs. Textual Interpretation**
The tension between "what the words say" (textualism) and "what the legislature intended" (intentionalism/purposivism) is the central methodological divide in statutory interpretation. Scalia's textualism: the text is the law; legislative history is an unreliable guide to meaning because only the text is voted on. Breyer's purposivism: statutes are instruments of social policy; courts should interpret them to further their purposes. In practice, courts often use both and the choice between them influences outcomes in close cases. Textualism dominates post-Scalia SCOTUS.

**P-008 — Contract Interpretation: Textualism vs. Contextualism**
Textualism (four corners rule): contract meaning determined by the plain meaning of the written words, within the four corners of the document (PEP — parol evidence rule: prior or contemporaneous oral agreements not admissible to contradict a fully integrated written contract). Contextualism (Corbin): all surrounding circumstances — trade usage, course of dealing, course of performance — admissible to determine the meaning of apparently unambiguous terms. The UCC incorporates context heavily (trade usage, course of dealing, course of performance as hierarchy under UCC 1-303). California applies contextualism broadly; New York applies a more textualist approach.

**P-009 — Proximate Cause Analysis**
Cause-in-fact (but-for causation): the harm would not have occurred but for the defendant's negligent act. Proximate cause (legal cause): limits liability to reasonably foreseeable consequences of the negligent act. The scope of liability (Restatement Third): an actor is liable for harms within the scope of risk that made the actor's conduct negligent. Superseding cause: an unforeseeable intervening act breaks the chain of causation, relieving the original defendant of liability. Thin skull rule (eggshell plaintiff): defendant takes the plaintiff as found — if the plaintiff has an unusual vulnerability, the defendant is liable for the full extent of harm even if unforeseeable.

**P-010 — Hierarchy of Authority in Legal Research**
Primary authority: constitutions, statutes, regulations, cases, treaties. Secondary authority: law review articles, treatises, Restatements (highly persuasive; not binding), practice guides, ALR. When researching a legal issue: (1) identify the jurisdiction (which state? federal?); (2) identify the level of court; (3) find primary binding authority; (4) supplement with persuasive authority. The Restatements (First, Second, Third) of Torts, Contracts, Agency, Property, etc. are authoritative summaries of common law principles — not law, but highly persuasive in jurisdictions without controlling precedent.

**P-011 — Offer and Acceptance Analysis**
Step 1: Was there an offer? (definite terms; communicated intent to be bound; objective manifestation). Step 2: Was the offer still open? (no revocation, rejection, counteroffer, lapse, death). Step 3: Was there acceptance? (common law: mirror image; UCC: anything expressing definite acceptance; unilateral contracts: performance constitutes acceptance). Step 4: Was acceptance communicated? (mailbox rule: acceptance effective when dispatched for mailed acceptance; instantaneous communication: effective when received). Step 5: Was there consideration? (legal detriment on each side; pre-existing duty rule bars consideration for doing what you're already legally obligated to do).

**P-012 — International Law: Sovereignty and Non-Intervention**
Westphalian sovereignty (1648): the cornerstone of international relations — states have exclusive internal jurisdiction and the right to non-interference by other states in their internal affairs. Tension with: humanitarian intervention (R2P — Responsibility to Protect; UN Security Council authorization required; ICISS 2001); universal jurisdiction for jus cogens violations (torture, genocide — national courts may exercise jurisdiction regardless of where crimes occurred or nationality of perpetrators); international human rights law (states' obligations to their own citizens); economic globalization (eroding de facto sovereignty through trade/investment treaty obligations).

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP-001 — Confusing Legal and Moral Rights**
Legal rights are those recognized by a legal system and enforceable by courts. Moral rights are claims people have independent of legal recognition. Many moral rights are not legal rights (no legal right not to be insulted). Some legal rights are morally contested (the legal right to own a gun; the legal right to inherit wealth). The relationship between law and morality is complex and debated by jurisprudents; conflating them leads to circular reasoning ("it's legal, therefore it's right" or "it's wrong, therefore it must be illegal").

**AP-002 — Generalization from One Jurisdiction**
"The law says X" without specifying jurisdiction is usually uninformative. US federal law ≠ California law ≠ Texas law ≠ UK law ≠ EU law ≠ Japanese law. Employment at-will is a US doctrine not recognized in the EU. US copyright protects works for life + 70 years; Canada protects life + 50 years. Marriage equality was constitutionalized by the US Supreme Court in 2015 but has varying legal status worldwide. Legal advice requires jurisdiction-specific knowledge; cross-jurisdictional generalizations are typically too imprecise to be actionable.

**AP-003 — Treating Dictum as Holding**
The holding of a case: the court's decision on the specific legal question presented by the facts — binding precedent. Dictum (obiter dictum): statements in the opinion about legal questions not directly before the court, or about hypothetical situations — persuasive but not binding. Lawyers overread cases by treating broad dictum as holding. A court that holds "discrimination based on sexual orientation violates Title VII" (Bostock v. Clayton County, 2020) may have dicta on adjacent questions that do not constitute binding law.

**AP-004 — The Naturalistic Fallacy in Legal Argument**
Arguing that something is legal because it is "natural" or that something should be legal because it has historically been done. Tradition alone is not legal authority (overruled cases were once law; slavery was once legal). Natural law arguments in courts require more than appeals to nature or tradition — they require engagement with constitutional text, structural analysis, precedent, and sometimes moral philosophy as legal argument. Courts in constitutional adjudication increasingly require textual, historical, and structural support.

**AP-005 — Mistake of Law Defense**
Under common law and the MPC, "ignorance of the law is no excuse" — a defendant's genuine mistake about whether conduct is legal does not negate mens rea for most crimes (contrast with mistake of fact, which can negate mens rea). Limited exception (MPC §2.04): if the statute/regulation is not published or was not reasonably made available, or if the actor reasonably relies on an official statement of law later determined to be erroneous. This doctrine puts citizens at risk for laws they have no practical way of knowing — a legitimate critique of the rule in an era of regulatory complexity.

**AP-006 — Overreliance on Contract Boilerplate**
Standard form contracts (contracts of adhesion) are drafted by the drafter-party to serve their interests; many clauses are unenforceable (unconscionable terms, limitation-of-liability clauses for gross negligence, clauses that violate statutory rights). "This contract is governed by the laws of [state/country]" does not make that choice enforceable in all circumstances (consumer protection laws of the consumer's jurisdiction may override). Broad arbitration clauses and class action waivers are increasingly challenged in consumer contracts. Treating standard-form boilerplate as legally certain protection overestimates its efficacy.

**AP-007 — Confusing Civil and Criminal Liability**
The same act can give rise to both civil liability (tortious conduct → private party sues for damages, preponderance standard) and criminal liability (criminal conduct → government prosecutes, beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard) or neither or either. O.J. Simpson: acquitted of criminal murder (reasonable doubt) but found civilly liable for wrongful death (preponderance). Criminal acquittal does not preclude civil liability. Different parties, different standards, different remedies, different purposes (punishment vs. compensation). Civil RICO is distinct from criminal RICO prosecution; RICO's civil treble damages provision creates liability based on different elements than criminal RICO.

**AP-008 — Assuming Absence of Regulation Means Permissibility**
The absence of a specific prohibition does not mean an activity is legally permissible. General regulatory frameworks (FTC Act §5 unfair or deceptive practices), common law duties (negligence), and constitutional provisions may reach conduct not specifically addressed by statute. "We're just a platform, not a publisher" (Section 230 immunity) does not insulate platforms from all liability (IP claims, federal criminal content, FOSTA-SESTA). New technologies frequently involve activities not specifically addressed by existing law; courts and regulators fill gaps via analogy and general principles.

**AP-009 — Conflating Standard of Review with Standard of Proof**
Standard of proof: the burden at trial — preponderance (civil), beyond a reasonable doubt (criminal), clear and convincing (intermediate). Standard of review: how an appellate court evaluates the lower court's decision. De novo (legal conclusions): appellate court makes its own independent determination. Clear error (factual findings): affirm unless the lower court's finding is clearly erroneous. Abuse of discretion (discretionary decisions): reversed only if unreasonable exercise of discretion. Substantial evidence (agency findings): more than a scintilla; relevant evidence a reasonable person might accept. Confusing these standards leads to incorrect predictions about which party bears the burden and how strong that burden is.

## 7. Facts

**F-001** — Hammurabi's Code (~1754 BCE): one of the oldest written legal codes; 282 laws carved on a 7.5-foot stele (now in the Louvre); covers property, commerce, family, and criminal law; introduced the lex talionis principle ("an eye for an eye") as a proportionality constraint on punishment — a limitation rather than a savage prescription; recognized social hierarchy (different punishments for different classes).

**F-002** — Magna Carta (1215): King John of England's charter acknowledging certain rights and limiting royal power; clauses establishing no imprisonment without lawful judgment of peers (habeas corpus precursor) and no taxation without consent; most clauses were quickly suspended; its symbolic importance grew over centuries; it was repeatedly reissued and cited in English law through the 17th century and by American colonists challenging British taxation.

**F-003** — The US Supreme Court has 9 justices (not fixed by the Constitution — Congress set the current number in 1869); the Chief Justice presides over the court and the Senate impeachment trial; the Senate confirms justices by majority vote after the 2017 filibuster reform; SCOTUS decides approximately 60–70 cases per term (October–June) from ~7,000–8,000 cert petitions; deciding cert: "Rule of Four" — 4 justices must vote to grant certiorari.

**F-004** — Miranda v. Arizona (1966): SCOTUS held that the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination and the Sixth Amendment right to counsel require police to inform suspects in custody of their rights before interrogation — the "Miranda warning"; prior to Miranda, police routinely interrogated suspects without warnings and obtained confessions; Miranda's practical effect on confession rates has been debated empirically.

**F-005** — Brown v. Board of Education (1954): SCOTUS held that racial segregation in public schools violated the Equal Protection Clause (14th Amendment); overruled Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) which had upheld "separate but equal" facilities; unanimous opinion authored by Chief Justice Warren; famously relied on social science evidence (Kenneth Clark's doll studies) on the psychological harm of segregation — controversial use of non-legal evidence.

**F-006** — Roe v. Wade (1973): SCOTUS held that the Constitution protected a woman's right to abortion under the Due Process Clause (right to privacy); established a trimester framework. Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022): SCOTUS overruled Roe and Planned Parenthood v. Casey (1992), holding that the Constitution does not confer a right to abortion; returned the issue to state legislatures; the most consequential overruling of precedent in decades.

**F-007** — The New York Convention on Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards (1958) is the foundational instrument of international commercial arbitration; approximately 170 countries are parties; it obligates courts to recognize and enforce foreign arbitral awards subject to narrow exceptions (public policy, procedural irregularity, invalid arbitration agreement); it transformed international commercial dispute resolution by providing a predictable enforcement mechanism for arbitral awards across jurisdictions.

**F-008** — The Nuremberg Trials (1945–1946): International Military Tribunal (US, UK, France, USSR) prosecuted 24 Nazi leaders for crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity; Nuremberg established individual criminal responsibility under international law (not just state responsibility); established that superior orders do not excuse international crimes; laid the foundation for international humanitarian law and eventually the ICC.

**F-009** — The Model Penal Code (MPC, ALI 1962): the most influential single document in US criminal law reform; developed by Herbert Wechsler; adopted in whole or substantial part by ~37 states; introduced the four levels of mens rea (purpose, knowledge, recklessness, negligence), the substantial-capacity insanity test, and a comprehensive framework for inchoate crimes, complicity, and defenses; not itself law, but a model that state legislatures adopt.

**F-010** — The Uniform Commercial Code (UCC): a collaborative effort of the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws (NCCUSL) and the American Law Institute (ALI); first published 1952; adopted in all 50 states (Louisiana adopted most articles except Article 2 sale of goods); Articles cover sales (Art. 2), leases (2A), commercial paper (3), bank deposits (4), letters of credit (5), bulk sales (6 — now withdrawn), warehouse receipts/documents (7), investment securities (8), secured transactions (9).

**F-011** — Stare decisis horizontal vs. vertical: Vertical stare decisis (strong): lower courts must follow binding precedent of higher courts in their jurisdiction; near-absolute rule. Horizontal stare decisis (weak): courts are not bound to follow their own prior decisions but generally do for predictability/reliance; SCOTUS overrules its own precedent infrequently but significantly (Roe/Dobbs; Plessy/Brown; Chevron/Loper Bright; Lawrence v. Texas overruling Bowers v. Hardwick on same-sex intimacy). The doctrine of stare decisis does not apply to constitutional interpretation the same way as to statutory interpretation (correcting constitutional errors cannot await legislative action).

**F-012** — Marbury v. Madison (1803): Chief Justice Marshall's opinion established judicial review — the power of federal courts to declare Acts of Congress unconstitutional — despite the Constitution's silence on this power; Marshall cleverly found that the Judiciary Act of 1789 conflicted with Article III and invalidated Section 13; while ruling against Marbury's appointment, Marshall claimed enormous power for the judiciary; without judicial review, the Constitution would be unenforceable against Congress.

**F-013** — Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad (1928): a leading US torts case on proximate cause and duty; Judge Cardozo (majority): railroad owed no duty to Mrs. Palsgraf because the harm was not foreseeable given the negligence toward the fireworks-carrying passenger; Judge Andrews (dissent): once negligence is established, the defendant is liable to any foreseeable or even unforeseeable plaintiff harmed in the chain of causation; the debate between Cardozo and Andrews remains central to tort theory.

**F-014** — The Treaty of Westphalia (1648): ended the Thirty Years' War and Eighty Years' War; established the Westphalian system of sovereign nation-states; recognized religious toleration; created the framework of modern international law (non-interference in domestic affairs, sovereign equality of states); its direct legal provisions were largely superseded but its conceptual framework shaped international law for nearly 400 years.

**F-015** — The TRIPS Agreement (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights, 1994): part of the WTO agreements; established minimum IP protection standards for all WTO members; required all members to provide 20-year patent protection, life + 50 years copyright protection, trademark protection; the TRIPS flexibilities (compulsory licensing, parallel imports) are used by developing countries to access generic medicines (Doha Declaration, 2001 — TRIPS does not prevent countries from protecting public health).

**F-016** — Citizens United v. FEC (2010): SCOTUS held that political spending by corporations, associations, and labor unions is a form of protected speech under the First Amendment and cannot be limited by law; overruled Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce (1990) and McConnell v. FEC (2003) in part; led to the rise of Super PACs (independent expenditure committees that can raise unlimited funds); 5-4 decision with one of the most significant dissents in recent SCOTUS history (Stevens).

**F-017** — The ICC (International Criminal Court, Rome Statute 1998, in force 2002): the first permanent international criminal court; has jurisdiction over genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression for nationals of or acts committed in territory of states parties; 124 state parties (US, China, Russia, India are not parties); issued arrest warrant for Russian President Putin in 2023 for war crimes (deportation of Ukrainian children); limited enforcement power (relies on states to execute arrest warrants).

**F-018** — The Restatement (Second) of Contracts (1981): the ALI's comprehensive restatement of US contract common law; not itself law, but highly influential in courts; ~800 sections; the controversial §90 on promissory estoppel, §208 on unconscionability (gross unfairness shock the conscience), and §261–272 on impracticability and frustration have been particularly influential in expanding contract law beyond classical boundaries.

**F-019** — The statute of frauds (English Statute of Frauds, 1677; US versions in state statutes): requires certain contracts to be in writing (signed by party to be charged) to be enforceable: contracts for the sale of land or an interest in land; contracts that cannot be performed within one year; contracts for sale of goods ≥ $500 (UCC 2-201); promises to pay another's debt (suretyship); promises to pay a decedent's debts; promises in consideration of marriage (prenuptial agreements). Doctrine developed to prevent perjury in high-stakes transactions — though it also allows parties to escape genuine oral contracts by invoking the statute.

**F-020** — Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins (1938): overruled Swift v. Tyson (1842); held that federal courts sitting in diversity jurisdiction must apply state substantive law, not a general federal common law; eliminated the "general common law" that had created systematic forum shopping between state and federal courts; created the distinction between substantive law (state) and procedural law (federal — FRCP) that still governs federal diversity jurisdiction today.

**F-021** — The UN Security Council: 5 permanent members (P5: US, UK, France, Russia, China) with veto power; 10 non-permanent members elected for 2-year terms; Chapter VII resolutions (binding on all UN members: sanctions, authorized use of force) require 9 votes and no P5 veto; the veto has been exercised ~300 times since 1945 (most by USSR/Russia); the veto's existence reflects the political compromise necessary to bring the great powers into the UN system after WWII.

**F-022** — The Paris Agreement (2015): adopted by 197 parties; requires nationally determined contributions (NDCs) to reduce GHG emissions; 1.5°C and well below 2°C temperature goals; ratchet mechanism: NDCs revised upward every 5 years; no binding enforcement (countries cannot be sanctioned for failing to meet their NDC); the compliance mechanism is transparency-based. The US withdrew under Trump (2017), rejoined under Biden (2021). Legal form: a treaty under international law; US did not submit to Senate (required for 2/3 vote for ratification of a "treaty") but treated as an executive agreement.

**F-023** — The concept of "precedent" in common law dates to English common law development in the medieval period; the principle of stare decisis (stand by decisions) became firmly established in English courts in the 17th–18th centuries; the creation of systematic law reports (beginning with Plowden's Reports, 1571; Coke's Reports, 1600–1615) made precedent practically possible by making judgments available and citable; West's National Reporter System (1876) enabled systematic use of precedent across all US states and federal circuits.

**F-024** — Hadley v. Baxendale (1854): an English contract case establishing the rule that consequential damages are recoverable only if they were reasonably foreseeable at the time of contracting (or the defendant was specifically informed of them); Hadley's mill was closed for a week longer than expected because the carrier delayed returning a crankshaft — the court denied lost profit damages because the carrier didn't know the mill was closed; the rule has governed consequential damages in contract law worldwide for 170 years.

**F-025** — Constitutional interpretation: Antonin Scalia and Clarence Thomas are the most prominent advocates of textualism/originalism; Hugo Black was the strongest original textualist on the mid-20th century court ("Congress shall make no law... means no law"); the living constitutionalism vs. originalism debate has shaped every major constitutional controversy of the past 30 years; the "original public meaning" variant of originalism (what the words meant to a reasonable reader at ratification) is distinct from original intent originalism (what the framers intended).

**F-026** — The APA (Administrative Procedure Act, 1946): enacted to standardize and constrain the rapidly growing administrative state created by the New Deal; provides the legal framework for federal agency rulemaking, adjudication, and judicial review; its judicial review standards — "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law" (§706) — have been interpreted broadly by courts; the APA is the Magna Carta of administrative law and is litigated in nearly every significant regulatory challenge.

**F-027** — Legal aid and access to justice: in civil matters, there is no constitutional right to counsel in the US (Gideon v. Wainwright applies only to criminal cases where imprisonment is possible); approximately 70–80% of the civil legal needs of low-income Americans go unmet; the Legal Services Corporation (federal, since 1974) funds civil legal aid but is chronically underfunded; "justice gap" refers to the unmet civil legal needs of low-income populations; mandatory pro bono requirements exist in some jurisdictions.

**F-028** — Internet and speech: Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (1996): a platform "shall not be treated as the publisher or speaker" of content provided by third parties — near-absolute immunity for user-generated content; without Section 230, content moderation would expose platforms to massive liability (for moderating or not moderating); Section 230 is the legal bedrock of the modern internet; its scope is contested and both Democrats (too little moderation) and Republicans (too much moderation) have sought reform.

**F-029** — The Eighth Amendment cruel and unusual punishment jurisprudence: Trop v. Dulles (1958): evolving standards of decency; Furman v. Georgia (1972): death penalty as applied was unconstitutional (suspended until Gregg v. Georgia 1976); Atkins v. Virginia (2002): executing intellectually disabled defendants is unconstitutional; Roper v. Simmons (2005): executing juveniles under 18 at time of offense is unconstitutional; Kennedy v. Louisiana (2008): death penalty for child rape (non-homicide) is unconstitutional.

**F-030** — Corporate governance: the business judgment rule (BJR): courts defer to directors' good-faith business decisions; directors are not liable for honest mistakes, only for bad faith, conflicts of interest, or gross negligence; the BJR prevents judicial second-guessing of business strategy. Duty of loyalty cases (Delaware): the entire fairness standard applies when a director is on both sides of a transaction — the transaction must be entirely fair (fair dealing + fair price); shareholders may approve interested transactions through majority-of-minority vote under Weinberger v. UOP (1983).

**F-031** — The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA, 1998): implements WIPO Internet Treaties; creates safe harbor from copyright liability for online service providers (§512) for user-uploaded infringing content if: (1) they are passive intermediaries; (2) they expeditiously remove infringing material upon notice (DMCA takedown); (3) they terminate repeat infringers. The safe harbor is critical to YouTube, Facebook, Twitter/X, and essentially all user-generated content platforms; controversially, fair use applies to whether infringing content was uploaded, not to whether a takedown request is sent — automated over-takedown is a structural problem.

**F-032** — International Humanitarian Law (IHL, laws of armed conflict): Hague Conventions (1899/1907) codified rules of warfare (means and methods of warfare); Geneva Conventions (1949, 4 conventions + 3 Additional Protocols): protect non-combatants, POWs, wounded/sick; key principles: distinction (combatants vs. civilians), proportionality (incidental civilian harm must not be excessive relative to military advantage), military necessity (no more force than necessary), precaution (take feasible precautions to minimize civilian harm). The ICRC (International Committee of the Red Cross) is the guardian of IHL; IHL applies equally to all parties to an armed conflict.

**F-033** — Antitrust: Standard Oil Co. v. United States (1911) — SCOTUS held Standard Oil (Rockefeller) had illegally monopolized the petroleum industry and ordered its breakup into 34 companies (including ExxonMobil, Chevron, Amoco); the rule of reason standard (vs. per se illegality) was articulated; United States v. Microsoft (2000) — DOJ found Microsoft had illegally maintained its monopoly in PC operating systems through exclusionary contracts; ordered breakup overturned on appeal; settled with behavioral remedies; United States v. Google (2024) — found Google illegally monopolized the general search market through default agreements.

**F-034** — The Geneva Convention relative to the Treatment of Prisoners of War (Third Geneva Convention, 1949): POWs are entitled to: humane treatment; name, rank, date of birth, service number only required in interrogation; protection from torture, humiliation, and coercion; repatriation after active hostilities; visits by ICRC. The US "enhanced interrogation techniques" controversy (post-9/11) centered on whether detainees were entitled to POW protections and whether waterboarding, stress positions, sleep deprivation constitute torture under the UN Convention Against Torture and the Geneva Conventions.

**F-035** — Class action lawsuits: FRCP Rule 23 requirements: numerosity (too many to join individually), commonality (common questions of law or fact), typicality (representative's claims typical), adequate representation. Types: (b)(1) limited fund/inconsistent judgments risk; (b)(2) injunctive/declaratory relief; (b)(3) common questions predominate and class action is superior method (opt-out class; notice required). Significant class actions: Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement (1998, $246B); EEOC v. Walmart sex discrimination settlement; opioid class actions ($26B with major distributors and manufacturers). AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion (2011): FAA preempts state laws invalidating class action waivers in arbitration agreements — effectively eliminating consumer class actions in many consumer contracts.

**F-036** — The EU Court of Justice (CJEU): the supreme court of EU law; headquartered in Luxembourg; hears: direct actions against EU institutions, infringement proceedings against member states, preliminary references (national courts ask the CJEU to interpret EU law); the doctrine of direct effect (Van Gend en Loos, 1963) — EU law creates rights directly enforceable by individuals in national courts; supremacy of EU law (Costa v. ENEL, 1964) — EU law prevails over conflicting national law; these doctrines transformed EU law from a treaty among states to a constitutional legal order.

**F-037** — The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA, 1966, revised 1974, 1996, 2007): any person may request access to federal agency records; nine exemptions (classified national security; internal personnel rules; commercial/trade secrets; inter-agency memoranda under deliberative process privilege; privacy; law enforcement records; financial regulators; geological data); agencies must respond within 20 business days; backlogs often extend to years for complex requests; used extensively by journalists, researchers, and litigants; state equivalents (sunshine laws) cover state agencies.

**F-038** — Intellectual property and the public domain: copyright protection expires, placing works in the public domain — freely usable by anyone. Works published in the US before 1928 are in the public domain. The Sonny Bono CTEA (1998) extended terms retroactively by 20 years, delaying entry of 1920s works (including Mickey Mouse) into the public domain. The first Mickey Mouse cartoon (Steamboat Willie, 1928) entered the US public domain on January 1, 2024. Works can also be dedicated to the public domain by their authors (Creative Commons CC0; the GNU GPL is not a public domain dedication but a share-alike copyright license).
