## 4. Core Concepts

**CC-001 — Time Value of Money**
A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future because of: opportunity cost (today's dollar can be invested), inflation (purchasing power declines), and risk (future payments are uncertain). Future value: FV = PV × (1 + r)ⁿ. Present value: PV = FV / (1 + r)ⁿ. Net present value (NPV): NPV = Σ[CF_t / (1+r)^t] − C₀; positive NPV = value-creating investment. Perpetuity: PV = CF/r. Growing perpetuity: PV = CF₁/(r−g). Annuity: PV = CF × [1 − (1+r)^{−n}]/r. Continuous compounding: FV = PV × e^{rt}. The discount rate r reflects the opportunity cost of capital and the riskiness of the cash flows — the central parameter in all valuation.

**CC-002 — Return and Risk Definitions**
Arithmetic return: R = (P₁ − P₀ + D)/P₀ (capital gain + dividend yield). Geometric (compound) return: (1+R_geo) = [(1+R₁)(1+R₂)...(1+Rₙ)]^{1/n}; geometric < arithmetic for volatile series. Log return: r = ln(P₁/P₀); additively sum over time; used in continuous-time models. Expected return: E[R] = Σ p_i R_i. Variance of return: σ² = E[(R − E[R])²]. Covariance: Cov(Rᵢ, Rⱼ) = E[(Rᵢ − μᵢ)(Rⱼ − μⱼ)]. Correlation: ρᵢⱼ = Cov(Rᵢ,Rⱼ)/(σᵢσⱼ) ∈ [−1, 1]. Sharpe ratio: SR = (R_p − R_f)/σ_p (excess return per unit of total risk — the standard performance measure).

**CC-003 — Modern Portfolio Theory (Markowitz, 1952)**
The expected return of a portfolio is a linear combination of constituent asset returns: E[R_p] = Σ wᵢ E[Rᵢ]. Portfolio variance: σ²_p = Σᵢ Σⱼ wᵢ wⱼ σᵢσⱼ ρᵢⱼ = wᵀΣw (Σ = covariance matrix). Diversification: adding uncorrelated assets reduces portfolio variance without reducing expected return; in the limit (many assets, zero correlations), idiosyncratic risk is eliminated. The efficient frontier: the set of portfolios that maximize expected return for a given variance (or minimize variance for a given return); optimal risky portfolio: the tangency portfolio on the efficient frontier (highest Sharpe ratio); the Two-Fund Separation theorem: all investors hold a combination of the risk-free asset and the tangency portfolio.

**CC-004 — Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)**
Sharpe-Lintner CAPM (1964, 1965): in equilibrium, all investors hold the market portfolio; the expected return on any asset is: E[Rᵢ] = Rₓ + βᵢ(E[R_M] − Rₓ), where βᵢ = Cov(Rᵢ, R_M)/Var(R_M) measures systematic risk. Market risk premium (MRP) = E[R_M] − Rₓ ≈ 5–7% historically in the US. Beta: βᵢ > 1 (more volatile than market); βᵢ < 1 (less volatile); βᵢ < 0 (negatively correlated with market — e.g., gold in some periods). Security Market Line (SML): assets on the SML are correctly priced; above SML = underpriced (positive alpha); below SML = overpriced (negative alpha). CAPM limitations: many empirical anomalies (size, value, momentum effects); market portfolio is not observable.

**CC-005 — Factor Models (Fama-French, Multi-Factor)**
The CAPM's single risk factor (market beta) is empirically inadequate. Fama-French Three-Factor Model (1992, 1993): R_i − Rₓ = αᵢ + β_M (R_M − Rₓ) + β_SMB × SMB + β_HML × HML + εᵢ. SMB (Small Minus Big): small-cap stocks outperform large-cap historically (~3% annualized since 1927). HML (High Minus Low): value stocks (high book-to-market) outperform growth stocks historically (~5% annualized). Carhart (1997) added Momentum (MOM = winners minus losers, trailing 12-1 month return). Fama-French Five-Factor Model (2015): adds RMW (Robust Minus Weak profitability) and CMA (Conservative Minus Aggressive investment). Factor investing ("smart beta"): construct portfolios tilted toward factors with empirical track records.

**CC-006 — Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)**
Fama (1970): stock prices reflect all available information. Three forms: Weak-form efficiency: prices fully reflect all past trading information (technical analysis is useless for generating alpha). Semi-strong efficiency: prices fully reflect all publicly available information (fundamental analysis cannot generate persistent alpha). Strong efficiency: prices reflect all information including private (insider information cannot generate alpha — an extreme claim). EMH implies: no abnormal profits from public information without abnormal risk; price changes are unpredictable (random walk); actively managed funds cannot consistently beat passively managed index funds. Counter-evidence: momentum, value premium, earnings announcement drift (PEAD), January effect — "anomalies" that persist but are partly explained by risk.

**CC-007 — Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Valuation**
The intrinsic value of an asset = PV of all future expected cash flows discounted at the appropriate risk-adjusted rate. For a firm: V_firm = Σ[FCFF_t / (1+WACC)^t] + TV/(1+WACC)^n. FCFF (Free Cash Flow to Firm) = EBIT(1−tax) + D&A − ΔWorking Capital − CapEx. Terminal value (TV): Gordon Growth Model TV = FCFF_{n+1}/(WACC − g) where g = perpetual growth rate (typically nominal GDP growth, ~2–3%). WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital) = w_D × r_D × (1−T) + w_E × r_E. DCF is highly sensitive to the discount rate and terminal growth rate — small changes produce large value differences. Reverse DCF: infer the growth rate implied by the current price and evaluate whether it is plausible.

**CC-008 — Equity Multiples and Relative Valuation**
Relative valuation compares a company's multiples to those of comparable firms (comps). Price/Earnings (P/E): market price / EPS; reflects growth expectations and risk; trailing (last 12 months) vs. forward (next 12 months). EV/EBITDA: enterprise value / EBITDA; capital-structure-neutral; preferred for comparing firms with different leverage; typical ranges: consumer staples 12–16×, technology 20–30×, utilities 8–12×. Price/Book (P/B): market cap / book value; useful for financial institutions; P/B < 1 suggests market expects ROE < cost of equity. EV/Revenue: useful for unprofitable growth companies. Limitations: comps may not be truly comparable; multiples capture relative value but not absolute value; cyclicality distorts earnings-based multiples at cycle extremes.

**CC-009 — Dividend Discount Models**
Gordon Growth Model (constant growth): P₀ = D₁/(r − g); r = D₁/P₀ + g (dividend yield + growth = required return). H-Model: two-stage model with a linear growth transition. Multi-stage DDM: explicit forecast period (5–10 years) + terminal value using GGM. Dividend vs. total return: firms that reinvest at ROE > cost of equity create more value than those that pay dividends (Modigliani-Miller dividend irrelevance theorem); firms with ROE < cost of equity should pay dividends rather than reinvest. Buyback yield: share repurchases are economically equivalent to dividends (all else equal); total shareholder yield = dividend yield + buyback yield; reduces share count, increasing EPS and DPS.

**CC-010 — Bond Pricing and Yield**
Bond price = PV of coupons + PV of par value: P = Σ[C/(1+y)^t] + F/(1+y)^n (y = yield to maturity). At par: coupon rate = yield. At discount: coupon rate < yield. At premium: coupon rate > yield. Yield to maturity (YTM): the IRR of the bond's cash flows at the current price — the promised return if held to maturity and no default. Yield curve: plot of yields vs. maturity; normal (upward sloping — longer maturity = higher yield for risk and liquidity), inverted (short yields > long yields — historically precedes recession), flat. Yield spread: difference between bond yield and benchmark (UST); credit spread reflects default and liquidity risk.

**CC-011 — Duration and Convexity**
Modified duration: D_mod = − (1/P) × dP/dy ≈ ΔP/P ≈ −D_mod × Δy; a bond with duration 5 years loses approximately 5% in price for each 100 bps increase in yield. Macaulay duration: weighted average time to receive cash flows (weights = PV of cash flows / price); zero-coupon bond duration = maturity. Convexity: the second-order term; ΔP/P ≈ −D_mod × Δy + ½ × Convexity × (Δy)²; a more convex bond is more desirable (gains more when yields fall, loses less when yields rise). Duration-matching (immunization): match the duration of assets and liabilities to create a portfolio whose value change is approximately zero for small parallel yield curve shifts.

**CC-012 — Credit Risk**
Default probability: estimated from historical data, structural models (Merton model), or CDS spreads. Merton model (1974): equity = call option on firm assets; debt = risk-free bond − put option; derives probability of default from firm value, volatility, and leverage. Credit ratings: Moody's, S&P, Fitch; investment grade (Aaa/AAA through Baa3/BBB−); speculative grade (below Baa3/BBB−, "junk"); 10-year cumulative default rates: Aaa ~0.1%, Ba ~15%, B ~28%, Caa-C ~50%+. Recovery rate: the fraction of par value recovered after default; depends on seniority (secured > senior unsecured > subordinated > equity); average recovery for senior unsecured corporate debt ~40%. Credit default swap (CDS): buyer pays periodic premium; receives par − recovery value if a credit event occurs (default, restructuring).

**CC-013 — Black-Scholes-Merton Option Pricing**
Call option value: C = S₀N(d₁) − Ke^{−rT}N(d₂), where d₁ = [ln(S₀/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T); d₂ = d₁ − σ√T. Put option value: P = Ke^{−rT}N(−d₂) − S₀N(−d₁). Put-call parity: C − P = S₀ − Ke^{−rT} (no-arbitrage). Key inputs: S₀ (spot price), K (strike), r (risk-free rate), T (time to expiration), σ (volatility — the only unobservable input; implied from market prices). Assumptions: no dividends, continuous trading, no transaction costs, geometric Brownian motion (constant σ, log-normal returns). Limitations: volatility is not constant (volatility smile/skew — implied σ varies with strike and maturity); fat tails; jump risk. BSM earned Scholes and Merton the 1997 Nobel Prize.

**CC-014 — Option Greeks**
Delta (Δ): ∂C/∂S — change in option price per $1 change in stock price; call delta ∈ (0,1); put delta ∈ (−1,0); at-the-money option delta ≈ 0.5. Gamma (Γ): ∂²C/∂S² = ∂Δ/∂S — change in delta per $1 change in stock price; highest at-the-money, decreasing as expiry approaches; long gamma benefits from large price moves regardless of direction. Vega: ∂C/∂σ — change in option price per 1% change in volatility; always positive for long options. Theta (Θ): ∂C/∂T — change per unit time (time decay); negative for long options (option loses value as expiry approaches). Rho (ρ): ∂C/∂r — change per unit change in risk-free rate. Greeks enable dynamic hedging: delta-neutral portfolio is insensitive to small price moves.

**CC-015 — Futures and Forwards**
A forward contract: obligates the buyer (long) to purchase and the seller (short) to sell an asset at a specified price (forward price) on a future date; customized, OTC, settlement at maturity. A futures contract: standardized forward traded on an exchange; daily mark-to-market (MTM) with margin accounts (eliminates counterparty credit risk). No-arbitrage forward price: F₀ = S₀ × e^{(r−y)T} where r = risk-free rate and y = dividend/storage/convenience yield; futures prices reflect cost of carry. Backwardation: F₀ < E[S_T] (futures price below expected spot — common in commodity markets with high convenience yield); contango: F₀ > E[S_T] (common when storage costs dominate). Futures are used for: hedging price risk, speculation, price discovery.

**CC-016 — Swaps**
An interest rate swap: two parties exchange fixed and floating rate interest payments on a notional principal; the most common: pay fixed / receive LIBOR (or SOFR post-2022); used to convert fixed-rate liabilities to floating or vice versa. Valuing an IRS: = PV(fixed leg) − PV(floating leg); at inception, priced so NPV = 0 (par swap rate is the fixed rate making PV equal). Total return swap (TRS): one party receives the total return (capital + income) on an asset, pays a floating rate; used for synthetic exposure to assets without legal ownership. Credit default swap (CDS): buyer pays premium; receives payoff on credit event; used for hedging and speculation. Commodity swaps: exchange fixed price for floating commodity price over multiple periods.

**CC-017 — Modigliani-Miller Theorems**
MM Proposition I (1958, no taxes): in a world without taxes, bankruptcy costs, or information asymmetry, the value of a firm is independent of its capital structure (financing irrelevance). MM Proposition II: the cost of equity increases with leverage to exactly offset the benefit of cheaper debt (the WACC remains constant). MM Proposition I with taxes: interest is tax-deductible → tax shield (PV of tax shield = T_c × D for permanent debt); levered firm value = unlevered value + T_c × D. Trade-off theory: the optimal capital structure balances the tax shield against the costs of financial distress; firms target a leverage ratio where the marginal benefit of the tax shield equals the marginal cost of distress.

**CC-018 — WACC and Cost of Capital**
WACC = (E/(E+D)) × r_E + (D/(E+D)) × r_D × (1−T_c). Cost of debt r_D: the yield to maturity on new (marginal) debt, after tax; typically 3–6% for investment grade. Cost of equity r_E (CAPM): r_E = Rₓ + β × MRP; or DDM: r_E = D₁/P₀ + g. Market weights vs. book weights: always use market weights (book values are historical costs, not opportunity costs). WACC is the hurdle rate for capital budgeting — accept projects with IRR > WACC and positive NPV. APV (Adjusted Present Value): V_levered = V_unlevered + PV(tax shield) − PV(financial distress costs); more flexible than WACC when capital structure changes during the project.

**CC-019 — Capital Budgeting**
The IRR (Internal Rate of Return): the discount rate that sets NPV = 0; accept if IRR > hurdle rate; problems: multiple IRRs for non-conventional cash flows, reinvestment rate assumption (assumes CFs reinvested at IRR, not WACC); cannot rank mutually exclusive projects correctly when size or timing differs. NPV is theoretically superior: additive, unique, correct for ranking mutually exclusive projects. Payback period: number of years to recover initial investment; ignores time value of money and terminal cash flows; simple but misleading — commonly used as a secondary screening criterion. Real options: the option to expand, delay, or abandon a project has value that DCF ignores; real option value = NPV_expanded − NPV_base; Black-Scholes or binomial trees value real options.

**CC-020 — Behavioral Finance**
Classical finance assumes rational agents with consistent preferences. Behavioral finance (Kahneman, Thaler, Shiller) documents systematic deviations: Loss aversion (losses loom approximately 2.5× larger than equivalent gains) → disposition effect (investors sell winners too early, hold losers too long). Overconfidence: investors trade excessively (overestimate skill, underestimate risk). Herding: investors follow the crowd; amplifies bubbles and crashes. Mental accounting: investors categorize money into separate "accounts" with different spending rules (house money effect). Anchoring: investors anchor to purchase price as a reference point. Framing effects: whether a gain or loss relative to reference point; dividend vs. capital gain framing. Market implications: momentum (continuation of recent trends), mean reversion over longer horizons, value premium, calendar anomalies.

**CC-021 — Market Microstructure**
The study of the process and outcomes of exchanging assets under specific trading rules. Bid-ask spread: the difference between the best ask (lowest price sellers will accept) and best bid (highest price buyers will offer); spreads compensate market makers for inventory risk, order processing costs, and adverse selection (trading against informed investors). Market depth: the quantity available at the bid and ask. Price impact: large orders move prices adversely — both temporary (liquidity) and permanent (information). Limit order book (LOB): queued buy and sell limit orders at various price levels; defines the market microstructure of modern electronic exchanges. High-frequency trading (HFT): algorithmic trading at microsecond speeds; provides liquidity but raises fairness questions.

**CC-022 — Risk Management: VaR and CVaR**
Value at Risk (VaR): the maximum loss not exceeded with probability 1−α over a holding period (typically 99% VaR over 1 day: the loss exceeded only 1% of trading days). VaR estimation: Historical simulation (use actual past returns), Parametric (assume normal distribution, VaR = μ − z_α × σ), Monte Carlo simulation. VaR limitations: does not capture tail risk beyond the confidence level; not coherent (subadditive fails — portfolio VaR can exceed sum of component VaRs for non-normal distributions). CVaR (Conditional Value at Risk, Expected Shortfall): the expected loss conditional on exceeding VaR; coherent risk measure; CVaR = E[Loss | Loss > VaR]. Basel III requires banks to use Expected Shortfall (CVaR at 97.5%) rather than VaR for market risk capital.

**CC-023 — Alternative Investments**
Private Equity: Venture Capital (early-stage equity), Growth Equity (expansion-stage), Leveraged Buyouts (LBO — acquisition using significant debt, typically 50–70% leverage; financial engineering, operational improvement, multiple expansion). PE returns measured by IRR and TVPI (Total Value to Paid-In capital); the "J-curve" (early negative cash flows from fees and investments, later positive from distributions). Hedge Funds: diverse strategies (long/short equity, global macro, event-driven, merger arbitrage, fixed income relative value, managed futures); fee structure "2 and 20" (2% management fee + 20% performance fee with high-water mark); the 2 and 20 model is declining to ~1 and 15 under competition. Real Assets: real estate (REIT vs. direct), infrastructure (toll roads, airports, utilities), commodities.

**CC-024 — Financial Markets and Institutions**
Equity markets: primary (IPO, secondary offerings) and secondary (exchange-listed, OTC). Fixed income: government bonds (USTs, Bunds), corporate bonds, mortgage-backed securities (MBS), ABS, municipal bonds. Money markets: short-term instruments (T-bills, commercial paper, repo, Fed funds). Currency markets (FX): largest market by volume (~$7.5 trillion/day in 2022); spot, forward, futures, options; reserve currencies (USD, EUR, JPY, GBP, CHF, CNY). Central banks: conduct monetary policy (set short-term rates, quantitative easing/tightening); act as lenders of last resort; regulate banks. Commercial banks: accept deposits, extend credit; maturity transformation (borrow short, lend long) creates liquidity but also systemic fragility. Investment banks: underwrite securities, advise M&A, proprietary trading (reduced by Volcker Rule).

**CC-025 — Fixed Income Yield Curve Analysis**
The yield curve describes the relationship between maturity and yield for bonds of the same credit quality. Theories of the yield curve shape: (1) Expectations theory: long-term rates = geometric average of expected future short-term rates (implies no risk premium — not empirically supported alone); (2) Liquidity preference theory: investors require a term premium for longer maturities (upward-sloping yield curve even if rates expected to be constant); (3) Market segmentation theory: different maturities are separate markets with distinct supply and demand (explains some anomalies). Key rates: Fed funds rate (the overnight rate, controlled by the FOMC); 2-year UST (most sensitive to expected Fed policy); 10-year UST (the global risk-free rate benchmark); 30-year UST (important for pension and insurance duration matching).

**CC-026 — The Capital Structure Irrelevance and Pecking Order Theory**
Modigliani-Miller (with no frictions): capital structure is irrelevant; the firm's total value depends only on its assets and their cash flows, not on how those assets are financed. With taxes: leverage adds value (tax shield). With financial distress costs: too much leverage destroys value (direct costs: legal/admin; indirect costs: lost customers, employees, suppliers). Trade-off theory: optimal structure balances tax benefits and distress costs; typically implies ~25–40% debt for investment-grade industrial firms. Pecking order theory (Myers & Majluf, 1984): managers prefer internal financing (retained earnings) over external; if external needed, debt preferred to equity (equity signals overvaluation to the market); explains why profitable firms have low debt ratios (they fund internally).

**CC-027 — Mergers and Acquisitions**
M&A transactions: horizontal (same industry), vertical (along the supply chain), or conglomerate. Valuation: DCF of standalone target + synergy value; precedent transaction multiples. Deal structure: cash (certain value, taxable), stock (market-price-contingent, tax-free reorganization if structured correctly), or mixed. Control premium: acquirers typically pay 20–40% above pre-announcement market price. Synergy sources: revenue synergies (cross-selling, new markets), cost synergies (eliminating redundant functions, economies of scale), financial synergies (lower cost of capital from size or diversification). Acquirer returns are typically flat or slightly negative around announcement; target returns are strongly positive (~20%). Most value in M&A is captured by the target shareholders, not the acquirer's shareholders.

**CC-028 — Asset Allocation and the Investment Policy Statement**
Asset allocation is the most important determinant of long-term portfolio performance (Brinson, Hood, Beebower, 1986 — asset allocation explains ~90% of total return variability). Strategic asset allocation (SAA): long-term target allocation based on return, risk, and correlation assumptions; mean-variance optimization; Black-Litterman model (combines equilibrium market-implied returns with investor views). Tactical asset allocation (TAA): short-term deviations from SAA based on valuations or macro views. The Investment Policy Statement (IPS): defines: investment objectives (return, risk tolerance), constraints (liquidity, time horizon, taxes, legal, unique), and permitted asset classes and instruments. Rebalancing: returning to SAA targets when market movements cause drift — typically annually or when drift exceeds a band (e.g., ±5%).

**CC-029 — Equity Styles: Value vs. Growth**
Value investing: investing in stocks trading below intrinsic value (low P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA). Benjamin Graham and David Dodd's "Security Analysis" (1934) — "margin of safety." Warren Buffett's evolution: "wonderful companies at fair prices" over "fair companies at wonderful prices." The value premium has persisted for decades but faced a protracted drawdown in 2008–2020 due to low interest rates (long-duration growth stocks benefit from low discount rates). Growth investing: investing in companies growing earnings faster than the market; willing to pay high multiples for sustained growth. Growth dominates in low-rate environments; value in high-rate environments. The growth-value rotation is one of the most systematic and exploited factor dynamics.

**CC-030 — Risk-Adjusted Performance Measurement**
Sharpe ratio: (R_p − R_f)/σ_p — excess return per unit of total risk; appropriate for undiversified portfolios or the only investment. Treynor ratio: (R_p − R_f)/β_p — excess return per unit of systematic risk; appropriate for diversified portfolios. Jensen's alpha: α = R_p − [R_f + β_p(R_M − R_f)] — the CAPM-adjusted excess return; positive alpha = outperformance adjusted for market exposure. Information ratio (IR): (R_p − R_b)/TE_p — active return per unit of tracking error; measures consistency of active management; IR > 0.5 considered good, > 1.0 excellent. The Fundamental Law of Active Management (Grinold): IR = IC × √BR; IC = information coefficient (correlation of forecasts with outcomes), BR = breadth (number of independent bets per year); more independent forecasts + better quality forecasts = higher IR.

**CC-031 — Passive vs. Active Investing**
Active management: professional portfolio managers aim to outperform a benchmark through stock selection and/or market timing. Evidence: average active fund underperforms its benchmark after fees by approximately the expense ratio (Sharpe, 1991 — arithmetic of active management: all active managers' aggregate return = market return; some must win, some must lose, and fees make the sum negative). Survivorship bias: funds that close are removed from performance databases, inflating apparent average active manager performance. SPIVA (S&P Indices vs. Active) report: ~80–90% of active large-cap US equity funds underperformed the S&P 500 over 10–15 year periods. Passive (index) investing: hold all stocks in proportion to market cap; guarantees market return minus a very low fee (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF: 0.03% expense ratio). John Bogle founded Vanguard and the first retail index fund (1976).

**CC-032 — Systemic Risk and Financial Stability**
Systemic risk: the risk that the failure of one financial institution or market triggers cascading failures across the financial system. Sources: interconnectedness (counterparty exposures), contagion (panic and information asymmetry), leverage (amplifies losses), liquidity mismatch (bank runs), procyclicality (mark-to-market reduces capital in downturns, forcing asset sales that depress prices further). 2008 crisis: mortgage defaults → MBS devaluation → bank capital impairment → Lehman bankruptcy → money market freeze → global recession. Regulatory responses: Basel III (higher capital requirements, leverage ratios, liquidity ratios); Dodd-Frank Act (stress tests, SIFI designation, Volcker Rule, central clearing of OTC derivatives, resolution planning); systemically important financial institutions (SIFIs).

**CC-033 — Personal Finance Fundamentals**
The foundation of wealth accumulation: spend less than you earn; save consistently; invest in low-cost, diversified assets; minimize taxes; avoid high-interest debt. Emergency fund: 3–6 months of living expenses in liquid, low-risk savings (HYSA, money market fund). The 4% rule (Bengen, 1994; Trinity Study): in retirement, withdraw 4% of the initial portfolio value annually (inflation-adjusted); historically, this portfolio has lasted 30 years for a 60/40 stock-bond portfolio. Time in market vs. timing the market: consistent long-term investing (dollar-cost averaging) outperforms most market-timing strategies after accounting for transaction costs, taxes, and behavioral errors. Tax-advantaged accounts (US): 401(k) (tax-deferred; 2024 limit $23,000 + $7,500 catch-up for 50+), IRA (Traditional: tax-deferred; Roth: after-tax contributions, tax-free growth), 529 (education savings).

**CC-034 — Financial Crises: Causes and Anatomy**
Kindleberger-Minsky model: (1) Displacement (new profit opportunity emerges — new technology, new market, new monetary policy); (2) Boom (credit expansion fuels investment in the new opportunity); (3) Euphoria (speculation detaches from fundamental value; leverage increases; assets become collateral for more borrowing); (4) Distress (insiders begin to sell; smart money exits; prices plateau); (5) Revulsion (widespread selling; panic; credit contraction; asset fire sales). Common features of major crises: excessive leverage, asset price bubbles, credit booms, inadequate regulation, and herding behavior. Resolution mechanisms: lender of last resort (Bagehot's dictum: lend freely against good collateral at a high rate), deposit insurance (prevents bank runs), fiscal stimulus, regulatory forbearance.

## 5. Patterns

**P-001 — Discounting First, Then Comparing**
Before comparing any two cash flows across time, convert both to a common point in time (present value) using the appropriate discount rate. Common mistake: adding a $100 cash flow today to a $100 cash flow in 5 years as if they were equal. The discount rate must reflect the riskiness of the cash flows — risk-free rate for certain cash flows, WACC for firm-level cash flows, cost of equity for equity cash flows, and specialized rates for structured or contingent claims.

**P-002 — Diversification is the Only Free Lunch**
Combining imperfectly correlated assets reduces portfolio volatility without necessarily reducing expected return. This is the only "free lunch" in finance — the benefit is mathematically guaranteed for ρ < 1. The benefit diminishes as correlation increases (ρ → 1 → no diversification benefit). International diversification: reduces domestic systematic risk; but correlations rise in market crises exactly when diversification is most needed. Within-asset-class diversification: even when adding low-correlation assets, the diversification benefit has an upper bound (systematic/market risk cannot be diversified away).

**P-003 — The No-Arbitrage Principle**
Arbitrage: a riskless profit opportunity requiring no net investment. No-arbitrage pricing: in equilibrium, no arbitrage opportunities persist; any two equivalent cash flow streams must sell at the same price. This principle underlies: derivatives pricing (replication), interest rate parity (FX forwards = spot + interest rate differential), put-call parity (options), and the law of one price. Real-world violations of no-arbitrage (twin share prices differing for identical cash flows, closed-end fund discounts) are exploited by arbitrageurs until equilibrium is restored — or persist due to limits to arbitrage (noise trader risk, capital constraints, synchronization risk).

**P-004 — Risk-Return Tradeoff**
Higher expected returns compensate for higher risk — this is the foundational principle of asset pricing. But only undiversifiable (systematic) risk is compensated (CAPM); idiosyncratic risk can be eliminated for free via diversification. Investors demand a positive risk premium for: market risk (equity risk premium), credit risk (credit spread), liquidity risk (illiquidity premium), duration risk (term premium). Identifying whether a return is a risk premium or an alpha is the central challenge of quantitative investing — many "anomalies" are subsequently explained as risk factors.

**P-005 — Total Return Attribution**
A manager's total excess return (alpha) can be decomposed into: factor exposures × factor returns (beta-driven) + true skill (residual alpha). Brinson-Hood-Beebower attribution: excess return = allocation effect (over/underweighting asset classes) + selection effect (within-class security selection) + interaction effect. A manager with positive raw returns may have negative alpha if those returns are fully explained by beta to known risk factors (value, small-cap, momentum). Alpha should be evaluated on a risk-adjusted, factor-adjusted, and fee-adjusted basis before concluding a manager adds value.

**P-006 — Scenario and Sensitivity Analysis**
A single-point DCF estimate is meaningless without understanding its sensitivity to key assumptions. Sensitivity analysis: vary one assumption at a time (± the discount rate, ± the terminal growth rate) and observe the resulting NPV change. Scenario analysis: define 3–5 discrete scenarios (base, bull, bear, crisis) with internally consistent assumptions; compute NPV for each; weight by probability. Monte Carlo simulation: model the full joint distribution of key inputs; produce a distribution of NPV outcomes. Presenting valuation as a range (based on sensitivity) rather than a point estimate is the standard practice in investment banking and buy-side analysis.

**P-007 — The Principle of Parsimony in Factor Investing**
More factors do not necessarily produce better portfolios. With many factors, in-sample optimization will appear excellent but out-of-sample will disappoint due to overfitting. Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016): over 300 factors have been published; most are likely false discoveries given the number tested. Tests: Is the factor economically motivated (not data-mined)? Is it robust across time periods, geographies, and market conditions? Does it survive transaction costs? Is it distinct from existing known factors? The Fama-French five-factor model is a parsimonious, economically grounded benchmark against which new factors must be justified.

**P-008 — Mean Reversion vs. Momentum**
Two opposing return dynamics operate at different timescales. Momentum (continuation): assets that have performed well over 1–12 months tend to continue outperforming in the next 1–12 months (Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993); momentum is one of the most robust anomalies across asset classes and geographies. Mean reversion (reversal): over longer horizons (3–5 years), past losers tend to outperform past winners (De Bondt & Thaler, 1985 — the value premium at long horizons). Short-term reversal: over 1 month, past winners underperform (microstructure effects). The combination — selling short-term reversal, buying momentum, buying long-term value — is the structural basis of many quantitative strategies.

**P-009 — Market Timing is Very Hard**
Systematic evidence: market timing strategies consistently underperform buy-and-hold over long periods after accounting for transaction costs and taxes. DALBAR's annual study: the average equity mutual fund investor earns 2–3% per year less than the funds they invest in — due to buying high (after strong performance) and selling low (after poor performance). The majority of stock market returns are concentrated in a small fraction of trading days: missing the 10 best days in the S&P 500 from 1993–2022 would have reduced the annual compound return from 9.8% to 5.6%. The difficulty: the best days cluster near the worst days (volatile markets).

**P-010 — Leverage Amplifies Both Gains and Losses**
Financial leverage (borrowing to invest) amplifies returns: $100 invested with $50 borrowed at 5%, invested at 10% → 15% equity return. But leverage also amplifies losses: the same position loses more than the unlevered position when returns are negative. At extreme leverage, losses can exceed the initial investment (forced liquidation, margin call). Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM, 1998): 25:1 leverage; $120 billion in assets, $4.6 billion in equity; small adverse price moves wiped out the equity; the Fed organized a $3.6 billion bailout by a consortium of banks. Leverage is procyclical: as collateral values rise, more can be borrowed; as they fall, margin calls force deleveraging at the worst time.

**P-011 — Liquidity Matters Most When You Need It Most**
Illiquid assets offer an illiquidity premium — a higher expected return to compensate for the inability to sell quickly at fair value. The problem: liquidity is highly correlated with market stress — assets that appear liquid in normal times (money market funds in 2008, high-yield bonds in March 2020) can become illiquid in a crisis. Flight-to-quality: in crises, capital flows to the most liquid safe assets (USTs, cash); liquidity premium spikes. Portfolio liquidity management: maintain sufficient liquid assets to meet foreseeable cash needs without forced selling at distressed prices; never rely on the ability to sell an illiquid asset at a reasonable price in a crisis.

**P-012 — Compounding is Powerful, Fees are Compounded Losses**
The power of compounding: $1 at 7% per year grows to $7.61 in 30 years; at 8%, $10.06; at 9%, $13.27 — a 2% difference in return produces a 74% difference in final wealth after 30 years. Investment fees compound against you: a 1% annual fee on a 7% gross return reduces the net return to 6% — reducing 30-year terminal wealth from $7.61 to $5.74 (a 25% reduction). The arithmetic of after-fee returns means that an actively managed fund with a 1% expense ratio must generate 1% of annual alpha simply to match a zero-cost index fund; with a 2% fee, it must generate 2% alpha. This is why low-cost passive investing is so powerful for most investors.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

**AP-001 — The Gambler's Fallacy in Investment**
Believing that a losing investment is "due" for a recovery simply because it has declined, or that a winning investment must soon reverse simply because it has gained. In efficient markets, past price movements do not predict future returns at short horizons (for individual stocks). In inefficient markets, the evidence is mixed: value premium suggests long-term mean reversion; momentum suggests short-term continuation. Holding a losing stock because "it has to come back" without a fundamental thesis for the recovery is a behavioral trap (coupled with loss aversion and the sunk cost fallacy).

**AP-002 — Confusing Correlation with Diversification**
An investor who adds a new asset correlated at 0.7 with their existing portfolio gains less diversification than one who adds an asset at 0.3 correlation. Portfolio VaR and volatility must be computed accounting for correlations — not just the volatility of individual assets. Correlation instability: pairwise correlations estimated in normal markets rise dramatically in crises (when diversification is most needed). Adding more assets within the same asset class (e.g., 20 tech stocks instead of 10) provides little diversification — the within-class correlation is high. True diversification requires exposure to fundamentally different risk drivers.

**AP-003 — Anchoring to Purchase Price**
An investor's willingness to sell a stock or property should depend on its current intrinsic value and future prospects — not on what they paid for it. The purchase price is a sunk cost. The disposition effect (Shefrin & Statman, 1985): investors systematically sell winners (locking in gains) and hold losers (avoiding realizing losses) — the opposite of a tax-optimal strategy (harvesting tax losses). The current market price, not the purchase price, is the relevant economic reference point for future investment decisions.

**AP-004 — Neglecting Tax Efficiency**
For taxable investors, after-tax returns matter, not pre-tax returns. Short-term capital gains are taxed as ordinary income (up to 37% federal in US) vs. long-term gains at 0%, 15%, or 20%. Tax-loss harvesting: realizing losses to offset gains (deductible up to $3,000/year against ordinary income); defer long-term gains; donate appreciated assets to charity (avoid capital gains, take fair-value deduction). Asset location: hold tax-inefficient assets (REITs, high-yield bonds, actively managed funds) in tax-advantaged accounts; hold tax-efficient assets (index funds, growth stocks) in taxable accounts. After-tax alpha is the correct measure of manager skill for taxable investors.

**AP-005 — Overtrading**
Excessive trading destroys wealth through transaction costs (bid-ask spreads, commissions), market impact, and taxes on realized gains. Barber & Odean (2000): men trade 45% more than women and earn 0.94% lower annual net returns; single men trade 67% more than single women and earn 1.44% less annually — overconfidence is the primary driver. Market liquidity: in liquid markets (large-cap US stocks), bid-ask spreads are small but still impose a cost; in illiquid markets (small-cap, emerging market), the cost of a round-trip trade can be 1–5% of portfolio value. Turnover ratio: a fund with 100% turnover replaces its entire portfolio annually; each round-trip generates costs.

**AP-006 — The Recency Bias in Return Expectations**
Investors systematically overweight recent returns when forming expectations of future returns, leading to: buying after strong performance (near market peaks) and selling after poor performance (near market troughs). Survey data: investor expected returns are highest at market peaks and lowest at market troughs. Valuation-implied returns (Shiller CAPE): long-term expected real equity returns are inversely related to current valuations — high CAPE → lower future returns. Rebalancing mechanically forces buying when cheap and selling when expensive — a systematic counter to recency bias.

**AP-007 — Ignoring Fees, Taxes, and Transaction Costs**
Gross returns are not what investors receive. The total expense ratio (TER) of active mutual funds in the US averages ~0.66% (2022, Morningstar); some are >2%. Active ETFs: 0.5–1%. Passive ETFs: 0.03–0.20%. Private equity fee loads: 2% management fee + 20% carry on top of fund-level fees, plus underlying company debt service; effective cost to LP can exceed 5% per year. Transaction costs in institutional markets: brokerage commissions (near-zero for retail) + bid-ask spread + market impact (for large orders). After all costs, the majority of active managers underperform their passive benchmarks — the math of fees is the primary explanation.

**AP-008 — Confusing Volatility with Risk**
Standard volatility (σ) measures the typical dispersion of returns around the mean — it captures both upside and downside variation. Many investors intuitively think of risk as only the bad outcomes. For long-term investors, volatility is less relevant than permanent capital loss. Downside risk measures: semi-deviation (standard deviation of returns below a threshold), maximum drawdown (peak-to-trough decline), VaR, CVaR. Low-volatility assets can have high tail risk (e.g., stable returns + rare catastrophic losses). The equity risk premium compensates for volatility only insofar as investors cannot diversify away market risk; idiosyncratic volatility earns no compensation.

**AP-009 — Herding and Following Recent Performance**
Investors systematically chase recent performance in fund selection: after strong returns, fund inflows surge; after poor returns, outflows follow. Since most of a fund's returns occur before the assets peak (when the fund is smaller), the dollar-weighted (IRR) investor return is typically lower than the time-weighted fund return. The pattern of chasing performance ensures that the average investor underperforms the average fund — by buying high and implicitly selling low by holding through redemptions. The Dalbar study documents this gap: the average equity fund investor earned ~3% less annually than the funds they invested in over 20 years.

**AP-010 — Assuming Historical Average Returns Will Repeat**
US equities returned approximately 10% nominal and 7% real per year since 1926 — but this includes survivorship bias (the US was the 20th century's most successful economy; other markets performed worse); starting valuation matters (Shiller CAPE in 2024 at ~30–35× vs. historical average ~17×); expected future returns from current valuations are lower. The equity risk premium is not guaranteed — it reflects compensation for bearing equity risk that sometimes materializes catastrophically (Japan's Nikkei fell 80% from 1989 peak and had not recovered by 2012; major bear markets in the US have produced 10-year negative real returns). Scenario-based planning acknowledges the distribution of outcomes, not just the historical average.

## 7. Facts

**F-001** — Benjamin Graham's "The Intelligent Investor" (1949): the most influential investing book ever written, according to Warren Buffett ("by far the best book on investing ever written"); introduced the concept of Mr. Market (market prices represent the irrational emotional quotations of a manic-depressive fellow, not intrinsic values) and the margin of safety (buying below intrinsic value provides protection against errors in estimation and adverse events); Graham managed the Graham-Newman Corporation from 1936 to 1956, compounding at approximately 17% per year.

**F-002** — The first stock exchange: the Amsterdam Stock Exchange (Beurs van Hendrick de Keyser, 1602) was the first formal stock exchange in the world; it traded shares of the Dutch East India Company (VOC — Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie), the first joint-stock company in the world; the VOC had a market capitalization that would be equivalent to approximately $7–8 trillion in today's dollars; it paid dividends of 16–18% annually on average over its first 200 years of existence.

**F-003** — The S&P 500 was introduced in 1957 as a broad market index (the predecessor Composite Index of 90 stocks dates to 1926); it is a market-cap-weighted index of 500 large US companies; as of 2024, the 10 largest companies constitute approximately 30–35% of the index weight (Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, Meta, Alphabet A/C, Berkshire Hathaway, Eli Lilly, Broadcom); from 1957 to 2023, the S&P 500 returned approximately 10.5% per year on average (nominal), including dividends.

**F-004** — Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway performance: from 1965–2022, Berkshire Hathaway achieved a compound annual gain of 19.8% per year vs. 9.9% for the S&P 500 including dividends; over that period, Berkshire's overall gain was 3,641,613% vs. the S&P 500's 30,209%; Buffett's 2023 letter to shareholders: "Charlie Munger and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers"; Berkshire's float (insurance premiums held before claims are paid) has provided interest-free leverage; as of 2024, Buffett has donated approximately $49 billion to the Gates Foundation and other charities.

**F-005** — The Black-Scholes-Merton paper "The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities" was published in the Journal of Political Economy in May 1973 (submitted 1970; rejected by two journals before acceptance); Myron Scholes and Robert Merton won the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economics; Fischer Black had died in 1995 and Nobel Prizes are not awarded posthumously; the formula was first used in practice by Scholes and his colleagues at options trading firms (including Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette) before publication.

**F-006** — The Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) collapse (1998): LTCM was founded in 1994 by John Meriwether with Myron Scholes and Robert Merton on the board of directors; peak assets $125 billion on $4.6 billion equity (~27:1 leverage); notional derivatives exposure $1.25 trillion; nearly collapsed after Russian default (August 1998) caused correlated price moves across its positions; the Federal Reserve Bank of New York organized a $3.6 billion bailout by 14 banks to prevent systemic crisis; LTCM's losses: 90% of its equity in 4 months; lessons: model risk (historical correlations fail in crises), liquidity risk, and leverage amplification.

**F-007** — John Bogle founded The Vanguard Group in 1974 and launched the first index mutual fund available to individual investors (Vanguard First Index Investment Trust, later Vanguard 500 Index Fund) on December 31, 1975; initially derided as "Bogle's Folly" and raised only $11 million at launch (target $150 million); by 2023, passive index funds held by Vanguard had grown to $7.7 trillion in assets under management; the shift to index investing is estimated to have saved US investors approximately $1 trillion in fees since the 1970s.

**F-008** — The Modigliani-Miller theorems: Franco Modigliani won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1985 (for MM and his life-cycle hypothesis); Merton Miller won it in 1990 (for MM and related contributions to corporate finance); their 1958 paper "The Cost of Capital, Corporation Finance, and the Theory of Investment" is one of the most cited papers in economics; their insight was to apply no-arbitrage reasoning to corporate finance — the same methodology that underlies all derivatives pricing.

**F-009** — Global equity market capitalization: as of end-2023, approximately $109 trillion (World Federation of Exchanges); US = ~43% (New York Stock Exchange + Nasdaq); Europe = ~18%; Asia-Pacific = ~28% (Japan ~6%, China A+H ~8%, Hong Kong ~3%); the US has historically traded at premium valuations to other markets (Shiller CAPE ~30–35 vs. international ~12–18); the US's share of global market cap has grown from ~30% in 1990 to ~43% in 2023 due to technology company dominance.

**F-010** — The 2008 Global Financial Crisis: key dates: Bear Stearns bailout (March 2008, $2 subsidy from Fed, JPMorgan acquires); Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac conservatorship (September 7, 2008); Lehman Brothers bankruptcy (September 15, 2008 — the largest in US history at the time, $613 billion in liabilities); AIG bailout ($182 billion total from the US government); TARP (Troubled Asset Relief Program, $700 billion authorized, ~$447 billion disbursed); the S&P 500 fell 57% from October 2007 to March 2009; global GDP declined by approximately 2.1% in 2009; US unemployment reached 10%.

**F-011** — Charles Dow founded the Wall Street Journal (1889) and developed the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA, 1896) as a price-weighted average of 12 industrial stocks; now 30 stocks; price-weighted (higher-priced stocks have more influence — a poor design characteristic); unlike the S&P 500, DJIA is not market-cap-weighted; the original 12 DJIA components included American Cotton Oil, Distilling & Cattle Feeding Company, and Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company; only General Electric survived (continuously) until it was removed in 2018.

**F-012** — The equity risk premium (ERP): the expected excess return of stocks over risk-free bonds; estimated historically at 5–7% annualized in the US; forward-looking estimates (Damodaran's implied ERP from S&P 500 earnings yield minus bond yield) typically range 4–6%; fundamental to all equity valuation (CAPM requires an ERP assumption); lower ERP (due to falling interest rates 1980–2020) drove the rise in P/E multiples; as rates rose in 2022, multiples compressed (higher r → lower present value of future earnings).

**F-013** — George Soros and the "breaking of the Bank of England" (Black Wednesday, September 16, 1992): Soros's Quantum Fund shorted £10 billion of sterling before the UK's forced withdrawal from the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM); the UK government raised interest rates to 15% to defend the peg; ultimately withdrew and devalued; Soros profited approximately $1 billion in one day; his macro thesis: the ERM forced the UK to maintain artificially high interest rates to defend the pound, while the German reunification drove Bundesbank rates higher, creating an unsustainable commitment.

**F-014** — The South Sea Bubble (1720): the South Sea Company (UK) was granted a monopoly on trade with South America; its directors spread rumors of enormous profits; stock price rose from £128 to £1,000 in 8 months; Isaac Newton lost £20,000 (approximately £3 million in today's terms) in the collapse; the Mississippi Company (France, John Law's scheme) collapsed simultaneously; both led to laws restricting joint-stock companies (the Bubble Act, 1720) for over a century; classical examples of speculative euphoria → bubble → crash anatomy.

**F-015** — The efficient market hypothesis empirical anomalies: momentum (12-month historical winner portfolios outperform by ~4–6% per year; Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993); value premium (high book-to-market portfolios outperform by ~5% per year historically; Fama & French, 1992); size premium (small-cap outperforms large-cap by ~3% historically; disappearing in recent decades); post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD: stocks drift in the direction of earnings surprises for 60 days after announcement); January effect (small-cap outperformance in January); these are "anomalies" relative to the EMH but may reflect risk factors rather than pure mispricings.

**F-016** — The Dutch Tulip Mania (1636–1637): tulip bulbs (particularly the "broken" color patterns caused by a virus) became the object of speculative buying in the Netherlands; at the peak, a single Semper Augustus tulip bulb was worth approximately 10× the annual income of a skilled craftsman; futures contracts for bulbs were traded on exchanges; the bubble collapsed in February 1637 when buyers failed to show up at a Haarlem auction; the actual extent of the mania is debated by economic historians (some argue it was confined to a narrow segment of the Dutch population and the macroeconomic impact was limited).

**F-017** — Quantitative easing (QE): the Federal Reserve's balance sheet expanded from approximately $900 billion before the 2008 crisis to $4.5 trillion by 2015 (QE1, QE2, QE3) and $9 trillion by 2022 (QE∞ during COVID); the mechanism: the Fed purchases long-term Treasury bonds and agency MBS, injecting reserves into the banking system, lowering long-term yields, and pushing investors into riskier assets (the "portfolio balance channel"); the 2022 rate hiking cycle (from 0% to 5.5% in 18 months) and quantitative tightening (QT) reversed a decade of accommodation.

**F-018** — The Sharpe ratio's inventor: William Sharpe (1964 CAPM, 1966 Sharpe ratio) won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1990, together with Merton Miller and Harry Markowitz; Sharpe's 1966 paper compared mutual fund performance using the ratio of excess return to standard deviation; the Sharpe ratio is the single most widely used risk-adjusted performance metric in the investment industry; Sharpe later developed the Sharpe-Lintner CAPM (with John Lintner independently developing the same model in 1965).

**F-019** — The Federal Reserve System: established by the Federal Reserve Act (1913) in response to the Panic of 1907; consists of 12 regional Federal Reserve Banks + the Board of Governors (Washington DC) + the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC, 12 members); the FOMC sets the federal funds rate (the target overnight interbank rate); the "dual mandate": maximum employment and price stability (2% inflation target); the Fed's balance sheet assets: USTs (~$5 trillion), agency MBS (~$2 trillion) as of 2023; the Fed is technically independent of the executive but is created by Congress and subject to its oversight.

**F-020** — Global bond market: approximately $130 trillion total outstanding (SIFMA, 2023); US is the largest (USTs ~$25 trillion, US corporate ~$10 trillion, US MBS/ABS ~$12 trillion); Japan (~$14 trillion), EU (~$13 trillion), China (~$10 trillion); the 30-year UST yield serves as the global benchmark for long-duration risk pricing; the 2022–2023 UST selloff (10-year yield rising from 1.5% to 5%) was the largest bond market decline since at least the 1950s — some metrics suggest the worst year for bonds in 200 years.

**F-021** — The Medallion Fund of Renaissance Technologies: founded by James Simons and colleagues in 1988; has produced annualized returns of approximately 66% before fees (39% after fees) since inception — the best sustained track record in investment history by a large margin; uses highly sophisticated quantitative mathematical and statistical models; available only to current and former employees of Renaissance Technologies; total estimated profits to investors: approximately $100 billion since inception; Simons died in May 2024 with a net worth of approximately $31 billion.

**F-022** — Gamma squeezes and the GameStop short squeeze (January 2021): retail investors coordinated on Reddit's WallStreetBets to buy GameStop (GME) stock and options; market makers who sold call options were forced to buy stock to delta-hedge (a gamma squeeze — accelerating as the stock moved up and dealer gamma exposure increased); hedge funds with large short positions (notably Melvin Capital) faced catastrophic losses; GME rose from ~$20 to ~$483 in one month; Robinhood and other brokers temporarily restricted buying, citing DTCC margin requirements — sparking congressional hearings; Melvin Capital ultimately lost $6.8 billion and was wound down.

**F-023** — The capital asset pricing model's empirical test by Jensen (1968): using a database of 115 mutual funds (1945–1964), Jensen found that the average mutual fund underperformed its CAPM-predicted return by approximately 1.1% per year after costs; this early empirical evidence for market efficiency and against active management's ability to generate alpha has been corroborated by virtually every subsequent large-scale study.

**F-024** — Structured products and the 2008 crisis: mortgage-backed securities (MBS) pool thousands of individual mortgages; collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) pool MBS tranches; CDO-squared pool CDOs; AAA-rated tranches of CDOs built on subprime MBS defaulted at rates 10–20× higher than predicted by the rating agencies' models; the models used to rate these securities assumed geographic diversification of mortgages would prevent correlated defaults — a critical assumption that failed when house prices fell nationally simultaneously; AIG sold credit default swap protection on CDOs without holding capital against these positions.

**F-025** — The 4% withdrawal rule limitations: Bengen (1994) found that a 4% initial withdrawal rate (inflation-adjusted) would sustain a 30-year retirement for a 60/40 portfolio in all historical US market environments; but: the rule was calibrated on US historical returns (which may not recur); a 30-year retirement is a 25th percentile outcome (many retirees live longer); the rule was designed for worst-case US historical scenarios, not median scenarios; with current valuations and expected returns, some analyses suggest a 3–3.5% withdrawal rate is more appropriate for long retirements; sequence-of-returns risk (early large drawdowns are most destructive) is the primary risk.

**F-026** — The private equity performance debate: PE proponents: Cambridge Associates and Preqin report median PE fund IRRs of approximately 13–15% vs. public market equivalents (PME) of approximately 11–12% for S&P 500, suggesting 2–4% outperformance; critics: top-quartile past performance does not predict future performance; J-curve reporting and IRR manipulation (continuation funds, NAV management); leverage (PE uses ~5–7× EBITDA debt vs. public companies ~2–3× EBITDA) explains a large portion of PE returns; illiquidity premium is real but may not be 4%; Kaplan and Schoar (2005) found median PE fund barely beats public markets net of fees.

**F-027** — The Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting ("Woodstock for Capitalists"): held annually in Omaha, Nebraska; attended by approximately 40,000 shareholders; Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger (until Munger's death in November 2023 at age 99) spent 5–6 hours answering unfiltered questions; the event is simultaneously a business meeting, educational seminar, and cultural phenomenon; Buffett's shareholder letters (1965–present) are freely available at berkshirehathaway.com and constitute the most comprehensive single investor education archive in existence.

**F-028** — The P/E ratio's inventor: the concept of valuing stocks relative to earnings is at least as old as Graham and Dodd (1934); the Shiller CAPE (Cyclically Adjusted Price/Earnings) ratio, introduced by Robert Shiller and John Campbell (1988) and popularized in Shiller's "Irrational Exuberance" (2000), uses a 10-year average of real earnings to smooth cyclicality; current Shiller CAPE for S&P 500 (2024): approximately 30–35× vs. historical average ~17×; Shiller won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2013 for his work on empirical asset pricing.

**F-029** — The carry trade in foreign exchange: borrowing in a low-interest-rate currency (funding currency: JPY, CHF) and investing in a high-interest-rate currency (target currency: AUD, MXN, TRY); the interest rate differential is the "carry"; the risk: the target currency depreciates (often dramatically and suddenly — the "carry trade unwind"); the JPY carry trade collapsed spectacularly in August 2024 when the Bank of Japan unexpectedly raised interest rates, triggering a massive yen appreciation and forced liquidation of global risk assets.

**F-030** — The venture capital industry: total US VC investment approximately $170 billion in 2022 (peak cycle); NVCA (National Venture Capital Association); returns are highly skewed — most VC funds lose money; the industry is sustained by a small number of outsized winners; Sequoia Capital's investment in Apple ($150,000 for 1/3 of the company in 1978), Google, Airbnb, Stripe; Andreessen Horowitz's investment in Facebook; the power law of VC returns: the top 1% of investments generate the majority of total returns; "spray and pray" (investing in many companies to hit the occasional unicorn) vs. concentrated conviction bets are the two dominant VC strategies.

**F-031** — The Black-Scholes option pricing formula assumptions and their violations: (1) constant volatility — violated by the volatility smile/skew (implied volatility varies with strike and maturity, especially pronounced for equity index options which show a volatility skew with higher implied volatility for lower strikes — the "smirk"); (2) log-normal returns — fat tails in actual returns; (3) continuous trading — market gaps at open/close/events; (4) no transaction costs — bid-ask spreads and commissions; (5) no dividends — addressed by modified Merton model; these violations motivate stochastic volatility models (Heston), jump-diffusion models (Merton, Kou), and variance gamma models.

**F-032** — The cryptocurrency market: Bitcoin was created by Satoshi Nakamoto (pseudonym) in 2009; maximum supply 21 million BTC (by design — ~19.7 million mined as of 2024); market capitalization at all-time high ~November 2021: ~$1.3 trillion for BTC; total crypto market cap ATH: ~$3 trillion (November 2021); Bitcoin halving: every ~4 years, the block reward is halved (~50 → 25 → 12.5 → 6.25 → 3.125 BTC per block in 2024); the Ethereum network hosts smart contracts and DeFi (decentralized finance); the SEC approved spot Bitcoin ETFs in January 2024; as of 2024, the total crypto market is approximately $2 trillion.

**F-033** — Convertible bonds: hybrid securities (debt + equity option); the bondholder receives fixed coupons and par value at maturity, but can convert to equity at a pre-specified conversion price; the conversion premium is typically 20–35% above the current stock price at issuance; convertible bonds are valued using an option-pricing model (the equity option component) + a straight bond component (yield premium over straight debt); typically issued by growth companies with weaker credit ratings (the equity option compensates for lower coupon); prominent in tech sector financing.

**F-034** — The SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission): established by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 in response to the 1929 crash; regulates securities markets, brokerage firms, investment advisers, mutual funds, ETFs; enforces insider trading prohibitions; requires disclosure (10-K annual reports, 10-Q quarterly reports, 8-K material event filings); the SEC's EDGAR database provides free public access to all public company filings; the SEC is primarily a disclosure-based regulator (not a merit regulator — it does not rule on whether investments are a good idea, only that material facts are fully disclosed).

**F-035** — Real estate as an asset class: global real estate value approximately $330 trillion (2022, Savills) — the largest single asset class globally; US commercial real estate: approximately $20 trillion; US residential real estate: approximately $45 trillion; real estate returns: private real estate (NCREIF Property Index) has returned approximately 8–9% per year over 20 years; REITs (publicly traded real estate investment trusts) have returned approximately 11–12% per year historically; real estate provides inflation protection (rental income and property values tend to rise with inflation), diversification (low correlation with stocks and bonds over long periods), and tax benefits (depreciation deductions, 1031 exchange deferral).

**F-036** — The 2022 UK Gilt Crisis (LDI crisis): pension funds in the UK used Liability-Driven Investment (LDI) strategies — using interest rate swaps and gilts to match long-duration liabilities; the strategies used leverage (borrowed to buy more gilts); when UK government bond yields spiked after Truss-Kwarteng's unfunded mini-budget (September 22, 2022), gilt prices collapsed; pension funds faced margin calls; forced gilt sales further depressed prices (a liquidity spiral); the Bank of England intervened with emergency gilt purchases (~£65 billion) to prevent pension fund insolvency and systemic collapse; the episode illustrates how apparently "safe" hedging strategies can create systemic risk through correlated leverage.

**F-037** — Benjamin Graham's net-net investing: stocks trading below "net current asset value" (NCAV = current assets − total liabilities); Graham found these were systematically undervalued because the market price was below the liquidation value of the business (current assets alone, ignoring the long-term assets entirely, exceeded the market cap + all liabilities); a study of Graham's 1926–1956 net-net portfolio found average returns of approximately 20% per year; this strategy is rarely applicable in liquid markets today but periodically reappears in small-cap and international markets.

**F-038** — The asset management industry: global AUM (assets under management) approximately $100 trillion (2023, BCG); top 10 asset managers by AUM: BlackRock (~$10 trillion), Vanguard (~$7.7 trillion), Fidelity (~$4.5 trillion), JPMorgan Asset Management, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, UBS, Amundi, PIMCO, T. Rowe Price; the passive-to-active shift: passive (index) AUM surpassed active AUM in US equity funds for the first time in 2019 (Investment Company Institute); fee compression is the primary trend: the asset-weighted average expense ratio of US mutual funds fell from 0.93% (2000) to 0.37% (2022) (ICI).

**F-039** — Insider trading regulations: in the US, Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and Rule 10b-5 prohibit fraudulent practices in connection with the purchase or sale of securities; the classical insider trading prohibition covers corporate insiders (employees, directors, officers) trading on MNPI (material non-public information); the misappropriation theory (US v. O'Hagan, 1997) extends liability to outsiders who misappropriate MNPI from their principal; Rule 10b5-1 allows insiders to set up pre-arranged trading plans that provide an affirmative defense; the SEC brought 68 insider trading enforcement actions in FY2022.

**F-040** — The Buffett Indicator: total US stock market capitalization / US GDP; Buffett commented in 2001 that "it is probably the best single measure of where valuations stand at any given moment"; at the peak of the dot-com bubble (2000) it reached ~140%; at the 2009 trough, ~70%; at 2021 peak ~200%; as of 2024 approximately 170–180%; historical mean approximately 100%; similar conceptually to Shiller CAPE — both measure current market valuations relative to fundamental anchors (GDP or 10-year average earnings); both suggest US equity markets are expensive relative to history (though structural changes including increased corporate profit margins and globalization may justify higher valuations than the historical mean).

**F-041** — Negative interest rates: the European Central Bank (ECB) set its deposit facility rate below zero (−0.10%) in June 2014 — the first major central bank to do so; the Bank of Japan introduced negative rates in January 2016; at the peak (2020), approximately $18 trillion of global government bonds traded at negative yields; the theoretical problem: negative rates flip the normal relationship between savers and borrowers; near-zero lower bound — cash pays 0%, so rates cannot go deeply negative without triggering conversion to physical cash (the "zero lower bound"); negative rates were intended to force banks to lend rather than hoard reserves; their effectiveness was debated.

**F-042** — The Nasdaq Composite: launched February 8, 1971 as the world's first electronic stock market; originally traded OTC (over-the-counter) securities; grew to host technology companies (Microsoft, Apple, Intel, Cisco, Amazon); at the dot-com peak (March 10, 2000): 5,048 points; trough (October 2002): 1,114 points (−78% from peak); did not recover to its year-2000 high until April 2015 — 15 years; the Nasdaq 100 (the 100 largest non-financial companies) is tracked by the QQQ ETF (one of the most traded ETFs globally); as of 2024, the Nasdaq Composite is approximately 17,000–19,000 points.

**F-043** — Initial Public Offerings (IPOs): the process by which a private company sells shares to the public for the first time; governed by the Securities Act of 1933 (registration statement, prospectus, SEC review); underwriters (investment banks) price the offering and allocate shares; bookbuilding: investment banks canvass institutional investors for demand ("anchor orders") before setting the price; IPO underpricing: first-day returns average approximately 18% historically (Jay Ritter's data, 1980–2023) — suggesting companies leave money "on the table"; reasons: deliberate underpricing to reward regular institutional investors (relationships), information uncertainty, winner's curse. DeSPAC (SPAC merger): blank-check company with proceeds raised in an IPO, then merges with a private company; became dominant in 2020–2021, collapsed in 2022–2023 due to poor post-merger performance.

**F-044** — Sovereign wealth funds (SWFs): state-owned investment funds managing sovereign assets; total AUM approximately $10.5 trillion (SWFI, 2023); largest: Norway Government Pension Fund Global (~$1.6 trillion — the world's largest single investor, owns approximately 1.5% of all global equities), China Investment Corporation (~$1.35 trillion), Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (~$1.0 trillion), Kuwait Investment Authority (~$750 billion), GIC Private Limited (Singapore, ~$744 billion); typically invest globally in public equities, fixed income, real assets, and alternatives; Norway's fund excludes companies involved in tobacco, weapons, or severe ESG violations.

**F-045** — ESG investing (Environmental, Social, Governance): ESG factors incorporated into investment analysis; assets in ESG-labeled strategies: approximately $20–30 trillion globally (2022, GSIA) — definitions vary widely and greenwashing is widespread; evidence on ESG alpha: mixed — some periods and strategies show outperformance, others show underperformance or no difference from non-ESG; the theoretical mechanisms: ESG companies may have lower regulatory/reputational risk (alpha from risk reduction); flows to ESG assets may inflate their prices temporarily (but subsequent returns should be lower); ESG exclusions limit the investable universe (cost). The most consistent finding: high-ESG portfolios tend to have lower volatility and beta than low-ESG portfolios.

**F-046** — The 1987 stock market crash (Black Monday, October 19, 1987): the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6% in a single day — the largest single-day percentage drop in history; global markets fell between 15% and 45%; the crash was triggered by a combination of program trading, portfolio insurance (dynamic hedging strategies that required selling stocks/futures as markets fell — a procyclical liquidity spiral), overvaluation, and rising interest rates; the Brady Commission report (1988) identified program trading and portfolio insurance as amplifiers; US markets recovered to pre-crash levels within 2 years; the SEC subsequently implemented circuit breakers to halt trading during extreme declines.

**F-047** — The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) in financial markets: a measure of market concentration = Σ(market share)²; in antitrust: < 1,500 = unconcentrated, 1,500–2,500 = moderately concentrated, > 2,500 = highly concentrated; relevant to: banking concentration (the top 4 US banks hold >40% of deposits), asset management concentration (top 3 passive managers own ~20% of S&P 500 companies and vote as a bloc), exchange concentration (NYSE + Nasdaq Group control ~95% of US equity volume); the growing concentration of passive ownership raises questions about corporate governance (passive managers cannot exit a company; they must be active in governance — the "stewardship" debate).

**F-048** — Financial repression: a set of policies that transfer wealth from savers to borrowers (typically governments) by keeping real interest rates artificially below their market level; mechanisms: below-inflation nominal interest rate ceilings, directed credit to government, capital controls preventing investment abroad, high reserve requirements; used historically to reduce large government debt-to-GDP ratios (UK and US after WWII reduced debt ratios from >100% to <50% through a combination of financial repression and growth); the period 2008–2022 (near-zero or negative real rates) has been characterized by some economists as mild financial repression; ZIRP and NIRP policies transferred approximately $5 trillion from fixed-income savers to debtors globally over this period (McKinsey estimate).
