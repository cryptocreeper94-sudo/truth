## The Narrowing

There is a question that has been pulling at me for a long time. Not a new question — one of those that sits in the back of your understanding, unresolved, until the day it finally demands to be answered.

The commandment says: *Lo Tirtzach.*

That's it. Two words. Carved in stone. Given directly by Yahuah to Mosheh on the mountain. Not filtered through a council. Not translated by a committee. Not edited by an institution. Given.

And the English version says: **Thou shalt not kill.**

Most people read that and immediately apply a limitation that isn't written there. They hear *kill humans.* They assume the commandment is about murder, about people, about the laws of men living among other men. And so it becomes manageable. It becomes something you can satisfy by simply not shooting your neighbor.

But the Hebrew doesn't say that.

*Ratsach* — the root of *Tirtzach* — means to kill. To slay. To take life. That's all it says. There is no qualifier. There is no carve-out. There is no asterisk that reads *"...except for animals, or in war, or when it's inconvenient."*

They added those.

---

### What the Word Actually Means

The Hebrew scriptures use several different words when they speak of killing. *Harag* — to kill in battle, to strike down an enemy. *Shachat* — to slaughter, used specifically for animal sacrifice. *Muth* — to die, to put to death. Each of these words exists. Each of them is specific. Each of them has a context.

And then there is *ratsach.*

When Yahuah wrote *Lo Tirtzach* on the stone tablets — not a scribe's copy, not a priest's interpretation, but the physical inscription made by the finger of the Creator Himself — He chose *ratsach*. Not *harag.* Not *shachat.* Not *muth.*

He chose the broadest possible word for taking life.

That is not an accident. The Creator of language, who spoke the world into existence, does not choose words carelessly.

---

### The Pen That Rewrote It

Here is where the discomfort sets in. Because if the commandment actually says what it says — if *Lo Tirtzach* means what the Hebrew means — then someone had to explain it away. Someone had to narrow it. Someone had to make it manageable for the system they were building.

Yirmeyahu (Jeremiah) 8:8 says it plainly:

*"How do ye say, We are wise, and the Torah of Yahuah is with us? Lo, certainly in vain made he it; the lying pen of the scribes is lying."*

The **lying pen of the scribes.**

This is not a fringe accusation. This is not an outside critic attacking the text. This is a prophet of Yahuah, speaking under inspiration, telling his own people: the scribes have falsified it. The men who were entrusted with copying, preserving, and teaching the Torah — the men whose entire identity was built around their knowledge of the law — had used their position to change what was written.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. Carefully. Narrowing here. Clarifying there. Adding a word. Adjusting an interpretation. Building a tradition of explanation around the text until the explanation became the authority and the original text became secondary to what the scholars said it meant.

This is the mechanism. This is how you rewrite a commandment without destroying the stone tablet. You leave the tablet alone. You corrupt the teaching around it.

---

### What Else Was Narrowed

If you accept that the scribes had the motive, the access, and the documented history of corruption — and Yirmeyahu tells us they did — then the logical question becomes: what else did they narrow?

Consider what Yahuah says about animal sacrifice. Not what the sacrificial system in Leviticus says. What Yahuah Himself says, in His own words, through His prophets:

Yirmeyahu (Jeremiah) 7:22:
*"For I spake not unto your fathers, nor commanded them in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices."*

He said He didn't command it.

Yeshayahu (Isaiah) 1:11:
*"To what purpose is the multitude of your sacrifices unto me? saith Yahuah: I am full of the burnt offerings of rams, and the fat of fed beasts; and I delight not in the blood of bullocks, or of lambs, or of he goats."*

He said He doesn't want it.

Tehilliym (Psalm) 50:10-13:
*"For every beast of the forest is mine, and the cattle upon a thousand hills. I know all the fowls of the mountains: and the wild beasts of the field are mine. If I were hungry, I would not tell thee: for the world is mine, and the fullness thereof. Will I eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats?"*

He said it makes no sense that He would ask for it — because He already owns it all.

This is Yahuah speaking. Not a prophet's opinion. Not a philosophical argument. The Creator of those animals telling you plainly: *I didn't ask you to kill them.*

And yet the sacrificial system became one of the central pillars of the religious structure. Animals by the thousands. Blood on altars. Priests with a monopoly on the process. A system that required the animal, required the priest, required the Temple — and made the common person completely dependent on an institution for their access to the Most High.

That's the pattern. That's always the pattern.

---

### Blood Is Sacred

Here is the piece that most people never sit with long enough:

Blood is life.

Not symbolically. Literally. Vayiqra (Leviticus) 17:14 says: *"For it is the life of all flesh; the blood of it is for the life thereof."* The Hebrew word is *nephesh* — the same word used for the living soul of a human being. The same soul. The same life-force. The blood of an animal carries nephesh. It is not a lower grade of life. It is life.

This is why *Lo Tirtzach* carries no qualifier. Because life is life. Yahuah doesn't create throwaway creatures. He doesn't make categories of beings whose death is insignificant. He made living things with nephesh in their blood, and then He wrote — in stone, with His own hand — *do not kill.*

The system needed that to not mean what it means. An entire economy of sacrifice, an entire institutional religion, an entire framework of priestly power — none of it works if *Lo Tirtzach* means what it says.

So the lying pen narrowed it.

---

### Free Will and the Knowing

I want to be clear about something. This is not me telling you what to believe. That would make me no different from every institution I've spent this entire book questioning.

Free will is a gift. It is also a responsibility. Yahuah gave it to you not as a leash but as a key — the ability to look at what you've been handed and decide for yourself whether it's true. Most people never use that key because they were never told they had it. They were given a tradition and told the tradition was the truth, and the difference between the two was never explained.

What I'm asking you to do is use the key.

Look at the Hebrew. Look at what *ratsach* means. Look at what Yirmeyahu 8:8 says the scribes did. Look at what Yahuah Himself says about burnt offerings through three separate prophets. And then decide.

Not what I think. Not what the institution thinks. What the text actually says.

That's what this entire journey has been — learning to read what's there instead of what I was told was there. And the more I clear away the layers of interpretation, the simpler and more coherent it becomes.

The commandment was given clearly. Life is sacred — all of it. The institution needed it not to be. So the lying pen did what it was paid to do.

What they could not change is the stone. And the stone still says: *Lo Tirtzach.*
