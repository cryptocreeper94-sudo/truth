# C-0045 — Dead Sea Scrolls vs Masoretic Text: Specific Variants

## The claim
The Dead Sea Scrolls, discovered between 1947 and 1956 in the Qumran Caves near the Dead Sea, present several textual variants when compared to the Masoretic Text (MT), the authoritative Hebrew text of the Jewish Bible. A significant example of this is found in the Book of Isaiah, where certain passages in the Dead Sea Scrolls align more closely with the Septuagint (LXX), an ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, than with the MT. This suggests the existence of different textual traditions during the Second Temple period. 

One notable variant is found in Isaiah 53:11, where the text in the Dead Sea Scrolls reads, "out of the suffering of his soul he will see light," specifically in the manuscript 1QIsa<sup>a</sup>, which is housed in the Shrine of the Book at the Israel Museum in Jerusalem. In contrast, the Masoretic Text, as represented in the Leningrad Codex (Catalog No. Firkovich B 19 A), held at the National Library of Russia in Saint Petersburg, omits the word "light" entirely. This discrepancy exemplifies the textual diversity that existed in ancient Jewish communities and provides critical insights into the development of the Hebrew Bible. 

## The evidence
The Dead Sea Scrolls, particularly the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa<sup>a</sup>), are cataloged at the Shrine of the Book, Israel Museum, Jerusalem. The manuscript can be accessed online at [1QIsa-a](https://www.imj.org.il/en/content/shrine-book). The relevant passage of Isaiah 53:11 is located on the scroll, and the specific reading can be found in the published editions of the scrolls, such as "The Dead Sea Scrolls: A New Translation" by Michael Wise, Martin Abegg, and Edward Cook (1996, p. 178).

The Masoretic Text is represented by the Leningrad Codex, which is the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, dated to 1008 CE. It is cataloged as Firkovich B 19 A at the National Library of Russia, Saint Petersburg, and can be viewed online at [Leningrad Codex](https://nlr.ru/eng/exib/Codex/). The omission of "light" in Isaiah 53:11 can be verified by comparing the relevant section of the Leningrad Codex (p. 681 in the printed edition).

These textual variants underscore the diversity of religious thought and textual transmission practices among Jewish communities prior to the establishment of the MT as the standard text.

## Objection at full strength
A strong counter-argument to the claim of significant textual variance is the assertion that the Masoretic Text represents a more stable and authoritative tradition, reflecting a final form of the Hebrew Scriptures that was widely accepted by Jewish communities. Scholars such as Emanuel Tov argue that the MT was systematically preserved and transmitted, which may indicate that the variants found in the Dead Sea Scrolls reflect earlier, perhaps less standardized, textual forms rather than offering a fundamentally different tradition (Tov, Emanuel. "Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible," 2012, pp. 150-152). Additionally, some variants in the Dead Sea Scrolls may be attributed to scribal errors or intentional adaptations rather than indicative of a distinct theological or literary tradition.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, further evidence would be needed, such as additional manuscript discoveries that consistently support the textual variants observed in the Dead Sea Scrolls compared to the Masoretic Text across multiple biblical books. A thorough comparative analysis of other biblical texts from the Dead Sea Scrolls that align with the Septuagint could also bolster the argument for a distinct textual tradition. Conversely, if new evidence were to emerge demonstrating that the variants in the Dead Sea Scrolls are primarily the result of scribal errors or that they do not significantly alter the theological content of the passages, this could lead to a downgrade in the confidence tag.
