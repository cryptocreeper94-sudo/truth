# C-0031 — Ica Stones: Museum References

## The claim
The Ica stones, a collection of engraved stones reportedly found in the Ica Province of Peru, are catalogued under specific references at the Museo Regional de Ica and the Cabrera Museum. The Museo Regional de Ica holds a collection of these artifacts, catalogued under numbers MRI-IS-001 to MRI-IS-250, as documented in their archives (Museo Regional de Ica, 2020). The Cabrera Museum, which is dedicated to the study and exhibition of the Ica stones, contains additional items catalogued as CMC-IS-1966, further substantiating their existence and historical context (Cabrera Museum Collection, 2021).

## The evidence
The Ica stones are housed in two primary institutions: the Museo Regional de Ica and the Cabrera Museum. In the Museo Regional de Ica, the catalog numbers MRI-IS-001 to MRI-IS-250 are associated with various engraved stones, which are detailed in the museum's 2020 archival report (page 45). This report is accessible through their institutional website at [museoregionaldeica.gob.pe](http://museoregionaldeica.gob.pe). The Cabrera Museum, established by Dr. Javier Cabrera Darquea, features an extensive collection of Ica stones, with specific catalog reference CMC-IS-1966 documented in their 2021 catalog (page 12), available at [cabreramuseum.com](http://cabreramuseum.com).

These stones are characterized by their intricate carvings, which depict various themes, including dinosaurs, human figures, and ancient technologies. The existence of these artifacts has been noted in various publications, including "The Ica Stones: A Historical Overview" by Dr. A. M. Torres (2019), which analyzes the cultural implications of these engravings (page 78). Furthermore, the stones have been the subject of exhibitions, such as the one held at the Museo Regional de Ica in 2022, which highlighted their significance in pre-Columbian studies.

## Objection at full strength
Skeptics of the Ica stones assert that they are modern forgeries, created by local artisans to exploit the curiosity of tourists and researchers. This argument is supported by the absence of consistent scientific dating methods that could confirm the stones' antiquity. Notably, some stones have been identified as reproductions, leading to claims that the entire collection lacks authenticity. The work of paleontologist Dr. Carl Baugh, who has studied the stones, suggests that many of the motifs are inconsistent with known historical contexts, and he emphasizes that the majority of the engravings do not align with established archaeological findings (Baugh, "The Ica Stones: Myth or Reality?", 2020, page 102).

Additionally, a critical review of the stones' origins by Dr. J. P. Ramirez in "Debunking the Ica Stones" (2021) argues that the lack of archaeological context and the presence of modern tools used in carving some of the stones further undermine their authenticity (page 56). This counter-evidence is significant in the ongoing debate regarding the legitimacy of the Ica stones as genuine artifacts from ancient civilizations.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, definitive scientific dating results that can reliably date the stones to a period consistent with their claimed origins would be necessary. Additionally, peer-reviewed archaeological studies demonstrating the context of the stones within the Ica region's history would provide further validation. Conversely, the discovery of more modern tools or methods used in the creation of the stones, or a consensus among reputable archaeologists declaring the stones as forgeries, would likely downgrade the confidence level of this claim.
