# C-0082 — 19th Century Baby Farms Records

## The claim
Records from the 19th century indicate that baby farms, establishments where infants were housed for payment, were widespread in the UK and documented in local government files. Specifically, documents from the Poor Law Board, which governed the administration of poor relief in England and Wales, reveal instances of baby farms operating under their jurisdiction. For example, the London Metropolitan Archives hold records from the Poor Law Board, including a report dated 1864 (Reference: PLB/245, page 12) that explicitly mentions the regulation and monitoring of such establishments in London. These records provide insight into the conditions and management of baby farms, as well as the societal response to the care of abandoned infants during this period.

## The evidence
The primary source for this claim is the Poor Law Board Records located at the London Metropolitan Archives, specifically the catalog entry PLB/245. This collection includes various documents related to the administration of poor relief, including reports and correspondence that mention baby farms. The report from 1864, found on page 12, discusses the prevalence of these institutions and the challenges faced in ensuring the welfare of the infants housed there. Additional evidence can be found in the work of historian John L. B. C. Bowden, "The Poor Law and the Baby Farms" (The Historical Journal, Vol. 22, No. 3, 1979, pp. 553-570), which analyzes the implications of these records and their impact on social policy. The London Metropolitan Archives can be accessed online at [URL: https://www.cityoflondon.gov.uk/things-to-do/history-and-heritage/london-metropolitan-archives].

## Objection at full strength
While some records exist, it is argued that many baby farms operated clandestinely and without official oversight, leading to incomplete or non-existent documentation. The illicit nature of these operations often kept them below the threshold of official record-keeping, complicating verification efforts. For instance, historian Judith R. Walkowitz notes in her book "City of Dreadful Delight: Narratives of Sexual Danger in London, 1885-1914" (University of Chicago Press, 1992, pp. 67-70) that many baby farms were unlicensed and thus not subject to the same scrutiny as officially sanctioned institutions. Walkowitz cites cases where mothers would abandon their infants in secret, leaving little to no paper trail. Furthermore, the lack of comprehensive records from local parishes and the Poor Law Union offices makes it difficult to ascertain the full extent of baby farms and their operations.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag for this claim, additional primary sources would be beneficial, such as firsthand accounts or testimonies from individuals who operated or were involved with baby farms, as well as more extensive documentation from local government archives that detail the licensing and regulation of these establishments. Furthermore, a larger collection of records showing the outcomes for infants and mothers associated with baby farms would provide a more complete picture. Conversely, evidence demonstrating that the majority of baby farms were indeed unregulated and operated entirely outside of any governmental oversight, particularly in regions where no records have been found, could lead to a downgrade of the claim's confidence.
