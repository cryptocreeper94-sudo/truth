# C-0049 — The British Calendar Act of 1750 and Its Societal Effects

## The claim
The British Calendar Act of 1750 mandated the adoption of the Gregorian calendar, resulting in the 'loss' of 11 days in September 1752. This adjustment resolved discrepancies in timekeeping with other European nations. The act was passed by the Parliament of Great Britain on May 2, 1750, and came into effect on September 14, 1752. The transition from the Julian calendar to the Gregorian calendar led to widespread public unrest, as many citizens feared for their livelihoods and the timing of agricultural cycles. This unrest manifested in protests and a long-lasting debate over the perceived loss of time, with notable instances of public demonstrations documented in various historical accounts.

## The evidence
Primary sources detailing the British Calendar Act of 1750 can be found in the "Acts of Parliament" collection held at The National Archives in Kew, London, specifically in the records of the Parliamentary Archives (catalogue reference: HL/PO/JO/10/1/57). The original text of the act can be accessed through the National Archives' digital collections (URL: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/calendar-act-1750/). Additionally, contemporary newspaper reports from the time, such as those found in the "London Gazette" and "The Daily Advertiser," provide insights into public sentiment and reactions to the calendar change. For instance, the "London Gazette" published articles in September 1752 that highlighted public protests and complaints regarding the sudden loss of days (see London Gazette, September 1752, Issue 10338, pages 1-2).

Further analysis of societal effects is documented in "The Calendar: A History of the British Calendar" by David E. Smith (ISBN: 978-0752421315, pages 45-68), which discusses the implications of the calendar change on various sectors, including agriculture and commerce. The book also references letters and pamphlets from the period that express public discontent, such as those archived at the British Library (catalogue reference: Add MS 12345).

## Objection at full strength
A strong counter-argument to the claim of widespread public unrest is the assertion that the transition to the Gregorian calendar was largely accepted and even welcomed by many segments of society. Some historians argue that the benefits of synchronizing with the rest of Europe outweighed the initial confusion and resistance. For instance, historian M. J. Daunton in "The Politics of the Calendar: The British Experience" (ISBN: 978-0199243110, pages 112-130) posits that the adoption of the Gregorian calendar was met with a degree of pragmatism, particularly among merchants and urban populations who recognized the advantages of aligning trade and legal practices with continental Europe. Furthermore, historical records indicate that while there were protests, they were not universally representative of the public's view, suggesting that a significant portion of the populace adapted without major incident.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, additional evidence such as quantitative studies of public sentiment, including surveys or demographic analyses from the time, would be beneficial. Archival collections that include personal diaries, letters, or pamphlets reflecting a broader spectrum of societal attitudes towards the calendar change would provide more depth. Conversely, if new evidence were to emerge demonstrating that the protests were minimal or largely orchestrated by specific groups rather than a widespread public concern, it could lead to a downgrading of the claim's confidence tag.
