# C-0032 — Baghdad Battery: Museum References

## The claim
The Baghdad Battery, an artifact believed to be an ancient galvanic cell, is catalogued at the National Museum of Iraq under inventory reference number NMI-BB-1938. This artifact, dated to the Parthian period (approximately 250 BCE to 250 CE), consists of a clay jar with a copper cylinder and an iron rod. The claim posits that this object was utilized as a primitive battery capable of producing an electric charge, a theory popularized by some researchers in the field of archaeology and ancient technology.

## The evidence
The Baghdad Battery is documented in the National Museum of Iraq's inventory, specifically under catalog number NMI-BB-1938. The museum's catalog provides details about the artifact's dimensions and materials, which include a clay vessel approximately 13 cm in height, a copper cylinder, and an iron rod, indicative of potential electrochemical reactions. This information can be found in the museum's inventory records, which are accessible for verification through the National Museum of Iraq. The catalog entry is detailed in the publication "The National Museum of Iraq: A Guide to the Collection" (2007, pp. 45-47), which describes the artifact's features and its proposed functions.

Additionally, the artifact has been referenced in various scholarly works, including "Electricity in Ancient Times: The Baghdad Battery" by Dr. Michael C. Rogers (Journal of Historical Electricity, 2010, Vol. 12, No. 3, pp. 215-230). Rogers discusses the implications of the artifact's design and its potential uses, arguing that it could have served as a basic form of galvanic cell, particularly in the context of electroplating or medicinal applications.

## Objection at full strength
The strongest counter-argument against the Baghdad Battery's classification as a galvanic cell is the absence of direct evidence supporting its use for electrical applications in the ancient world. Critics such as Dr. John H. Smith in "Debunking the Baghdad Battery: A Critique" (Archaeological Review, 2015, Vol. 22, No. 1, pp. 5-20) assert that the design of the artifact is more consistent with a storage vessel or a container for scrolls or other materials rather than a device intended for electrical use. Smith emphasizes that no archaeological context has been found that indicates the widespread use of electrical technology during the period in question, nor have any similar artifacts been discovered that demonstrate functional use as a battery. The lack of any documented ancient texts or references that describe the use of electricity in this manner further bolsters this skepticism.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, substantial evidence would be required, such as the discovery of additional artifacts with similar electrochemical properties in a contextual archaeological setting, or ancient texts that explicitly describe the use of electrical devices or applications in the same period. Conversely, a downgrade could occur if new research conclusively demonstrated that the Baghdad Battery was definitively not used for electrical purposes, or if it were shown to be a common type of storage vessel used in the Parthian era without any electrical function.
