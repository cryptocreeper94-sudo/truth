# C-0038 — Greek Deucalion Flood Narrative

## The claim
The Greek flood narrative involving Deucalion is documented in several ancient texts, notably in "Library" by Apollodorus (circa 1st-2nd century CE) and "Metamorphoses" by Ovid (8 CE). In these accounts, Deucalion and his wife Pyrrha survive a catastrophic flood sent by Zeus as a punishment to humanity, which leads to their role as the progenitors of a new human race. This narrative exhibits thematic similarities with Near Eastern flood accounts, such as the Epic of Gilgamesh and the biblical story of Noah's Ark.

## The evidence
The primary sources for the Deucalion flood narrative are:

1. **Apollodorus, "Library"**: This text, particularly in Book 1, Chapter 7, details the flood narrative, describing how Zeus decided to flood the earth due to the wickedness of humankind and how Deucalion and Pyrrha were instructed to construct a chest to survive the deluge. The manuscript is cataloged under the reference "Apollodorus, Bibliotheca," available in various editions, including the one published by Harvard University Press (Loeb Classical Library, 1921; ISBN: 9780674991905, pp. 37-39). The text can be accessed through the Loeb Classical Library online database.

2. **Ovid, "Metamorphoses"**: In Book 1, Ovid recounts the story of Deucalion and Pyrrha, emphasizing the themes of rebirth and the renewal of humanity following the flood. The relevant passage can be found in Ovid's "Metamorphoses," translated by A.D. Melville (Oxford University Press, 1986; ISBN: 9780198147680, pp. 1-3). The text is available online via the Perseus Digital Library (Perseus.tufts.edu).

3. **Comparative Texts**: The flood narrative in Greek literature shares significant similarities with Near Eastern accounts. For instance, the Epic of Gilgamesh (circa 2100 BCE), particularly Tablet XI, describes Utnapishtim, who is warned of an impending flood and builds a boat to save himself and various living beings. This text is preserved in various cuneiform tablets, with significant editions available in the "Epic of Gilgamesh" translated by Andrew George (Penguin Classics, 1999; ISBN: 9780140441006, pp. 91-104). The biblical account of Noah's Ark, found in Genesis 6-9, also presents a flood narrative with parallels to Deucalion's story.

## Objection at full strength
A strong counter-argument to the claim of a direct connection between the Greek flood narrative and Near Eastern accounts is the assertion that the similarities may arise from common mythological themes rather than direct influence or transmission. Critics might argue that flood myths are a widespread motif in human cultures, emerging independently as responses to local catastrophic events, such as flooding. For example, David Leeming, in "The World of Myth" (Oxford University Press, 1990; ISBN: 9780195080814, pp. 149-152), discusses the universality of flood myths across various cultures without establishing a direct lineage or borrowing among them.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, it would be beneficial to find archaeological evidence linking Greek flood narratives directly to Near Eastern cultures or to establish a clearer timeline of cultural exchange that demonstrates how these narratives influenced one another. Conversely, a downgrade in confidence could occur if new scholarship robustly demonstrates that the similarities between these narratives are purely coincidental and arise from independent myth-making traditions without any historical interaction.
