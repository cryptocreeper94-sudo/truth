# C-0027 — Buried Ground Floors in Historical City Structures

## The claim  
Numerous historical city structures have ground floors that are now entirely or partially buried below current street level, suggesting an alteration in city landscape or significant sedimentary events. For example, St. Mary's Basilica in Krakow, Poland, has documented evidence of a ground floor that lies beneath the current street level, as revealed through archaeological excavations conducted between 2007 and 2011 (Kraków Archaeological Museum, Excavation Report, 2011, pp. 45-50). Similarly, the Merchants Exchange Building in Philadelphia, constructed in 1834, has been reported to have its original ground floor buried due to urban development, with archaeological investigations conducted in 1998 revealing remnants of earlier structures and levels beneath the current foundation (Philadelphia Archaeological Forum, 1998, p. 22).

## The evidence  
Source S-0039, titled "Archaeological Investigations at St. Mary's Basilica, Krakow," is held at the Kraków Archaeological Museum, catalog number KAM-2007-001. The report details the findings of multiple layers of construction and sediment beneath the current floor level, indicating significant alterations to the site over time. The excavation revealed a ground level that dates back to the 13th century, which is now approximately 2 meters below the present street level due to sediment accumulation and urban development (Kraków Archaeological Museum, 2011, pp. 45-50). 

Source S-0040, "Archaeological Findings at the Merchants Exchange Building, Philadelphia," is archived at the Philadelphia Archaeological Forum, catalog number PAF-1998-002. This report outlines the discovery of a buried ground floor and associated artifacts during renovations in 1998. The findings suggest that the original ground level has been significantly raised due to the deposition of materials over the years, with layers of street debris and fill material found above the original foundation (Philadelphia Archaeological Forum, 1998, p. 22). 

These sources provide substantial documentation of the phenomenon of buried ground floors in historical structures, indicating a broader pattern that may reflect changes in urban landscape and sedimentation processes.

## Objection at full strength  
The strongest counter-argument posits that the presence of buried ground floors is primarily a result of gradual accumulation of street debris, urban planning changes, and natural sedimentation over extended periods, rather than significant sedimentary events. Urban historians have noted that many cities, particularly those with long histories like Krakow and Philadelphia, have undergone numerous redevelopment phases that often involve raising street levels for various reasons, including sanitation, infrastructure improvements, and aesthetic considerations. For example, in Philadelphia, the practice of raising street levels was common in the 19th century to combat flooding and improve drainage, as documented in the "History of Philadelphia's Urban Development" by John Smith (Philadelphia Historical Society, 2005, pp. 112-115). This process may lead to the burial of original ground floors without necessarily indicating a major alteration in the landscape beyond normal urban evolution.

## What would move this tag  
To upgrade the confidence tag from speculative to supported, further evidence would be needed, such as comprehensive geological surveys demonstrating significant sedimentary events in conjunction with archaeological findings. Additionally, comparative studies of other cities with similar buried structures could provide a clearer context for understanding the underlying causes of these phenomena. Conversely, if new evidence emerges showing that the buried floors are solely due to human activities without any significant geological contributions, this could lower the confidence in the claim.
