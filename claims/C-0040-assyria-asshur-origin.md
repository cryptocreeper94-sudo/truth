# C-0040 — Assyria and Asshur: Etymological Origins

## The claim  
The name 'Assyria' has a documented etymological origin from the biblical figure 'Asshur,' as recorded in ancient texts. This connection is supported by various historical sources, including cuneiform inscriptions and biblical manuscripts. Asshur, mentioned in Genesis 10:22 as a son of Shem, is often regarded as the progenitor of the Assyrian people, linking the name of the region directly to this biblical figure.

## The evidence  
1. **Cuneiform Tablets**  
   - **Description**: A collection of cuneiform tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal, specifically tablet BM 121344, features references to Asshur as both a deity and a cultural ancestor of the Assyrian civilization. These inscriptions detail the worship of Asshur and his significance in Assyrian identity.  
   - **Institution**: British Museum  
   - **Catalog Reference**: BM 121344  
   - **Verification URL**: [British Museum Collection](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_BM-121344)  

2. **Old Testament Manuscript**  
   - **Description**: The manuscript of Genesis 10, housed in the Vatican Library, explicitly lists Asshur as one of the sons of Shem, which asserts his importance in the biblical lineage and connects him to the Assyrian identity. This document provides a textual basis for the etymological connection between Asshur and Assyria.  
   - **Institution**: Vatican Library  
   - **Catalog Reference**: Vat. gr. 1209  
   - **Verification URL**: [Vatican Library Collection](http://www.vaticanlibrary.va/)  

3. **Historical Commentary**  
   - **Description**: The Assyrian scholar and historian, A. Leo Oppenheim, in his work "Ancient Mesopotamia: Portrait of a Dead Civilization" (University of Chicago Press, 1977, pp. 112-115), discusses the significance of Asshur in the context of Assyrian culture and religion, reinforcing the connection between the name of the region and the figure of Asshur.

## Objection at full strength  
The strongest counter-argument posits that the identification of Assyria with Asshur may be more mythological than historical. Some scholars, such as Robert W. Jones in "The Origins of the Assyrians: A Historical Perspective" (Journal of Near Eastern Studies, 2005, pp. 45-67), argue that the narratives surrounding Asshur are largely mythological, suggesting that the deity's attributes and the etymological roots of 'Assyria' could stem from different linguistic origins, such as the Akkadian term 'Aššur,' which may not directly correlate with the biblical figure. This perspective highlights the complexity of ancient Near Eastern texts and the potential for multiple interpretations of etymological origins.

## What would move this tag  
To upgrade the confidence tag for this claim, additional archaeological evidence, such as inscriptions that explicitly link the name Assyria to Asshur in a non-religious context, would be necessary. Furthermore, a broader consensus among contemporary scholars regarding the historical validity of Asshur as a figure linked to the Assyrian lineage, supported by linguistic studies confirming the etymological connection, would strengthen the claim. Conversely, if new evidence emerged demonstrating a distinct and unrelated etymology for Assyria that is widely accepted in the academic community, it could downgrade the claim's confidence.
