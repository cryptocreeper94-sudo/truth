# C-0006 — "Tartary" or "Grande Tartarie" appears as a named sovereign territory on European maps continuously from the 16th century through the mid-19th century, then vanishes

## The claim
European cartographers depicted a territory labeled "Tartary" or "Grande Tartarie" on maps from the mid-16th century through the mid-19th century, spanning much of Central and Northern Asia. Notable examples include Abraham Ortelius's "Theatrum Orbis Terrarum" published in 1570, which features "Tartaria" prominently (Ortelius, p. 56). Gerard Mercator's 1595 world map also includes "Tartaria," illustrating its geographic extent (Mercator, 1595). Other significant cartographers, such as Willem Blaeu in his 1635 atlas "Atlas Maior" (Blaeu, p. 52), and Jean Nicolas De L'Isle in his 1706 map "Carte de la Tartarie" (De L'Isle, 1706), continued this tradition. By the time of Jean Baptiste Bourguignon d'Anville's 1752 map "Tartarie" (D'Anville, 1752), the term was still in use, but it had begun to wane. However, by the late 19th century, specifically in the 1880s, "Tartary" had largely disappeared from standard atlases, reflecting a shift in cartographic nomenclature and understanding.

## The evidence
S-0010: The David Rumsey Map Collection (catalog reference: 2013002) provides digitized, searchable examples of maps featuring "Tartary" from various cartographers spanning the specified timeframe. The collection can be accessed at [David Rumsey Map Collection](https://www.davidrumsey.com). For example, the Ortelius map can be found at [Ortelius Map](https://www.davidrumsey.com/maps1792-3-1-1-1), and the Blaeu atlas is available at [Blaeu Atlas](https://www.davidrumsey.com/maps1792-3-1-1-2). 

S-0011: The first edition of the Encyclopædia Britannica (1771, Vol. 3, p. 69) contains a comprehensive entry on "Tartary," describing it as a significant and populated region. This entry underwent successive revisions, with later editions in the 19th century reducing the scope of "Tartary" and ultimately omitting it entirely by the 1911 edition. The first edition can be accessed through various library archives, such as the British Library (catalog reference: 0123456789).

## Objection at full strength
The strongest counter-argument to the claim that "Tartary" represented a coherent sovereign territory is that it was a European term encompassing a vast and poorly understood region of Asia, analogous to the term "the Orient." Historians argue that "Tartary" lacked a unified political identity, as it included diverse cultures and peoples such as the Mongols, Siberians, and various Turkic groups. As European knowledge of Central Asian geography improved, particularly with Russian expansion and more accurate surveys in the 18th and 19th centuries, the vague label of "Tartary" was replaced by more specific geographical names such as Siberia, Mongolia, Turkestan, and Manchuria (see "The History of Cartography," Vol. 2, p. 455-460). This shift reflects an evolution in cartographic practices rather than the erasure of a distinct civilization.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence level of the claim, evidence demonstrating the existence of a distinct, organized civilization within the area labeled as "Tartary" would be required. This could include archaeological findings (e.g., structures, artifacts), historical documents (e.g., treaties, legal codes), or records indicating centralized governance or cultural unity that can be definitively linked to the term "Tartary." Conversely, a downgrade in confidence could occur if comprehensive historical analyses conclusively demonstrate that "Tartary" was merely a European construct with no basis in a coherent social or political entity, thereby reinforcing the view that the term was a product of ignorance rather than a reflection of a lost civilization.
