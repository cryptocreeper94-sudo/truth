# C-0006 — "Tartary" on maps for 300+ years, then absent

## The claim
European cartographers — including Ortelius (1570), Mercator (1595), Blaeu (1635), De L'Isle (1706), and D'Anville (1752) — depicted a territory labeled "Tartary" or "Grande Tartarie" spanning much of Central and Northern Asia. The territory appears consistently across independently produced maps for over three centuries. By the late 19th century, the name no longer appears on any standard atlas.

## The evidence
S-0010: the David Rumsey Map Collection contains digitized, searchable examples across the full date range. S-0011: the Encyclopædia Britannica, 1st edition (1771), contains a substantial entry on "Tartary" describing it as a real, populated territory. Later editions progressively reduce and then eliminate the entry.

## The objection, at full strength
"Tartary" was never a single polity — it was a European catch-all term for the vast, poorly understood interior of Asia, much like "the Orient." As European knowledge of Central Asian geography improved (Russian expansion, better surveys), the vague label was replaced by specific names: Siberia, Mongolia, Turkestan, Manchuria, etc. This is the standard explanation in the history of cartography and is well-supported. The name vanishing reflects improved knowledge, not erasure of a civilization.

## What would move this tag
The cartographic fact (the name's presence and disappearance) is DOCUMENTED. Claims that Tartary was a *specific civilization that was deliberately erased* would be separate claims starting at SPECULATIVE and requiring Tier-1 evidence (architecture, coins, legal documents) beyond maps alone.
