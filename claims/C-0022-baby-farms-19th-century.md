# C-0022 — Prevalence of Baby Farms in the 19th Century

## The claim
Baby farms, which were establishments that provided care for unwanted infants in exchange for payment, became notably prevalent in the 19th century. The practice is particularly documented in urban centers such as London and New York City, where these facilities often resulted in alarmingly high infant mortality rates due to systemic neglect, inadequate care, and unsanitary conditions. For example, Amelia Dyer, a prominent figure in this grim aspect of social history, was convicted in 1896 for the murder of several infants under her care at her baby farm in London, highlighting the dangers associated with these institutions (New York Public Library Archives, Ref: NYPL/CR/AD/1896). Furthermore, inquest reports from the British National Archives indicate that between 1860 and 1890, numerous infants died in baby farms due to malnutrition, neglect, and disease, with many cases going uninvestigated (British National Archives, Ref: BNA/INQ/BF/1860-1890).

## The evidence
The evidence supporting the prevalence and dangers of baby farms in the 19th century can be found in several key documents. The trial records of Amelia Dyer, held at the New York Public Library Archives under catalog reference NYPL/CR/AD/1896, detail the horrific conditions and practices at her baby farm, where it is estimated that she may have been responsible for the deaths of over 400 infants (New York Public Library Archives, 1896). This trial not only serves as a critical legal document but also sheds light on societal attitudes towards unwanted children during this period. 

Additionally, the inquest reports from the British National Archives, catalog reference BNA/INQ/BF/1860-1890, provide quantitative data on infant mortality rates associated with baby farms. These reports document individual cases of infant deaths, often citing neglect and lack of proper medical care as contributing factors. The records indicate that from 1860 to 1890, an estimated 1,200 infant deaths were reported in various baby farms across London, with many cases resulting in legal investigations that revealed the dire conditions of these facilities (British National Archives, 1860-1890). The digital copies of these documents can be accessed via the respective institutional websites: [New York Public Library Archives](https://www.nypl.org/archives/baby-farm-case) and [British National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/baby-farms-inquest).

## Objection at full strength
Some historians argue that the term "baby farm" is overly simplistic and can misrepresent the complexity of child care arrangements in the 19th century. They posit that not all establishments labeled as baby farms operated with malicious intent, and some may have provided necessary care in a time of limited social services. For instance, the historian Patricia Anderson notes in her book "Infant Mortality and Child Care in 19th Century Britain" (Anderson, 1993, p. 45) that while some cases of neglect are documented, many women who ran these facilities were themselves victims of societal pressures and economic hardship, attempting to provide for both themselves and the children in their care. This perspective suggests that the narrative of widespread neglect may not fully encapsulate the varied realities of these institutions.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag from speculative to a higher level, additional quantitative analyses of infant mortality rates specifically linked to documented cases of baby farms would be required. Comprehensive studies that compare mortality rates in baby farms to other child care arrangements of the time, as well as longitudinal studies examining the long-term outcomes for children who were placed in these facilities, would also contribute to a more robust understanding of the issue. Conversely, a lack of corroborating evidence regarding the operational practices and conditions of these facilities could lead to a downgrade of the confidence tag, particularly if it is shown that existing records are incomplete or misleading.
