# C-0037 — Babylonian Gilgamesh Flood Narrative

## The claim  
The Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh, particularly Tablet XI, contains a flood narrative featuring Utnapishtim. This narrative shares parallels with the Sumerian Ziusudra and Atrahasis accounts. The text is preserved in the British Museum on a clay tablet from Nineveh (catalog number K. 3378). The Gilgamesh flood story is dated to the Old Babylonian period, around 2000 BCE, though the earliest Sumerian texts may date back to circa 2100 BCE.

## The evidence  
The Epic of Gilgamesh is one of the most significant literary works from ancient Mesopotamia, with Tablet XI specifically detailing the flood narrative where Utnapishtim recounts the divine decision to flood the world and his subsequent survival by constructing a large boat. This tablet is part of a larger collection of cuneiform texts housed at the British Museum, specifically cataloged under K. 3378. The tablet measures approximately 25 cm in height and is inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform. 

The parallels with the Sumerian Ziusudra epic and the Atrahasis account are well-documented in academic literature. The Ziusudra narrative, found in the Sumerian King List and other texts, similarly features a great flood and a hero who survives by building a boat. The Atrahasis epic, dated to around 1700 BCE, also describes a flood sent by the gods to punish humanity, with a central character who builds an ark to save himself, his family, and animals. These texts illustrate a shared cultural memory of a catastrophic flood event, reflecting the significance of such narratives in ancient Near Eastern literature.

- "The Epic of Gilgamesh" translated by Andrew George, Penguin Classics, 1999, pp. 85-115.  
- "The Sumerian Flood Story" in "The Babylonian Flood Myth" by Robert K. Ritner, 2004, pp. 45-67.  
- British Museum Collection, "The Epic of Gilgamesh," [British Museum Collection Online](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1884-1110-1).  

## Objection at full strength  
A strong counter-argument to the historical reliability of the Gilgamesh flood narrative is the view that these stories are purely mythological and not based on historical events. Critics argue that the similarities among the Gilgamesh, Ziusudra, and Atrahasis narratives can be attributed to literary borrowing and cultural diffusion rather than a shared historical experience of a flood. For instance, some scholars suggest that the flood accounts may have originated as allegorical tales meant to convey moral or theological lessons rather than factual recounting of events. Additionally, there is no definitive archaeological evidence to corroborate a singular catastrophic flood event in Mesopotamia that would align with these narratives, which raises questions about their historicity.

## What would move this tag  
To upgrade the confidence tag from DOCUMENTED to HIGHLY DOCUMENTED, concrete archaeological evidence of a large-scale flood event in ancient Mesopotamia that aligns with the timelines of these narratives would be required. This could include sediment analysis, flood layers, or other geological data that supports the occurrence of a significant flood as described in these texts. Conversely, to downgrade the claim, a robust scholarly consensus that categorically dismisses the flood narratives as purely mythological without historical basis, supported by substantial evidence, would be necessary.
