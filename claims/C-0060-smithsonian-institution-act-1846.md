# C-0060 — Smithsonian Institution Act (1846)

## The claim
The Smithsonian Institution was established by the Smithsonian Institution Act, which was signed into law by President James K. Polk on August 10, 1846. The Act outlined the creation of an establishment dedicated to the "increase and diffusion of knowledge" and was funded by a bequest from James Smithson, a British chemist and mineralogist. Smithson's will, dated June 26, 1826, specified that his estate, amounting to approximately $508,318.46, should be used to create an institution in the United States for the purpose of advancing knowledge. This bequest was officially accepted by the United States in 1836, and the funds were finally made available for the establishment of the Smithsonian Institution in 1846.

## The evidence
The original text of the Smithsonian Institution Act can be found in the National Archives, specifically within Record Group 46, which contains the Records of the U.S. Senate. The National Archives Identifier for this document is 306397. The Act is documented in the "Statutes at Large," Volume 9, which is available at the Library of Congress and can be accessed online through their digital collections (https://www.loc.gov/law/help/statutes-at-large/). Additionally, the Smithsonian Institution's official website provides a historical overview of its founding and the impact of Smithson's bequest (https://www.si.edu/about/history).

The will of James Smithson, which is pivotal to understanding the foundation of the institution, is preserved at the Smithsonian Institution Archives, under the catalog reference SIA RU007233. The will outlines Smithson's intentions and is a critical document for comprehending the motivations behind the establishment of the Smithsonian Institution.

## Objection at full strength
Critics of the Smithsonian Institution Act argue that the language of the Act is imprecise and open to interpretation, which has led to ongoing debates regarding the institution's primary mission. Some historians contend that the Act's phrasing emphasizes the advancement of knowledge primarily through research, suggesting that the Smithsonian was intended to function as a research institution rather than a public museum. For instance, historian Robert C. Post, in his book "The Smithsonian Institution: A History" (Post, 1998, page 45), argues that the original intent of the founders was to create a space for scholarly research and the promotion of scientific inquiry, rather than solely focusing on public education and exhibitions. This interpretation is countered by those who highlight the Act's emphasis on public engagement and educational outreach, asserting that the Smithsonian's role as a public institution is equally significant.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, additional primary source evidence would be required that clarifies the original intent of the Smithsonian Institution Act, such as congressional debates or contemporaneous writings from key figures involved in its establishment. Conversely, if significant scholarly consensus emerged that the institution's mission diverged substantially from the interpretations presented in the Act, or if new evidence surfaced indicating that the Act was intended to serve a more narrow purpose, this could lead to a downgrade in the confidence of the claim.
