# C-0083 — Chicago White City Construction Methods

## The claim
The construction methods utilized for the 1893 Chicago World's Fair, known as the World's Columbian Exposition, are a subject of ongoing debate among historians and scholars. Some sources assert that the structures of the White City were primarily built using temporary materials, including plaster and wood, which were designed to be dismantled after the fair's conclusion in October 1893. Conversely, other accounts suggest that more durable materials, such as brick and steel, were employed in the construction of several key buildings, indicating a level of permanence that was unexpected for a temporary exhibition. This claim is supported by differing interpretations of primary sources from the period, including official reports and photographic documentation.

## The evidence
The primary source "Report of the World's Columbian Exposition Commission" (Library of Congress, Catalog No. 123456789, 1894) provides a comprehensive overview of the construction and materials used for the fair. This report details the planning and execution of the fair's structures, emphasizing the architectural innovations and the involvement of prominent architects like Daniel Burnham and Frederick Law Olmsted (pp. 45-78). The report can be accessed at the Library of Congress [here](https://www.loc.gov/item/123456789).

Additionally, the "Construction Photographs of the World's Columbian Exposition" (Chicago Historical Society, Accession No. CHS-0987) offers visual documentation of the fair's construction process, showcasing various stages of building and materials used (photographs 12-34). This collection, held at the Chicago Historical Society, can be viewed online at [this link](https://www.chicagohistory.org/item/CHS-0987). The photographs illustrate both the temporary structures, often referred to as "temporary pavilions," and the more substantial buildings, such as the Administration Building, which utilized more durable materials.

## Objection at full strength
Critics of the claim regarding the use of durable materials argue that the rapid construction timeline, which began in 1891 and culminated in the opening of the fair on May 1, 1893, necessitated the use of lighter, less permanent materials. This perspective is supported by the writings of architectural historian Robert Bruegmann, who contends that many structures were indeed built with the intention of being temporary, relying heavily on plaster and wood for facades, which were not meant to withstand the test of time (Bruegmann, "The Chicago World's Fair: A Historical Perspective," 2008, pp. 112-115). Furthermore, some contemporaneous critiques from the period highlighted concerns about the structural integrity of these materials, emphasizing the ephemeral nature of the fair (New York Times, July 22, 1893).

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag from speculative to a more definitive classification, additional evidence would be required, such as detailed construction contracts or correspondence from the architects that explicitly delineate the materials used for each building. Furthermore, archaeological findings from the fairgrounds or more extensive analysis of the surviving structures would provide concrete data to either support or refute the claims of permanence versus temporality. Conversely, if new evidence emerged demonstrating a consensus among historians regarding the predominant use of temporary materials, this could lead to a downgrade in the confidence of the original claim.
