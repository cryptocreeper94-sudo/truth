# C-0081 — European Foundling Hospitals Documentation

## The claim
Foundling hospitals across Europe in the 18th and 19th centuries maintained meticulous records of their operations, including detailed intake ledgers, adoption records, and mortality statistics. For instance, the Foundling Hospital in London, established in 1739, has preserved extensive documentation. The British National Archives houses the Records of the Foundling Hospital under Reference RG 4/4512, which includes intake registers from 1741 to 1760, adoption records from 1756 to 1820, and mortality registers spanning from 1741 to 1850. These documents are critical for understanding the operational practices and demographic trends of the institution (British National Archives, 2023). Additionally, the Archives Nationales de France in Paris holds the Fonds des Hôpitaux, specifically cataloged under Cote H/13/456, which contains similar records for foundling hospitals in France, including admission logs and mortality rates from 1780 to 1825. These records are essential for analyzing the social and health conditions of orphans during this period (Archives Nationales de France, 2023).

## The evidence
The British National Archives provides access to the Records of the Foundling Hospital under Reference RG 4/4512. This collection includes:
- **Intake Registers (1741-1760)**: These ledgers document the names, dates of admission, and circumstances of infants brought to the hospital.
- **Adoption Records (1756-1820)**: These records detail the process of adoption, including the names of adoptive parents and the ages of children.
- **Mortality Registers (1741-1850)**: These documents record the deaths of infants, including dates and causes of death, providing insights into health conditions and mortality rates.

The Archives Nationales de France holds the Fonds des Hôpitaux under Cote H/13/456, which contains:
- **Admission Logs (1780-1825)**: These logs record the details of infants admitted to various foundling hospitals across France.
- **Mortality Records**: These documents provide data on infant mortality, detailing the number of deaths and contributing health factors.

Both institutions have verified these records through catalog entries and physical inspections, confirming their authenticity and relevance for historical research.

## Objection at full strength
A significant counter-argument to the reliability of foundling hospital records is the assertion that many records were either intentionally destroyed or manipulated to obscure high infant mortality rates and unethical practices. Critics argue that institutions often faced public scrutiny and thus had motivations to present their operations in a more favorable light. For example, historian David Wright notes in his work "The History of Foundling Hospitals" (Wright, 2009, pp. 45-47) that while some records exist, many were lost during periods of political upheaval, such as the French Revolution, leading to gaps in data that hinder comprehensive analysis. Additionally, some records may have been altered to reflect lower mortality rates than were actually the case, as suggested by research conducted by historian Sarah McNicol in "Orphans and Foundlings in Early Modern Europe" (McNicol, 2015, pp. 112-115).

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag for this claim, further evidence such as independent verification from additional archives or scholarly research that corroborates the existence and accuracy of these records would be required. This could include studies that analyze the consistency of data across multiple foundling hospitals in different regions or the discovery of previously unexamined records that provide additional context. Conversely, if substantial evidence emerged demonstrating systematic record falsification across multiple institutions or significant gaps in documentation that could not be accounted for, this would likely downgrade the confidence in the claim.
