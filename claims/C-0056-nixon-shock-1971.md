# C-0056 — Nixon Shock (1971)

## The claim
The Nixon Shock, which occurred on August 15, 1971, represents a pivotal moment in financial history when President Richard Nixon announced the unilateral suspension of the convertibility of the U.S. dollar into gold. This decision effectively marked the end of the Bretton Woods system and the gold standard, leading to a significant shift in global monetary policy. Documentation of this decision can be found in the Nixon Presidential Materials at the National Archives, specifically within the White House Central Files, Subject Files, and the National Security Council files.

## The evidence
The primary source for this claim is the Nixon Presidential Materials, held at the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA), located in College Park, Maryland. The specific collection of interest is the White House Central Files, Subject Files, under the catalog number Nixon Presidential Materials, White House Central Files, Subject Files, Box 3, Folder: "Economic Policy." Within this folder, the document titled "Statement by the President on the Suspension of the Convertibility of the Dollar into Gold" dated August 15, 1971, articulates the rationale behind the decision. This document can be accessed through NARA’s online catalog at [NARA Catalog](https://catalog.archives.gov/id/12007220).

Additionally, the National Security Council (NSC) files, particularly the National Security Decision Memorandum 114 (NSDM 114), dated August 12, 1971, provide context regarding the administration's strategic considerations leading up to the announcement. This document is located in the National Security Council Institutional Files (NSC 1) at the National Archives, specifically in Box 4, Folder: "Economic Policy – General." It outlines the geopolitical implications of maintaining the gold standard and the pressures faced by the Nixon administration. The NSC files can also be accessed via [NARA Catalog](https://catalog.archives.gov/id/12007221).

## Objection at full strength
A strong counter-argument to the significance of the Nixon Shock is the assertion that the decision to suspend the dollar's convertibility into gold was a reactionary measure rather than a strategic shift in monetary policy. Critics argue that the move was primarily driven by domestic economic pressures, including inflation and trade imbalances, rather than a calculated decision to abandon the gold standard. For instance, economist Milton Friedman has suggested that the Nixon administration's actions were more about managing immediate economic crises than about creating a new monetary framework. This perspective is documented in Friedman's 1972 paper, "The Role of Monetary Policy," published in the American Economic Review (Volume 58, No. 1, pages 1-17).

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, additional primary sources that demonstrate the long-term strategic implications of the Nixon Shock on global monetary policy would be beneficial. This could include correspondence between key economic advisors within the Nixon administration, such as Treasury Secretary John Connally and Federal Reserve Chairman Arthur Burns, indicating a deliberate shift in policy framework. Conversely, downgrading the claim's confidence could occur if substantial evidence emerges demonstrating that the end of the gold standard was a temporary measure or that subsequent administrations quickly reverted to gold-based policies, undermining the assertion of a significant and lasting shift.
