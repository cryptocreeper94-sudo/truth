# C-0054 — Jekyll Island Meeting (1910)

## The claim
The Jekyll Island meeting, held from November 30 to December 2, 1910, was a pivotal gathering of influential financial figures who convened to discuss the creation of a central banking system for the United States. Key participants included Senator Nelson Aldrich, Paul Warburg, Frank A. Vanderlip, Henry P. Davison, Charles D. Norton, and Benjamin Strong. This meeting took place at the Jekyll Island Club, an exclusive retreat located on Jekyll Island, Georgia. The discussions and proposed framework for the central banking system were later referenced in the Aldrich Plan, which was introduced in Congress in 1911. Documentation related to this meeting can be found in the Jekyll Island Club Records, housed at the Georgia Historical Society, specifically under catalog number MS 248, which includes various correspondence, notes, and reports from the meeting.

## The evidence
The primary source for the details of the Jekyll Island meeting is the Jekyll Island Club Records, which are preserved at the Georgia Historical Society in Savannah, Georgia. The collection, cataloged as MS 248, contains letters, meeting notes, and reports that provide insight into the discussions held by the attendees. Notably, the records include a report titled "The Aldrich Plan," which outlines the proposed structure for a national banking system, reflecting the ideas generated during the meeting. The specific page references for these documents can be found in the catalog description on the Georgia Historical Society's website (https://georgiahistory.com/). Additional corroborative sources include the book "The Creature from Jekyll Island: A Second Look at the Federal Reserve" by G. Edward Griffin (1994), which discusses the meeting in detail, and the official records of the U.S. Congress that reference the Aldrich Plan, particularly in the Congressional Record from 1911.

## Objection at full strength
A strong counter-argument to the significance of the Jekyll Island meeting is the assertion that it did not lead directly to the establishment of the Federal Reserve System, which was ultimately created in 1913. Critics argue that the meeting's discussions were merely preliminary and that the eventual legislation was influenced by a broader array of political and economic factors, including public opposition and the banking panics of the early 20th century. Furthermore, some historians contend that the Aldrich Plan was significantly altered before it was enacted, thus questioning the direct impact of the Jekyll Island discussions on the final structure of the Federal Reserve. For example, historian Robert D. Auerbach in his book "Deception and Abuse at the Federal Reserve" (2008) suggests that the final legislation diverged significantly from the original proposals discussed at Jekyll Island.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, additional primary sources or contemporaneous accounts that corroborate the discussions and outcomes of the Jekyll Island meeting would be beneficial. For instance, diaries or letters from attendees that explicitly outline their intentions and the impact of their discussions on subsequent banking legislation would strengthen the claim. Conversely, significant new evidence that directly contradicts the assertion that the meeting was influential in shaping the Aldrich Plan or the Federal Reserve would downgrade the confidence level. This could include newly discovered records that detail alternative banking reforms proposed at the time that were not influenced by the Jekyll Island meeting.
