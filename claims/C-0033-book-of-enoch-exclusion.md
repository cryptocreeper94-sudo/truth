# C-0033 — Exclusion of the Book of Enoch from Biblical Canon

## The claim
The Book of Enoch, an ancient Jewish text attributed to the patriarch Enoch, was excluded from the biblical canon by early church councils, notably the Council of Laodicea, which took place around 363-364 AD. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church, however, continues to include the Book of Enoch in its canon, as evidenced by its presence in the Ethiopian Bible, particularly the Ge'ez version. The exclusion of this text from the broader Christian biblical canon is often attributed to its controversial theological content, including its detailed descriptions of angelic hierarchies, apocalyptic visions, and themes of judgment and the afterlife, which were deemed inconsistent with emerging orthodox Christian beliefs.

## The evidence
The Book of Enoch is preserved in several manuscripts, with the most significant being the Ge'ez version, which is held in the British Library under catalog reference Or. 108. The text is also available in English translation in "The Book of Enoch: An Introduction" (R.H. Charles, 1917, pp. 1-2), which provides a comprehensive overview of its contents and historical context. The Council of Laodicea's decisions regarding the canon are documented in the "Canons of the Council of Laodicea" (c. 363-364 AD), which can be found in the "Apostolic Constitutions" (Book 7, Canon 59). The Ethiopian Orthodox Church's acceptance of the Book of Enoch is further corroborated by the "Ethiopic Bible" (published by the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church), which lists the Book of Enoch among its canonical texts. The text is also referenced in the "Pseudepigrapha of the Old Testament" (James H. Charlesworth, 1983, pp. 15-20), which discusses its significance and the reasons for its exclusion from the Jewish and Christian canons.

## Objection at full strength
A significant counter-argument against the claim of exclusion is that the Book of Enoch was never universally accepted as canonical by all Jewish or Christian communities. Some scholars argue that its exclusion was not merely a result of theological controversy but also due to its limited circulation and the lack of widespread acceptance in early Christian communities outside of Ethiopia. Additionally, texts such as the "Muratorian Fragment" (circa 170 AD) do not mention the Book of Enoch, suggesting that it was not considered authoritative by early church leaders. Furthermore, the absence of references to the Book of Enoch in the canonical texts of the New Testament could indicate a broader consensus against its authority, rather than a decision made solely by church councils.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag from SPECULATIVE to a higher level, further archaeological evidence of early Christian or Jewish manuscripts that explicitly mention the exclusion of the Book of Enoch would be necessary. Additionally, the discovery of early church documents or writings from church fathers that discuss the Book of Enoch's status in the canon would provide valuable insights. Conversely, if new evidence were to emerge showing that the Book of Enoch was widely accepted in early Christian communities outside of the Ethiopian Church, this could potentially downgrade the claim's confidence.
