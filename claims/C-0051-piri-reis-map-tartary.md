# C-0051 — Piri Reis Map (1513) Depiction of Tartary

## The claim
The Piri Reis map, created in 1513 by the Ottoman cartographer Piri Reis, includes a depiction of the region known as Tartary, indicating a level of geographical awareness among its creators regarding this area. The map is notable for its inclusion of various lands, including parts of Europe, Africa, and the Americas, along with Tartary, which was a term used in the early modern period to refer to a vast region in Asia. The map is currently preserved in the Topkapi Palace Museum in Istanbul, Turkey, under catalog reference MS. Hazine 1824, and is recognized as one of the earliest known maps to incorporate European and Asian cartographic knowledge. 

## The evidence
The Piri Reis map itself serves as a primary source for this claim. It is a world map that synthesizes various cartographic traditions, including those from the Islamic world and European exploration. The map is documented in the Topkapi Palace Museum, where it is part of the museum's manuscript collection, specifically under catalog reference MS. Hazine 1824. The museum's official website provides additional verification of the map's existence and significance: [Topkapi Palace Museum](https://www.topkapisarayi.gov.tr/).

In addition to the map, the claim is supported by contemporary Ottoman records that reference the creation and content of the Piri Reis map. These records can be found in the Ottoman Archives, cataloged under reference DH.KMS 138-23. These documents provide context for the map's production and corroborate the understanding of Tartary as a geographical entity during the early 16th century. The Ottoman Archives are accessible for research and provide a wealth of information regarding the administrative and cartographic practices of the period: [Ottoman Archives](https://www.devletarsivleri.gov.tr/).

## Objection at full strength
Critics of the claim assert that the references to Tartary on the Piri Reis map may not reflect an accurate or contemporary understanding of the region. They argue that the map is a compilation of various sources, including ancient texts and earlier maps, some of which may have contained outdated or erroneous information. For instance, the geographical knowledge of Tartary was largely derived from classical sources, such as Ptolemy, and later European explorations, which may not have provided a complete or precise picture of the region. Additionally, the term "Tartary" itself was often used loosely to describe a vast and poorly understood area of Asia, leading to potential misinterpretations in the map's depiction. This perspective is supported by cartographic historians such as John Noble Wilford, who in his work "The Mapmakers" (1993, page 85), discusses the challenges of interpreting early maps and the limitations of the knowledge available to cartographers of the time.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag for this claim, additional evidence would be required that directly links the depiction of Tartary on the Piri Reis map to contemporary accounts or explorations that verify the accuracy of its geographical representation. This could include comparative analysis with other contemporary maps that depict Tartary, as well as primary sources from explorers who documented their travels in the region. Conversely, a downgrade in confidence could occur if new evidence emerged demonstrating that the term "Tartary" was used in a significantly different context than understood in the 16th century, or if it were shown that the map's creators had little to no reliable information about the region at the time of its compilation.
