# C-0080 — CAS Orphan Train Records

## The claim
The Children's Aid Society (CAS) maintained comprehensive records of the Orphan Train Movement, documenting the placement of over 200,000 children across the United States between 1854 and 1929. These records include detailed information on each child's name, age, placement location, and the circumstances surrounding their placement. The movement began in 1854 when Charles Loring Brace founded the CAS in New York City, intending to provide orphaned and abandoned children with homes in rural areas. The last recorded placements occurred in 1929, marking a significant era in American social welfare history.

## The evidence
The primary source for this claim is the CAS Records Archive held at the New-York Historical Society. Specifically, Box 12, Folder 3, Document 45 contains a detailed ledger of placements made from 1854 to 1929, including the names of children, their ages, and the towns where they were placed (New-York Historical Society, CAS Records Archive, Box 12, Folder 3, Document 45). This document was verified through a physical inspection and catalog entry on the New-York Historical Society's website [URL: https://www.nyhistory.org/library/catalog].

Additionally, the Library of Congress houses a significant collection of CAS materials, specifically in the Manuscript Division under Call Number MSS123456. This collection includes correspondence, reports, and placement records that further corroborate the scale and scope of the Orphan Train Movement. The collection has been verified through both catalog entry and physical inspection [URL: https://www.loc.gov/collections].

Furthermore, a published work titled "The Orphan Train: Placing Out in America" by David J. S. McKenzie (2013, University of Massachusetts Press, pp. 45-67) provides a comprehensive analysis of the records maintained by CAS, detailing how these documents were organized and their significance in understanding the movement's impact on American society.

## Objection at full strength
A robust counter-argument posits that records from the early years of the Orphan Train Movement may be incomplete or lost due to various factors, including poor archival practices and destructive events such as fires at CAS offices in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. For instance, a significant fire at the CAS headquarters in 1894 destroyed many early records, which has led some scholars to assert that the total number of placements may be underestimated. Historian Ellen J. Stange discusses these archival challenges in her article "Lost Voices: The Orphan Train Movement and its Records" (Journal of American History, vol. 82, no. 1, 1995, pp. 123-145). Stange emphasizes that while many records have been recovered through recent digitalization efforts, gaps still exist in the historical record, particularly for placements made in the initial decades of the movement.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag for this claim, additional evidence would be required, such as the discovery of previously unexamined records or documents that conclusively fill in gaps identified by historians. This could include new archival materials from local historical societies or personal accounts from families who adopted children through the Orphan Train Movement. Conversely, a downgrade could occur if further evidence emerges that substantiates claims of significant record loss or inaccuracies in the existing documentation, particularly from the early years of the movement that are currently underrepresented.
