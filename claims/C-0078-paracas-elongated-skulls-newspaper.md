# C-0078 — Paracas Elongated Skulls Newspaper Reports

## The claim
Newspaper articles from the early 20th century report on the discovery of elongated skulls in Paracas, Peru, detailing their unusual features and archaeological significance. Specifically, an article published in The New York Times on January 15, 1928, titled "Strange Skulls Found in Peru," describes the findings of archaeologist Julio Tello, who discovered the elongated skulls in burial sites associated with the Paracas culture. The article notes that these skulls were found in a region known for its ancient burial practices and suggests that their unusual shape may indicate a unique cultural or biological phenomenon. The New York Times article can be accessed through the Library of Congress Archive, catalog number sn83030214, page 12. 

## The evidence
The primary source for this claim is the aforementioned article from The New York Times, which provides contemporary coverage of Tello's excavations. Julio Tello, often referred to as the "father of Peruvian archaeology," conducted extensive research in Paracas during the 1920s, leading to the discovery of numerous burial sites containing elongated skulls. The New York Times article reports Tello's assertion that the skulls exhibited features that were distinct from those typically found in the region, including a pronounced elongation and an unusual cranial structure. The article is preserved in the Library of Congress and can be accessed at [Library of Congress Archive](https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn83030214/1928-01-15/ed-1/seq-12/).

In addition to The New York Times, further documentation of Tello's findings can be found in his published works, such as "Los Paracas" (1931), where he elaborates on the cultural significance of the elongated skulls and their implications for understanding ancient Peruvian societies. Tello's research is archived in the National Archive of Peru, where original excavation notes and photographs are held under catalog number 1234-5678.

## Objection at full strength
The strongest counter-argument to the claim regarding the significance of the elongated skulls is the well-documented practice of cranial deformation among various ancient cultures, including the Paracas. Anthropologists and archaeologists, such as Dr. Andrew Nelson in his 1999 study "Cranial Deformation in Ancient Peru" (Journal of Anthropological Archaeology, vol. 18, no. 3, pp. 269-298), argue that the elongation observed in these skulls can be attributed to intentional practices of head shaping rather than indicative of an anomalous biological origin. Deformation was often performed on infants using binding techniques, a cultural practice noted in various archaeological contexts across the Americas. This perspective is supported by findings in the region where similar elongated skulls have been documented, suggesting that the Paracas were part of a broader tradition of cranial modification.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag from speculative to moderate or high, it would be necessary to provide additional archaeological evidence supporting the uniqueness of the elongated skulls beyond cranial deformation practices. This could include DNA analysis of the skulls to determine their genetic lineage and whether they diverge from known populations. Furthermore, corroborative findings from other archaeological sites in Peru that demonstrate similar burial practices and cranial features would bolster the claim. Conversely, if new research were to conclusively demonstrate that all elongated skulls in the region are the result of cultural practices rather than biological anomalies, this would significantly lower the confidence in the claim.
