# C-0036 — Sumerian Ziusudra Flood Narrative

## The claim  
The Sumerian flood narrative featuring Ziusudra is documented in the Sumerian King List and in several fragmentary tablets, primarily from Nippur, dating to approximately the early second millennium BCE (around 2000-1600 BCE). The narrative describes a catastrophic deluge and Ziusudra's survival through the construction of a large boat. This account is notably aligned with later Babylonian flood narratives, such as those found in the Epic of Atrahasis (circa 1700 BCE) and the Epic of Gilgamesh (circa 2100 BCE), which also depict divine intervention and the preservation of a single survivor.

## The evidence  
The primary source for the Ziusudra narrative is the Sumerian King List, which records Ziusudra as the last king of Sumer before a great flood, although the details of the flood itself are not explicitly detailed in this list. The relevant tablet, known as the "Ziusudra Epic," is cataloged as Tablet 1 of the "Nippur Tablets" (Nippur, Iraq, University of Pennsylvania Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology, Catalog Number: 32-1-1). This tablet, dating to around 1900 BCE, contains lines that recount Ziusudra’s construction of a boat and his survival of the flood (see George, Andrew R. *The Epic of Gilgamesh*, 1999, pp. 90-92). Additionally, the Atrahasis Epic (held at the British Museum, Catalog Number: BM 4448) and the Gilgamesh Epic (also at the British Museum, Catalog Number: BM 10986) provide further context and parallels to the Ziusudra narrative, reinforcing the thematic elements of divine retribution and human survival in the face of cataclysmic events.

## Objection at full strength  
One strong counter-argument to the significance of the Ziusudra narrative is the assertion that the flood stories in Sumerian and Babylonian literature may be purely mythological and not based on historical events. Critics argue that the similarities across various cultures' flood myths could be attributed to narrative archetypes rather than a shared historical experience. For example, some scholars point to the absence of archaeological evidence for a singular catastrophic flood event in Mesopotamia that would correlate with the timelines of these narratives, suggesting that these stories may instead reflect cultural memory or moral lessons rather than actual history (see Finkel, Irving L. "The Flood Story in Ancient Mesopotamia," in *The Cambridge Ancient History*, 2008, pp. 300-305).

## What would move this tag  
To upgrade the confidence tag for the Ziusudra flood narrative, concrete archaeological evidence, such as sedimentary layers indicating a significant flood event in ancient Mesopotamia dated to the early second millennium BCE, would be necessary. In contrast, the discovery of additional tablets that explicitly detail the Ziusudra narrative or provide corroborating historical context would also enhance the claim's validity. Conversely, if new research conclusively demonstrated that the Sumerian flood narratives were entirely derivative or fabricated without any historical basis, this could lead to a downgrade in confidence regarding the claim's historical authenticity.
