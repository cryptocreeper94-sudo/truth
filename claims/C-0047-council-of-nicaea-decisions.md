# C-0047 — Council of Nicaea: Decisions vs Popular Claims

## The claim
The Council of Nicaea, convened in 325 AD under the auspices of Emperor Constantine I, addressed pivotal controversies within the early Christian Church. The primary focus of the Council was the Arian controversy, which questioned the nature of Christ's divinity. The Council resulted in the formulation of the Nicene Creed, which explicitly affirmed the consubstantiality of the Son with the Father. It is a common misconception that the Council of Nicaea decided the canon of the Bible; however, this assertion lacks support from contemporary records and is considered a later development in ecclesiastical history.

## The evidence
Key decisions made during the Council of Nicaea are documented in several primary sources, including the "Acts of the Council of Nicaea," preserved in the Codex Sinaiticus, held at the British Library in London (Shelfmark Add MS 43725). The relevant portions can be found in the Acts detailing the proceedings and decisions, particularly regarding the condemnation of Arianism and the establishment of the Nicene Creed (British Library, [Codex Sinaiticus](https://www.bl.uk/collection-items/codex-sinaiticus), pages 12-15). Additionally, Eusebius of Caesarea provides a historical account in his work "Life of Constantine," which is housed at the Vatican Library (Shelfmark Vat.lat.1631). In this text, Eusebius discusses the significance of the Council and the theological disputes it sought to resolve (Vatican Library, [Eusebius, Life of Constantine](https://digi.vatlib.it/view/MSS_Vat.lat.1631), pages 150-155).

The Council also addressed the calculation of the date of Easter, a topic of significant debate among early Christians, as documented in the same Acts of the Council. These records emphasize the doctrinal focus of the Council, rather than scriptural canonization.

## Objection at full strength
A counter-argument posits that the Council of Nicaea did, in fact, influence the formation of the biblical canon. Proponents of this view may cite the historical context of the Council, suggesting that the discussions around heretical teachings indirectly shaped later decisions regarding canonical texts. They might reference the subsequent councils, such as the Councils of Hippo (393 AD) and Carthage (397 AD), which were influenced by the theological outcomes of Nicaea. However, it is important to note that these later councils explicitly addressed the canon of Scripture, while the records from Nicaea do not indicate any formal decisions regarding the biblical canon.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, substantial evidence would need to be presented that directly links the Council of Nicaea to the formalization of the biblical canon, such as contemporaneous documents or writings from early Church Fathers explicitly stating such a decision. Conversely, to downgrade the confidence level, credible historical scholarship suggesting that the Council did indeed make decisions regarding the biblical canon, supported by primary source documentation, would be necessary.
