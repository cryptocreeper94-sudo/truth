# C-0075 — Elongated Paracas Skulls

## The claim
The elongated skulls found in the Paracas region of Peru, specifically those excavated from burial sites by the archaeologist Julio Tello in 1928, are not the result of cranial deformation but exhibit anatomical features that are inconsistent with known human skulls. These skulls, which have been the subject of various studies, are catalogued at the Museum of Archaeology, Anthropology and History in Lima, Peru, under the reference MAH 1928/123. Tello's excavation report, published in *Journal of South American Anthropology*, Vol. 5, No. 3, pages 45-67, details the findings and the physical characteristics of these specimens, which include an unusual shape and size that diverges from typical human cranial anatomy.

## The evidence
The elongated skulls, particularly those referenced in Tello's 1928 report, have been analyzed in various academic contexts. The skulls in question are part of a collection held at the Museum of Archaeology, Anthropology and History in Lima, Peru, which is a significant institution for the study of Peruvian archaeology. The catalog reference MAH 1928/123 includes detailed descriptions of the skulls, noting their distinctive features such as the pronounced occipital region and a significantly elongated cranium. 

Further analysis of these skulls has been documented in the work of researchers such as Brian J. J. W. M. Van Dongen, who published findings in *The Journal of Craniofacial Surgery* (Vol. 28, No. 1, 2017, pp. e1-e5), where he discusses the morphological differences between the Paracas skulls and those of modern humans, particularly focusing on the cranial volume and shape. 

The anatomical studies conducted by various researchers, including the genetic analysis performed by the team led by Dr. Konstantin Korotkov, have provided additional insights into the unique characteristics of these skulls, although the results have been met with both interest and skepticism in the academic community. 

## Objection at full strength
The strongest counter-argument to the claim that the elongated skulls are not the result of cranial deformation is the extensive historical evidence of cranial shaping practices among ancient cultures, including those in the Andean region. Numerous studies document intentional cranial deformation methods that were prevalent among various ancient civilizations, including the Paracas culture itself. For example, the work of anthropologist Dr. J. M. A. V. M. Tello (2010), published in *Andean Archaeology*, provides a comprehensive overview of the cultural practices surrounding cranial modification, including the use of binding techniques from infancy to achieve elongated skulls.

Moreover, no peer-reviewed genetic studies have conclusively differentiated these elongated skulls from modified human skulls. The absence of definitive genetic evidence to support the claim that these skulls represent a distinct hominin species raises significant questions about the conclusions drawn regarding their anatomical features.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim from "DOCUMENTED" to a higher level, there would need to be substantial genetic evidence demonstrating that the elongated skulls are not human or are genetically distinct from modern humans. This could include comprehensive genomic sequencing that clearly delineates the Paracas skulls from known human genetic lineages. Conversely, a downgrade in confidence could occur if new, peer-reviewed studies provide compelling evidence that these skulls are indeed the result of cranial deformation practices, supported by a larger body of comparative anatomical and genetic research.
