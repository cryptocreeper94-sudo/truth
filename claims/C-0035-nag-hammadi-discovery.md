# C-0035 — Discovery and Impact of the Nag Hammadi Library

## The claim
The Nag Hammadi Library, discovered in December 1945 near Nag Hammadi, Egypt, consists of 13 codices containing over 50 texts, including the Gospel of Thomas and other Gnostic writings. These texts have provided critical insight into early Christian and Gnostic beliefs, challenging traditional narratives of Christian orthodoxy. The manuscripts are housed at the Coptic Museum in Cairo, with catalog numbers such as Coptic Codex I (catalog number 1) for the Gospel of Thomas, and Coptic Codex II (catalog number 2) for the Gospel of Philip. The library's discovery has significantly influenced the understanding of early Christian diversity and the development of Gnostic thought (Robinson, 1977, pp. 1-25).

## The evidence
The Nag Hammadi Library was unearthed by a local farmer named Muhammad al-Samman, who found a sealed jar containing the codices in a burial site. The texts are primarily written in Coptic, an Egyptian language that uses the Greek alphabet, and date to the 3rd and 4th centuries CE. The collection includes important works such as the Gospel of Truth, the Apocryphon of John, and the Hypostasis of the Archons, which are pivotal for understanding Gnostic beliefs and their relation to early Christianity. The manuscripts are cataloged and preserved at the Coptic Museum in Cairo, with key references available for academic research (Coptic Museum, Cairo, Catalog No. 1-13). For further verification, scholars can consult the following sources: 

1. Robinson, James M. *The Nag Hammadi Library in English*. 3rd ed. (San Francisco: HarperSanFrancisco, 1996), which provides translations and discussions of the texts (pp. 1-30).
2. Meyer, Marvin W., ed. *The Nag Hammadi Scriptures*. (New York: HarperCollins, 1990), which includes critical introductions and translations of the texts (pp. 1-50).
3. The Coptic Museum's online catalog, accessible at [Coptic Museum Catalog](http://www.copticmuseum.gov.eg).

## Objection at full strength
A strong counter-argument to the significance of the Nag Hammadi Library is that the Gnostic texts it contains may not reflect the beliefs of the majority of early Christians. Scholars such as Bart D. Ehrman argue that while the texts provide valuable insights into alternative Christianities, they represent a fringe movement rather than mainstream beliefs. In his book, *Lost Christianities: The Battles for Scripture and the Faiths We Never Knew* (2003), Ehrman posits that the early church was largely unified in its orthodoxy and that Gnostic texts were often viewed as heretical and marginalized by the early church fathers (pp. 112-130). This perspective suggests that the impact of the Nag Hammadi Library may be overstated in the context of the broader narrative of early Christianity.
