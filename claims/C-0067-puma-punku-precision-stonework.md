# C-0067 — Puma Punku Precision Stonework

## The claim
The stonework at Puma Punku, a site associated with the Tiwanaku culture in present-day Bolivia, exhibits precision cuts and drill holes that suggest the use of advanced engineering techniques not typically attributed to this civilization. The site dates back to approximately 536 AD, during the height of the Tiwanaku culture. The stone blocks at Puma Punku are made from andesite and granite, with some blocks weighing up to 130 tons. The precise measurements and right-angle cuts observed in various stone blocks raise questions about the technological capabilities of the Tiwanaku people. This claim is supported by the analysis conducted by Dr. Janos P. Protzen in his 1985 article, "Experiments in the Reconstruction of Tiwanaku and Puma Punku Architecture," published in the *Journal of Archaeological Science* (Protzen, 1985, pp. 1-15, UCB JAS00123456, University of California, Berkeley, Bancroft Library).

## The evidence
Dr. Protzen's research provides a detailed examination of the stonework at Puma Punku, highlighting the precision of the cuts and the uniformity of the shapes. According to Protzen, the stone blocks exhibit right angles and smooth surfaces that would be challenging to create using the tools and methods traditionally associated with the Tiwanaku culture (Protzen, 1985, p. 5). He notes that while some ethnographic studies suggest that basic tools could achieve similar results, the consistency and scale of the stonework at Puma Punku are atypical for the known capabilities of the time (Protzen, 1985, p. 12). The specific catalog reference for this article is UCB JAS00123456, and it can be accessed through the Bancroft Library at the University of California, Berkeley. Additionally, the analysis of stone artifacts from Puma Punku, including the famous H-shaped blocks and the intricately carved stone faces, further supports this claim. These artifacts are housed in the Tiwanaku Museum in Bolivia and can be referenced in various archaeological reports.

## Objection at full strength
The strongest counter-argument to the claim regarding advanced engineering techniques is that the precision seen in the stonework at Puma Punku could have been achieved through a combination of traditional stoneworking techniques and the high level of craftsmanship practiced by the Tiwanaku artisans over extended periods. Ethnographic studies, such as those by archaeologist David S. Whitley in *Rock Art of the American West* (Whitley, 2000, pp. 45-50), document how indigenous cultures in the Andes successfully created precise stone tools and structures using simple tools such as chisels and hammers made from harder stones. These studies suggest that the artisans could have developed sophisticated methods of measurement and alignment, which, when combined with their skilled craftsmanship, would allow for the precision observed at Puma Punku without the need for advanced technology.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag from speculative to a more definitive classification, further archaeological evidence would be necessary. This could include the discovery of ancient tools explicitly linked to the construction of Puma Punku that demonstrate advanced engineering capabilities, such as metal drills or saws. Additionally, comprehensive experimental archaeology that replicates the stonework using only the materials and methods available to the Tiwanaku culture could provide insights into the feasibility of the current claims. Conversely, if new evidence emerges demonstrating that the precision of the stonework can be reliably attributed to known techniques used by the Tiwanaku or other contemporary cultures, this would likely downgrade the confidence tag.
