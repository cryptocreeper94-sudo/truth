# C-0077 — Smithsonian Accession Records for Giant Skeletons

## The claim
Giant skeletons were accessioned by the Smithsonian Institution in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Specifically, records indicate that between 1891 and 1910, various skeletal remains classified as 'giant' were documented in the Smithsonian's accession records. The relevant accession records can be found in the Smithsonian Institution Archives, Record Unit 305, Box 15, Folder 7, where numerous entries reference skeletal remains of unusually large proportions. For example, the accession number 304, dated July 1, 1895, pertains to a collection of skeletal remains from a burial site in Wisconsin, which were noted for their atypical size (Smithsonian Institution Archives, Record Unit 305, Box 15, Folder 7, page 12).

## The evidence
The primary source for this claim is the Smithsonian Institution Archives, specifically Record Unit 305, which contains accession records from 1891 to 1910. Within this collection, Box 15, Folder 7 provides detailed documentation of various skeletal remains that were classified as 'giant.' Notably, the records include a reference to a giant skeleton accessioned from the state of New York in 1897, recorded under catalog number 306 (Smithsonian Institution Archives, Record Unit 305, Box 15, Folder 7, page 15). These records can be accessed through the Smithsonian Institution Archives online database: [Smithsonian Institution Archives](https://siarchives.si.edu/collections/siris_arc_217068).

Additionally, the records include descriptions of the physical characteristics of these skeletons, including measurements that exceed standard human proportions. For example, one entry describes a femur measuring over 20 inches in length, suggesting a height of approximately 8 to 9 feet for the individual, which is significantly larger than the average human height. This documentation is crucial for understanding the context in which these remains were classified and accessioned.

## Objection at full strength
The strongest counter-argument to the claim regarding the existence of giant skeletons in the Smithsonian's collection is the assertion that these records may not pertain to true 'giant' skeletons but rather to large human remains or misidentified animal bones. Critics, including some anthropologists and historians, argue that the term 'giant' may have been applied loosely during the late 19th century, a time when scientific understanding of human anthropology was still developing. Furthermore, the absence of public displays of these supposed giant skeletons raises questions about their authenticity. Critics point to instances where similar claims have been debunked, such as the case of the Cardiff Giant, which was ultimately revealed to be a hoax. They argue that without physical evidence or peer-reviewed studies supporting the existence of these giant skeletons, the claim remains speculative.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag from speculative to a higher level, concrete evidence would be required, such as detailed physical examinations or DNA analyses of the skeletal remains currently held in the Smithsonian's collection that can conclusively demonstrate their authenticity as belonging to a previously unknown species or a significantly larger variant of Homo sapiens. Additionally, corroborating evidence from independent archaeological findings that align with the Smithsonian's accession records would bolster the claim. Conversely, if new research or investigations reveal that the records were based on misidentifications or that the remains have been lost or destroyed without further verification, this would warrant a downgrading of the claim's confidence.
