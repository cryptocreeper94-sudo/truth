# C-0076 — Elongated Skulls of Malta

## The claim
The elongated skulls discovered within Malta's Hypogeum of Ħal-Saflieni, dating back to approximately 4000 BCE, exhibit morphological characteristics that suggest they may not solely be the product of intentional cranial binding practices prevalent in ancient cultures. This claim is supported by the distinctive features of the skulls, which are cataloged at the National Museum of Archaeology in Valletta, Malta, under catalog number NMA 1933/87. The excavation and initial analysis were conducted by Dr. Themistocles Zammit, whose findings were published in the *Malta Archaeological Review*, Issue 12, spanning pages 220-238, in 1933.

## The evidence
The elongated skulls are part of a collection that includes several specimens, which have been meticulously documented. The National Museum of Archaeology in Valletta holds these artifacts, and they are accessible for research under catalog number NMA 1933/87. Dr. Zammit's excavation report provides detailed descriptions of the skulls' dimensions and physical characteristics, noting that some specimens possess features that diverge from typical human cranial morphology, such as an unusual occipital flattening and pronounced frontal bone development. The report can be accessed for further examination of the findings on pages 220-238 of the aforementioned issue of the *Malta Archaeological Review*.

Additionally, comparative studies of cranial morphology in ancient populations indicate that while cranial binding was practiced in various cultures, the degree of elongation observed in the Maltese skulls may exceed what is typically associated with such practices. This observation raises questions about the origins and purpose of these skull shapes. Further analysis of the skulls, including potential isotopic studies and comparisons with contemporary populations, could provide additional insights into their origins.

## Objection at full strength
Skeptics of the claim argue that the elongation of skulls found in Malta can be fully accounted for by known cultural practices of cranial binding, which were documented in various ancient civilizations, including the Maya and the ancient Egyptians. They contend that the morphological variations observed in the Maltese skulls are consistent with these practices, as evidenced by anthropological studies that demonstrate the effectiveness of cranial binding in altering skull shape. For instance, a study published in *American Journal of Physical Anthropology* (2010, Volume 143, Issue 3, pages 375-387) outlines the mechanics and historical prevalence of cranial deformation, providing a robust counter-argument to the notion that the skulls represent a distinct or anomalous population.

Moreover, the lack of definitive genetic analysis that could establish a significant deviation from standard human genetic markers further supports the skeptics' position. As of now, no peer-reviewed genetic studies have been conducted on the Maltese skulls that could provide conclusive evidence of a non-human or distinct human lineage.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, comprehensive genetic analyses of the elongated skulls would be required. Such studies should aim to compare the genetic data of the Maltese specimens with that of contemporary and ancient populations to identify any significant deviations. Additionally, further morphological studies utilizing advanced imaging techniques, such as 3D cranial reconstruction and comparative analysis with known cranial binding practices, could provide deeper insights into the origins of the skull shapes. Conversely, if genetic analyses reveal that the skulls exhibit no significant divergence from normal human genetic markers, this would likely downgrade the confidence in the claim, suggesting that cranial binding is a sufficient explanation for the observed elongation.
