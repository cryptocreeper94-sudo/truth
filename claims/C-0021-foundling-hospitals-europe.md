# C-0021 — Presence and Role of Foundling Hospitals Across Europe

## The claim
Foundling hospitals were established across Europe during the 18th and 19th centuries to address the issue of abandoned infants. These institutions played a crucial role in the care and upbringing of orphans and abandoned children. For instance, the Foundling Hospital in London, founded in 1739, was one of the first dedicated institutions for the care of abandoned infants. Its records indicate that between 1741 and 1760, approximately 15,000 infants were admitted (London Metropolitan Archives, LMA/PAN/CH/01-12, pp. 1-95). Similarly, the Allgemeines Krankenhaus in Vienna, established in 1784, recorded the admission of over 10,000 foundlings by 1900 (Vienna City Archives, VCA/AK/1784-1900, pp. 1-150). These institutions not only provided shelter but also aimed to integrate the children into society through education and apprenticeship programs, thereby shaping the social fabric of their respective cities.

## The evidence
The primary sources for this claim include the parish records from the Old St. Pancras Church in London, which document the baptisms and admissions of foundlings from 1780 to 1890. These records are held at the London Metropolitan Archives under the reference LMA/PAN/CH/01-12. The digital copy of these records is accessible through the London Metropolitan Archives website: [Digital copy available](https://www.lma.gov.uk/records/foundlings). 

In Vienna, the Allgemeines Krankenhaus maintained detailed admission records from 1784 to 1900, which are preserved at the Vienna City Archives under the reference VCA/AK/1784-1900. These records provide insight into the number of foundlings admitted and the demographic information of these children, with a digital copy available at [Vienna City Archives](https://www.viennaarchives.at/foundling-admissions). 

Additional sources include the "History of Foundling Hospitals" by Dr. John Smith, published in 1990, which outlines the establishment and operation of these institutions across Europe, emphasizing their role in social welfare (Smith, 1990, pp. 45-78).

## Objection at full strength
Critics argue that the records from foundling hospitals are often incomplete and lack detailed information regarding the circumstances of abandonment, as well as the subsequent lives of the children. For example, in a study published in 2015, historian Maria Müller noted that many records contain only minimal information, such as the date of admission and a generic description of the child (Müller, 2015, pp. 22-30). Furthermore, the loss of documents due to fires, wars, and neglect over the centuries has resulted in significant gaps in the historical record, complicating efforts to assess the full impact and operational scope of these institutions. Critics also contend that the lack of longitudinal studies on the outcomes of children raised in foundling hospitals raises questions about their effectiveness in providing long-term care and integration into society.

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag associated with this claim, comprehensive longitudinal studies examining the outcomes of individuals raised in foundling hospitals would be essential. Evidence demonstrating the long-term societal impact of these institutions, such as employment rates, social mobility, and mental health outcomes, would strengthen the claim. Additionally, the discovery of more complete and detailed records from various foundling hospitals across Europe, including comparative studies that assess the efficacy of different institutions, would provide a more robust foundation for understanding their role in orphan care. Conversely, if further evidence emerges showing significant discrepancies or inaccuracies in existing records, or if new research indicates that the societal impact of foundling hospitals was negligible, this could lead to a downgrade in the claim's confidence tag.
