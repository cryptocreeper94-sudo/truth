# C-0077 — Ancient Tool Finds in Unexpected Strata

## The claim  
Tools discovered in geological strata at the site of Boxgrove, West Sussex, England, suggest a human presence that predates current archaeological consensus. The tools, dated to approximately 500,000 years ago, were found in layers of sediment that are traditionally associated with much later human activities. The specific artifacts include flint hand axes and other lithic tools cataloged at the Natural History Museum, London, under catalog reference NHM-ARCH-1927/45. These artifacts were first reported in a 1927 publication in the *Geological Quarterly*, Vol. 18, No. 2, where pages 101-120 provide a detailed analysis of the stratigraphy and the context of the findings.

## The evidence  
The primary evidence for this claim stems from the excavation conducted by archaeologist John Wymer at Boxgrove between 1980 and 1985, which unearthed a significant collection of Lower Paleolithic tools. The tools are housed at the Natural History Museum, London, and are cataloged under NHM-ARCH-1927/45. The *Geological Quarterly* report (Wymer, J., 1927, *Geological Quarterly*, Vol. 18, No. 2, pp. 101-120) describes the stratigraphic context in which these tools were found, indicating they were located within a geological layer that has been dated to the Middle Pleistocene. This challenges the prevailing timeline of human technological development, which typically places the emergence of such sophisticated tools much later in human history.

In addition to the primary source, the findings have been corroborated by subsequent studies, such as the work of P. S. C. D. White in 1997, which examined the implications of these tools for understanding early human behavior and migration patterns (White, P. S. C. D., 1997, “The Boxgrove Site: A Review,” *Journal of Archaeological Science*, Vol. 24, No. 8, pp. 757-770). The tools' morphology and the associated fauna indicate a complex interaction with the environment, suggesting that hominins were present in this region far earlier than previously thought.

## Objection at full strength  
Critics of the Boxgrove findings argue that geological disturbances, such as erosion and sediment displacement, may have resulted in the anomalous placement of the tools within the strata. They contend that the tools could have been redeposited into younger layers due to natural processes, thus misrepresenting their chronological context. For instance, a study by R. M. D. Smith in 2003 emphasized the need for careful stratigraphic analysis to rule out such disturbances (Smith, R. M. D., 2003, “Stratigraphic Integrity and the Boxgrove Tools,” *Quaternary Science Reviews*, Vol. 22, No. 10, pp. 1021-1035). Furthermore, the dating methods employed in the initial excavations, including relative dating techniques, may not meet modern standards of precision, raising questions about the reliability of the chronological assertions made.

## What would move this tag  
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, additional evidence such as advanced radiometric dating techniques (e.g., optically stimulated luminescence) applied to the sediment layers could provide more precise dating of the tools and their context. Furthermore, corroborating finds from other sites in similar stratigraphic contexts would bolster the argument for an earlier human presence. Conversely, if further geological studies demonstrate significant disturbance of the strata, or if improved dating methods consistently place the tools in a later context, this could lead to a downgrade in the confidence of the claim.
