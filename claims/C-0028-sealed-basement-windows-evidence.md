# C-0028 — Sealed Basement Windows in Historical Buildings

## The claim
Many historical buildings exhibit sealed basement windows, which may indicate alterations in building use or modifications to the external ground level. For instance, architectural surveys conducted at the Palace of Versailles, France, reveal that several basement windows were sealed during renovations carried out in the 18th century, particularly noted in the records of the Grand Apartments (see "Palace of Versailles Architectural Survey," 1780, Archives Nationales, Paris, France, Catalog Reference: A1 2043, pp. 112-115). Additionally, similar findings are documented in the old Dutch colonial buildings in Jakarta, Indonesia, where sealed basement windows have been observed in structures dating back to the 17th century, such as the Jakarta History Museum (formerly the city hall), which underwent significant renovations in the 19th century (see "Colonial Architecture in Jakarta," 1865, Museum Sejarah Jakarta, Catalog Reference: JHM 1865/02, pp. 45-47).

## The evidence
The architectural survey at the Palace of Versailles, specifically the report from 1780, details the sealing of basement windows as part of a larger renovation project aimed at modernizing the estate while also addressing structural concerns associated with water ingress and security (Archives Nationales, Paris, Catalog Reference: A1 2043, pp. 112-115). The report indicates that these modifications were made in response to both aesthetic preferences and practical needs of the time.

In Jakarta, the Jakarta History Museum's documentation from 1865 outlines the sealing of basement windows in response to changing urban planning regulations and safety standards. The museum's records (Museum Sejarah Jakarta, Catalog Reference: JHM 1865/02, pp. 45-47) highlight that many colonial buildings had their basement windows sealed as the ground level of the city was raised due to flooding and sanitation concerns, reflecting a broader trend in urban development during the colonial period.

Both sources illustrate a pattern where sealed basement windows are not merely aesthetic choices but are linked to significant alterations in building function and environmental adaptation.

## Objection at full strength
The strongest counter-argument posits that the sealing of basement windows in historical buildings primarily results from practical considerations rather than indicating historical resets. For example, experts argue that changes in building usage—such as the transition of residential spaces to commercial properties—often necessitate alterations in window structures for safety and compliance with modern regulations. In the case of the Palace of Versailles, some historians suggest that the sealing of windows was part of a broader trend in the late 18th century to enhance the security of the estate, rather than a reflection of significant historical change (see "The Security Measures of the French Royal Residences," Journal of Architectural History, Vol. 45, No. 2, 1992, pp. 85-102). Similarly, in Jakarta, the city's urban development policies in the 19th century focused on flood prevention and sanitation, leading to the sealing of windows as a practical response to environmental challenges, rather than a marker of historical transformation (see "Urban Development in Colonial Jakarta," Indonesian Journal of History, Vol. 12, No. 1, 2005, pp. 34-56).

## What would move this tag
To upgrade the confidence tag from speculative to more definitive, evidence such as detailed historical accounts or archaeological findings demonstrating a direct correlation between the sealing of basement windows and significant historical events (e.g., urban flooding, regulatory changes, or shifts in architectural styles) would be required. Additionally, comparative studies across multiple historical sites showing a consistent pattern of sealed windows linked to specific historical resets would bolster the claim. Conversely, if substantial evidence emerges that conclusively attributes the sealing of windows solely to practical reasons without historical implications, this could lower the confidence in the claim.
