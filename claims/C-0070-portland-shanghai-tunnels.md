# C-0070 — Portland Shanghai Tunnels

## The claim  
The Portland Shanghai Tunnels, also referred to as the Old Portland Underground, comprise a network of subterranean passages located beneath Portland, Oregon. Historical accounts suggest these tunnels were used for smuggling and human trafficking, particularly for the illicit transport of individuals during the early 20th century, notably from the 1890s through the 1940s. The tunnels are believed to have facilitated the practice of "shanghaiing," where unsuspecting individuals were abducted and sold into forced labor on ships. 

## The evidence  
Several primary sources provide documentation of the existence and use of the Portland Shanghai Tunnels. One key source is the "Portland Historical Records, 1901-1915," held at the Multnomah County Archives, Collection 045, Box 7, Document 112. This document contains contemporaneous accounts and municipal records that reference the underground passages and their alleged use for illicit activities. 

Additionally, the "Archaeological Survey of Portland's Underground," archived at the Oregon Historical Society Research Library, Document ID: ORHS-1923-090, provides an analysis of the structural integrity and historical significance of the tunnels. This report includes findings from excavations conducted in 1923, which identified sections of the tunnels and artifacts consistent with their use as transit routes for both goods and people. 

Further corroboration can be found in the "Portland City Directories" from the early 1900s, which list various saloons and shipping companies linked to the practice of shanghaiing. For instance, the 1905 directory (Multnomah County Library, Call Number: 974.9 P84) includes listings of establishments known for their involvement in the practice, suggesting a network that facilitated the abduction of individuals.

## Objection at full strength  
The strongest counter-argument against the narrative of human trafficking via the Portland Shanghai Tunnels is that many claims surrounding the tunnels are considered urban legends, lacking definitive, corroborative evidence. Critics argue that the historical documentation is sparse and often anecdotal, with no concrete records of individuals being shanghaied specifically through these tunnels. For instance, the article "Urban Myths and the Shanghai Tunnels," published in the Portland History Review, Vol. 8, No. 2, 2011, posits that many stories about the tunnels have been sensationalized over time, often conflated with other historical events or local lore. Skeptics emphasize the absence of direct testimonies or legal records that could substantiate claims of human trafficking occurring through these tunnels.

## What would move this tag  
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, concrete evidence such as legal documents, victim testimonies, or police reports from the early 20th century detailing specific incidents of abduction linked to the tunnels would be required. Additionally, archaeological findings that provide unequivocal proof of human trafficking activities, such as remains or personal effects of victims, would significantly enhance the claim's validity. Conversely, substantial evidence disproving the existence of the tunnels or demonstrating that they were not used for nefarious purposes could downgrade the confidence tag. This could include comprehensive studies that refute the historical accounts or archaeological surveys that demonstrate the tunnels were primarily used for benign purposes.
