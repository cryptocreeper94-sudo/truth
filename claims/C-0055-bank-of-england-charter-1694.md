# C-0055 — Bank of England Charter (1694)

## The claim
The original charter of the Bank of England, issued on July 27, 1694, formally established the institution as a private company with public responsibilities. This charter delineated the Bank's authority to issue banknotes, manage government debt, and provide loans to the Crown, thereby playing a crucial role in the financial system of England. The document is held at the British Library, catalog reference Add MS 27808, and is considered a foundational text in the history of modern banking.

## The evidence
The original charter of the Bank of England is preserved in the British Library, specifically under the catalog reference Add MS 27808. This document outlines the Bank's formation, objectives, and operational framework. It articulates the terms under which the Bank was established, including the initial capital of £1.2 million raised through public subscriptions. The charter also specifies the governance structure, including the appointment of a governor and directors, as well as the rights granted to the Bank concerning the issuance of banknotes (British Library, Add MS 27808, folios 1-12). 

Additionally, the charter was published in various formats shortly after its issuance, including pamphlets and books that disseminated its contents to the public. A notable publication is "The Charter and Act of Parliament for the Bank of England," printed in 1694, which can be found in the archives of the British Library (Shelfmark: 1036.e.10). This document further elucidates the Bank's role in facilitating government borrowing and managing national finances.

For further verification, the charter has been referenced in numerous scholarly works, including "A History of the Bank of England" by Charles Goodhart (2011), which discusses its implications on the banking system (Goodhart, p. 25-30). 

## Objection at full strength
One potential counter-argument to the claim regarding the Bank of England's charter is the interpretation of its private versus public status. Critics argue that while the Bank was established as a private institution, its operations and responsibilities increasingly aligned with public interests, thereby complicating the understanding of its role. For instance, the Bank's close relationship with the government and its involvement in managing public debt may lead some to assert that it functions more as a public institution than a private one. This perspective is articulated in "The Bank of England: A History" by Richard Roberts (2003), which discusses the evolving nature of the Bank's responsibilities and its impact on public policy (Roberts, p. 45-50).
