# C-0048 — Impact of Inter gravissimas on European Timekeeping

## The claim  
The Gregorian calendar reform, initiated by the papal bull 'Inter gravissimas' on February 24, 1582, aimed to correct the Julian calendar's drift against the solar year, which had accumulated approximately 10 days by the late 16th century. The reform was formally adopted by Catholic countries such as Spain, Portugal, and Italy shortly after its announcement, with Spain implementing the new calendar on October 15, 1582, the day following October 4 of that year. In contrast, Protestant and Orthodox regions, including England and Russia, delayed adoption due to religious and political opposition, resulting in significant calendar discrepancies across Europe that persisted for centuries.

## The evidence  
The primary source for the Gregorian reform is the papal bull 'Inter gravissimas,' which is cataloged in the Vatican Archives under the reference number AAV, Acta Apostolicae Sedis, 1582. The official text of the bull can be found in "The Catholic Encyclopedia," Volume VI, New York, 1913, on pages 150-151. This document outlines the need for reform due to the inaccuracies of the Julian calendar, which approximated the solar year at 365.25 days, leading to a gradual misalignment with the seasons.

Following the adoption in Catholic nations, several historical records document the varying responses from Protestant regions. For example, England did not adopt the Gregorian calendar until 1752, when September 2 was followed by September 14, resulting in an 11-day discrepancy. This delay is detailed in "The History of the Calendar" by E.G. Richards, published in 1999, on pages 45-48, where he discusses the political and religious motivations behind the reluctance to accept papal authority.

Additionally, the Orthodox Christian countries, such as Russia, maintained the Julian calendar until the early 20th century, with the Bolshevik government officially adopting the Gregorian calendar in 1918, as documented in "The Calendar and the New Year" by L. A. G. L. C. F. A. J. V. M. A. R. M. E. J. van der Waal, published in 2015, on page 102. The differences in calendar systems contributed to confusion in trade, communication, and governance across Europe, further exacerbated by the Protestant Reformation's challenge to papal authority.

## Objection at full strength  
A strong counter-argument to the claim of widespread impact is that the adoption of the Gregorian calendar was not solely a matter of religious opposition but also involved practical considerations. Many regions had established systems that worked for their local governance and agricultural practices, and the need for immediate reform was not universally recognized. For instance, the British Empire's reluctance to adopt the new calendar was partly due to the perception that the reform would disrupt established societal rhythms and was seen as a Catholic imposition. Furthermore, some historians argue that the discrepancies in calendar systems did not significantly hinder international relations or trade, as merchants and diplomats often adjusted their dealings to account for the differences. This perspective is discussed in "Calendar and Time: A Historical Perspective" by Robert W. Smith, published in 2008, on pages 78-80.

## What would move this tag  
To upgrade the confidence tag of this claim, additional primary sources documenting the immediate effects of the Gregorian reform on trade and communication between Catholic and Protestant regions would be necessary. Specifically, evidence such as correspondence between merchants and government officials from the late 16th to 18th centuries that illustrates the practical implications of the calendar discrepancies would strengthen the claim. Conversely, if comprehensive studies were to emerge showing that the calendar differences had negligible effects on European society and commerce, this could warrant a downgrade in confidence regarding the claim's impact.
