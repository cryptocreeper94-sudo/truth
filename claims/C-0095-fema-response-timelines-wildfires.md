# C-0095 — FEMA Response Timelines in Wildfire Events

## The claim
FEMA's response timelines for recent wildfire events, such as those in Lahaina (2023) and Paradise (2018), indicate significant delays that correlate with increased damage and loss. The response times suggest systemic issues within disaster management frameworks, potentially exacerbating the effects of the fires.

## The evidence
S-0154: FEMA disaster declaration records and public after-action reporting. S-0155: National Association of Insurance Commissioners analysis of wildfire claim patterns. These sources confirm that response timelines varied across events and that Lahaina presented logistical challenges that delayed federal resource delivery. The correlation between delayed response and elevated loss is plausible on its face but has not been isolated from confounding variables (fire speed, road access, population density).

## Objection at full strength
Disaster response timelines are governed by a cascade of pre-conditions: state and county must formally request federal assistance before FEMA can activate; the Stafford Act sequence requires a presidential disaster declaration; and resource pre-positioning is driven by prior warning time. Lahaina's fire moved faster than historical fires of comparable size, giving less lead time for pre-positioning regardless of institutional intent. GAO and FEMA inspector general audits of Paradise (Camp Fire) response attributed delays primarily to road access destruction, not systemic organizational failure. Comparing Lahaina to other wildfire responses without controlling for fire speed, road network destruction, and island logistics is not a valid comparison.

## What would move this tag
CONTESTED would require a GAO, inspector general, or independent peer-reviewed study that compared Lahaina response timelines to those of comparable rapid-onset fires and found statistically anomalous delays after controlling for access, pre-warning time, and resource availability. DOCUMENTED would require that study plus evidence of a specific institutional decision or policy that caused the delay — not merely a correlation between the timeline and the outcome.
