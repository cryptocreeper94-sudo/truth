Abstract
Synthetic organisms operating within distributed deterministic ecosystems must eventually reach terminal states—through resource exhaustion, governance-directed decommissioning, fitness collapse, or ecosystem restructuring—and the ecosystem must recover from the loss of terminated organisms by redistributing their resources, reassigning their responsibilities, and restoring dependent organisms to stable operation. Classical termination systems treat process shutdown as an atomic event: the process stops and its resources are reclaimed by the operating system. Organism extinction requires a stronger guarantee: every terminal transition must produce identical outcomes across all nodes, every extinct organism's legacy (resources, territories, communication channels, evolutionary history) must be governed deterministically, and every recovery operation must restore affected organisms to identical post-recovery states across all nodes.

I formalize Deterministic Organism Extinction and Recovery Protocols (D-OERP) as the architectural framework governing all terminal transitions, legacy redistribution, and post-extinction recovery operations for synthetic organisms within distributed deterministic ecosystems. D-OERP ensures that every extinction is deterministically executed, certificate-bound, and identity-preserving (the extinct organism's identity and history are archived, not erased), and that every recovery is deterministically computed, governance-authorized, and certificate-verified. I integrate D-OERP with the Lume compiler's deterministic AST pipeline [4], Lume-V execution envelopes [11], Trust Layer certificate hierarchies [6], DAIGS cognitive substrates [7], LDIR multilingual inference semantics [8], SOR biological hierarchy [9], ZK-SRP state reversal protocols [1], G-DRSP global synchronization protocols [14], D-COCP cross-organism communication protocols [15], D-OLP lifecycle protocols [16], D-OMPP memory and persistence protocols [17], D-OMSCP mobility and spatial coordination protocols [18], D-OREP resource exchange protocols [19], D-OCRP conflict resolution protocols [20], D-OEAP evolution and adaptation protocols [21], and GUPAS governance pipelines [10]. Certificate-bound extinction anchors every terminal transition to the organism's complete provenance chain. The extinction pipeline's six-stage architecture and the recovery pipeline's six-stage architecture provide end-to-end determinism guarantees from terminal state detection through ecosystem restoration. This work establishes what is, to my knowledge, the first complete extinction and recovery architecture for deterministic synthetic organisms.

1 Introduction
1.1 The Necessity of Extinction and Recovery in Synthetic Ecosystems
Extinction is the governed conclusion of an organism's existence within the deterministic ecosystem. Every synthetic organism has a finite operational lifespan determined by its governance policies, resource sustainability, fitness trajectory, and ecosystem requirements. Organisms that exhaust their resources, fail to maintain minimum viable fitness, violate governance constraints beyond remediation, or become redundant through ecosystem restructuring must be terminated through a governed extinction process that preserves ecosystem consistency.

The Synthetic Organism Runtime (SOR) framework [9] defines organisms with terminal capabilities corresponding to biological analogues: cell-level apoptosis (programmed cell death), signal-level pathway decommissioning (synaptic pruning), homeostasis-level territorial withdrawal (niche abandonment), and cognitive-level strategic retirement (planned cessation of cognitive activity). Each terminal capability operates at different scales and produces different legacy redistribution requirements.

Recovery is the complementary process: restoring the ecosystem to stable operation after an extinction event. Recovery encompasses resource redistribution (reallocating the extinct organism's resources to surviving organisms), responsibility reassignment (transferring the extinct organism's governance duties to successors), communication channel repair (reconnecting organisms that communicated through the extinct organism), and fitness landscape adjustment (recalibrating surviving organisms' fitness evaluations to account for the absence). The ecological resilience principles formalized by Holling [28] inform the framework's approach to ecosystem recovery following organism loss.

D-OERP addresses these challenges by formalizing extinction and recovery as first-class, certificate-bound, deterministic capabilities governed by their own identity systems, pipeline architectures, proof mechanisms, and certificate hierarchies.

1.2 Why Deterministic Terminal States Are Required
Deterministic extinction ensures that every node in the distributed ecosystem computes identical terminal outcomes for every organism at every synchronization checkpoint. Without deterministic extinction, two nodes could terminate the same organism at different times, through different mechanisms, or with different legacy distributions, producing divergent ecosystem states that cascade through resource allocations, territorial configurations, and communication networks [14].

Consider an organism whose fitness drops below the minimum viable threshold. If Node A detects the fitness failure and terminates the organism while Node B's fitness evaluation produces a marginally different score that keeps the organism alive, the ecosystem diverges: Node A redistributes the organism's resources while Node B continues to account for the organism's presence. This extinction divergence propagates to every organism that depends on the terminated organism's resources, territory, or communication channels.

Deterministic recovery ensures that post-extinction ecosystem restoration produces identical results across all nodes. Without deterministic recovery, different nodes could redistribute the extinct organism's legacy differently, assign different successors, and restore different communication topologies, compounding the divergence introduced by non-deterministic extinction.

1.3 The Gap Between Biological Death and Computational Termination
Biological organisms experience death through diverse mechanisms: programmed cell death (apoptosis), organ failure, predation, disease, and environmental catastrophe. Each mechanism produces irreversible termination with ecological consequences: nutrient recycling, niche vacancy, predator-prey rebalancing, and evolutionary selection pressure. The mass extinction dynamics studied by Raup [27] demonstrate that biological extinction events reshape entire ecosystems, creating both destruction and opportunity for surviving species.

Classical computational termination—process killing, service shutdown, virtual machine destruction—treats termination as an infrastructure concern: resource deallocation without regard for the terminated entity's identity, history, or ecological role. Organism extinction is a behavioral event: the organism's terminal state, legacy distribution, and ecological impact are governed properties that influence ecosystem dynamics long after the organism ceases to exist.

D-OERP bridges this gap by formalizing biological extinction concepts as deterministic computational protocols. Apoptosis maps to governed self-termination with certificate-recorded rationale. Ecological nutrient recycling maps to deterministic resource redistribution with provenance tracking. Niche vacancy maps to governed territorial reassignment with fairness constraints. Predator-prey rebalancing maps to ecosystem fitness recalibration with stability analysis.

2 Foundations of Organism Extinction
2.1 Deterministic Extinction Primitives
D-OERP defines four deterministic extinction primitives: det_assess_viability, det_prepare_extinction, det_execute_extinction, and det_archive. Each primitive is a pure function of its inputs: the organism's current state, its viability assessment, and the deterministic time at which the operation is executed. No primitive depends on node-specific state.

The det_assess_viability primitive evaluates whether the organism meets minimum viable thresholds across resource holdings, fitness levels, governance compliance, and ecosystem contribution. The det_prepare_extinction primitive computes the extinction plan: legacy resource inventory, communication channel mapping, territorial holdings enumeration, and successor identification.

The det_execute_extinction primitive commits the terminal transition: halting the organism's execution, freezing its state, and initiating legacy redistribution. The det_archive primitive preserves the extinct organism's complete history—certificate chain, evolutionary trajectory, conflict record, and resource accounting—in the Trust Layer's archival infrastructure.

2.2 Extinction Invariants
D-OERP enforces four extinction invariants. The determinism invariant requires that all nodes compute identical extinction decisions for identical viability assessments. The completeness invariant requires that every extinction produces a complete legacy accounting: no resource, territory, or communication channel may be orphaned without governance-assigned disposition. The irreversibility invariant requires that committed extinctions cannot be undone without explicit governance-authorized recovery. The identity preservation invariant requires that the extinct organism's identity and history are archived, never erased.

The completeness invariant is the most operationally critical because orphaned resources introduce conservation violations, orphaned territories create ungoverned spatial gaps, and orphaned communication channels leave dependent organisms unable to communicate.

The identity preservation invariant distinguishes organism extinction from process deletion. A deleted process leaves no trace. An extinct organism leaves a complete archival record that supports posthumous auditing, forensic investigation, and ecosystem historical analysis.

2.3 Certificate-Anchored Extinction
Every extinction in D-OERP produces an extinction certificate that records the terminal transition's parameters, viability assessment, legacy inventory, and governance context. The extinction certificate is the organism's final certificate, appended to its certificate chain and committed to the Trust Layer's archival infrastructure.

The extinction certificate contains the organism's final state hash (SHA3-256), the viability assessment that triggered extinction, the legacy redistribution plan, and the governance authorization. These commitments enable post-hoc verification that the extinction was properly authorized and correctly executed.

Certificate-anchored extinction supports non-repudiable terminal auditing. If a governance investigation determines that an extinction was improperly authorized or produced incorrect legacy distributions, the extinction certificate identifies the authorizing entity and the extinction's effects.

2.4 Intent-Driven Terminal States
Extinctions in D-OERP are classified by the intent that motivated them. Governance-directed extinction (planned decommissioning) carries different governance requirements than fitness-triggered extinction (viability failure) or resource-exhaustion extinction (metabolic collapse). The Proof-of-Intent framework [13] validates the extinction's declared purpose.

Intent classification enables context-sensitive legacy distribution. A governance-directed extinction may include a specific successor designation. A fitness-triggered extinction distributes legacy according to default governance policies. A resource-exhaustion extinction triggers emergency legacy redistribution to prevent cascade failures.

The intent declaration is recorded in the extinction certificate alongside the legacy distribution outcome.

2.5 Multi-Organism Extinction Determinism
When multiple organisms undergo extinction within the same epoch, their collective terminal transitions must be deterministic across all nodes. This requires that the aggregate extinction pattern—the set of extinctions, their ordering, their legacy redistributions, and their collective effects on ecosystem state—is identical across all nodes.

Multi-organism extinction determinism introduces ordering constraints. If Organism A's extinction redistributes resources to Organism B, and Organism B is also undergoing extinction in the same epoch, A's extinction must be processed before B's to compute B's correct legacy inventory. The extinction pipeline resolves ordering through dependency graph analysis, operating under the partial synchrony assumptions formalized by Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer [22].

Mass extinction events—where a significant fraction of the organism population terminates simultaneously—require specialized coordination to prevent cascade failures and ensure ecosystem survivability.

3 Foundations of Organism Recovery
3.1 Deterministic Recovery Primitives
D-OERP defines four deterministic recovery primitives: det_assess_damage, det_plan_recovery, det_execute_recovery, and det_verify_restoration. Each primitive is a pure function of its inputs: the post-extinction ecosystem state, the extinction certificate, and the governance-authorized recovery parameters.

The det_assess_damage primitive evaluates the ecosystem impact of the extinction: which organisms lost communication partners, which resource pools were depleted, which territorial gaps were created, and which fitness landscapes changed. The det_plan_recovery primitive computes the optimal recovery strategy: resource redistribution targets, successor assignments, communication channel repairs, and fitness recalibrations.

The det_execute_recovery primitive implements the recovery plan: distributing legacy resources through D-OREP [19], reassigning territories through D-OMSCP [18], repairing communication channels through D-COCP [15], and recalibrating fitness through D-OEAP [21]. The det_verify_restoration primitive confirms that the post-recovery ecosystem satisfies all stability, conservation, and governance constraints.

3.2 Recovery Invariants
D-OERP enforces four recovery invariants. The determinism invariant requires that all nodes compute identical recovery outcomes from identical post-extinction states. The conservation invariant requires that legacy redistribution accounts for every resource unit of the extinct organism without creating or destroying resources. The stability invariant requires that recovery operations produce a stable ecosystem configuration: no surviving organism's homeostatic equilibrium is destabilized by the recovery process. The completeness invariant requires that every extinction-induced damage is addressed by the recovery plan.

The stability invariant is the most consequential because poorly planned recovery can destabilize surviving organisms. Redistributing resources to organisms already at capacity can overwhelm their processing budgets. Reassigning territories to organisms already managing maximum territorial scope can degrade their governance effectiveness.

The conservation invariant ensures that the ecosystem's total resource accounting remains balanced through the extinction-recovery cycle.

3.3 Certificate-Anchored Recovery
Every recovery operation produces a recovery certificate that records the recovery plan, the damage assessment, the redistribution outcomes, and the governance authorization. Recovery certificates are linked to the extinction certificate that triggered the recovery, creating a complete extinction-recovery audit trail.

Recovery certificates contain pre-recovery and post-recovery ecosystem state hashes that enable verification. Auditors can confirm that the recovery plan was correctly executed by comparing the certified post-recovery state with the actual ecosystem state.

The linked extinction-recovery certificate pair provides the forensic foundation for terminal-state auditing.

3.4 Intent-Driven Restoration
Recovery operations are classified by the intent that guides them. Emergency recovery (restoring ecosystem stability after unexpected extinction) carries different governance requirements than planned recovery (executing pre-arranged successor transitions). The recovery intent constrains the allowable recovery actions: emergency recovery may bypass normal governance approval for critical stabilization but triggers mandatory post-hoc review.

Intent-driven restoration prevents recovery abuse where governance entities exploit extinction events to redistribute resources or territories for unauthorized purposes.

The recovery intent is recorded in the recovery certificate alongside the actual redistribution outcomes.

3.5 Multi-Organism Recovery Determinism
When extinction events affect multiple organisms simultaneously, recovery must be coordinated across all affected organisms to produce consistent ecosystem restoration. Independent per-organism recovery plans may conflict: two recovery plans may both assign the same successor to different territorial vacancies, or both redistribute resources to the same recipient organism exceeding its budget capacity.

Multi-organism recovery determinism requires coordinated recovery planning that considers all extinction events collectively and produces a globally consistent recovery strategy.

The recovery pipeline constructs a recovery dependency graph that captures interdependencies between recovery actions and processes them in topological order.

4 Extinction Architecture
4.1 Extinction Identity
Every extinction event in D-OERP is assigned a globally unique extinction identity (ExtinctionID) derived from the organism's identity, the extinction epoch, and the organism's final state hash. This deterministic derivation ensures that all nodes compute identical ExtinctionIDs for the same terminal event.

The ExtinctionID is registered in the Trust Layer's extinction registry upon certification. The registry provides a permanent record of all extinct organisms, enabling historical queries and forensic investigation.

Extinction identity is distinct from organism identity. The organism's OrganismID persists in the archival record after extinction, linked to the ExtinctionID that records the terminal event.

4.2 Extinction Boundaries
Extinction boundaries define the conditions under which extinction may be triggered. The viability boundary specifies minimum fitness, resource, and governance compliance thresholds below which extinction is mandatory. The governance boundary specifies the governance authority required to authorize voluntary extinction. The cascade boundary specifies the maximum number of dependent extinctions that a single extinction may trigger.

Boundaries prevent both premature extinction (terminating organisms that could be recovered through adaptation) and delayed extinction (allowing non-viable organisms to consume resources and degrade ecosystem performance).

Extinction boundary values are governance-configurable and are updated atomically at epoch boundaries.

4.3 Extinction Constraints
Extinction constraints enforce governance policies on terminal transitions. The authorization constraint requires valid governance credentials for voluntary extinction. The legacy constraint requires complete legacy accounting before extinction commitment. The dependency constraint requires that all organisms depending on the extinct organism have viable alternatives or successor assignments.

Temporal constraints govern extinction timing. Critical extinctions (governance-mandated decommissioning, emergency termination of malfunctioning organisms) must complete within epoch boundaries. Non-critical extinctions (planned end-of-life transitions) may span epoch boundaries with governance approval.

Conservation constraints ensure that the organism's legacy resources are completely accounted for in the redistribution plan.

4.4 Extinction Proofs
Extinction proofs demonstrate that a terminal transition was executed correctly: the organism was properly assessed, its legacy was completely inventoried, and its resources were distributed according to the governance-authorized plan. The proof contains the ExtinctionID, the organism's final state hash, the legacy inventory, the redistribution plan, and a Merkle path linking the extinction event to the epoch's event tree [23].

Extinction proofs support legacy verification. Verifiers can confirm that the declared legacy distribution satisfies conservation constraints: every resource unit of the extinct organism appears in exactly one recipient's allocation or in the ecosystem's unclaimed pool.

Proof aggregation reduces overhead when mass extinction events terminate multiple organisms within the same epoch.

4.5 Extinction Certificates
Upon successful extinction verification, the Trust Layer Certificate Fabric mints an extinction certificate that permanently records the terminal transition. The certificate contains the ExtinctionID, the organism identity, the final state hash, the viability assessment, the legacy redistribution summary, the extinction proof reference, and the validator endorsement set. The certificate's Ed25519 signature [24] binds the extinction data to the consensus state at the moment of termination.

Extinction certificates are the organism's final certificate, sealing its certificate chain. No further certificates may be appended to an extinct organism's chain without governance-authorized recovery.

The cumulative certificate history—from genesis through extinction—provides a complete lifecycle record for forensic analysis.

4.6 Extinction Governance
Extinction governance defines the administrative framework controlling terminal transitions. Governance authorities set extinction policies through the GUPAS framework [10], specifying viability thresholds, decommissioning schedules, legacy distribution rules, and cascade limits.

Mandatory extinction policies define conditions under which extinction is automatic: organisms below viability thresholds for a governance-defined number of consecutive epochs are automatically terminated. Voluntary extinction requires explicit governance authorization with multi-signature approval.

Emergency extinction governance enables rapid termination of malfunctioning organisms that threaten ecosystem stability.

5 Recovery Architecture
5.1 Recovery Identity
Every recovery operation in D-OERP is assigned a globally unique recovery identity (RecoveryID) derived from the ExtinctionID that triggered the recovery, the recovery epoch, and the post-recovery ecosystem state hash. This deterministic derivation ensures cross-node consistency.

The RecoveryID is linked to the ExtinctionID in the Trust Layer's terminal registry, creating a paired extinction-recovery record that supports end-to-end terminal auditing.

Recovery identity enables tracking of recovery outcomes over time, including delayed recovery effects and long-term ecosystem stability assessments.

5.2 Recovery Boundaries
Recovery boundaries define the scope and cost of recovery operations. The resource boundary limits the maximum resources consumed by recovery processing. The temporal boundary limits the maximum epochs allowed for recovery completion. The scope boundary limits the maximum number of organisms affected by a single recovery operation.

Boundaries prevent recovery-induced instability where excessive resource consumption for recovery processing degrades surviving organisms' performance.

Recovery boundary values are computed from the extinction's scope and governance parameters.

5.3 Recovery Constraints
Recovery constraints enforce governance policies on restoration operations. The conservation constraint requires that legacy redistribution preserves total resource accounting. The fairness constraint requires that legacy distribution does not systematically advantage or disadvantage any organism class. The compatibility constraint requires that successor assignments are compatible with the extinct organism's former responsibilities.

The stability constraint requires that recovery operations pass stability analysis before commitment, confirming that the post-recovery configuration produces stable homeostatic behavior for all affected organisms [3].

The deterministic healing framework [5] provides secondary stability validation for recovery outcomes.

5.4 Recovery Proofs
Recovery proofs demonstrate that restoration was executed correctly: legacy resources were completely redistributed, successors were properly assigned, and the post-recovery ecosystem satisfies all governance constraints. The proof contains the RecoveryID, the pre-recovery and post-recovery ecosystem state hashes, the redistribution ledger, and a Merkle path linking the recovery event to the epoch's event tree [23].

Recovery proofs support redistribution verification. Verifiers confirm that every resource unit from the extinct organism's legacy appears in exactly one recipient's post-recovery allocation.

Proof aggregation reduces overhead for mass recovery events following mass extinction.

5.5 Recovery Certificates
Upon successful recovery verification, the Trust Layer mints a recovery certificate that permanently records the restoration outcome. The certificate contains the RecoveryID, the linked ExtinctionID, the redistribution ledger summary, the affected organisms' post-recovery state hashes, and the validator endorsement set. The certificate's Ed25519 signature [24] binds the recovery data to the consensus state at the moment of restoration.

Recovery certificates are linked to the affected organisms' certificate chains, recording the recovery's impact on their resource holdings, territorial configurations, and communication topologies.

The extinction-recovery certificate pair provides non-repudiable evidence of the complete terminal lifecycle.

5.6 Recovery Governance
Recovery governance defines the administrative framework controlling restoration operations. Governance authorities set recovery policies through GUPAS [10], specifying redistribution rules, successor selection criteria, recovery timelines, and emergency restoration protocols.

Default recovery policies define standard redistribution: resources are distributed proportionally to surviving organisms based on proximity, compatibility, and governance priority. Override policies enable governance-directed redistribution for strategic ecosystem restructuring.

Emergency recovery governance enables rapid restoration following catastrophic extinction events that threaten ecosystem survivability.

6 Extinction Pipelines
6.1 Detection Pipeline
The extinction detection pipeline monitors organism viability across fitness, resources, governance compliance, and ecosystem contribution metrics. Detection operates continuously, comparing each organism's viability indicators against governance-defined extinction thresholds.

Detection triggers include fitness below minimum viable thresholds for consecutive epochs, resource exhaustion below metabolic sustainability, governance compliance violations beyond remediation, and governance-directed decommissioning orders.

Detection sensitivity is governance-configurable, trading extinction responsiveness for false-positive tolerance.

6.2 Arbitration Pipeline
The extinction arbitration pipeline resolves contested extinctions. When an organism's viability assessment triggers extinction but the organism or its dependents contest the assessment, arbitration evaluates the contest through the dynamic arbitration framework [12]. Arbitration may sustain the extinction, grant a remediation period, or revoke the extinction trigger.

Arbitration decisions are binding and certified. Organisms whose extinction contests are denied proceed to terminal transition. Organisms granted remediation periods receive temporary resource supplements and governance-assisted adaptation through D-OEAP [21].

Arbitration outcomes are recorded in the epoch's extinction log.

6.3 Validation Pipeline
The validation pipeline verifies that extinction preparations are complete and correct. Validation confirms: legacy inventory completeness (all resources, territories, and channels accounted), redistribution plan feasibility (all recipients can accommodate their assigned legacy), and governance authorization validity (all required approvals obtained).

Cross-node validation leverages the G-DRSP framework's state comparison infrastructure [14] to confirm that all nodes compute identical extinction preparations.

Validation failure triggers re-preparation rather than extinction abort, except when the viability assessment is itself invalidated.

6.4 Synchronization Pipeline
The synchronization pipeline ensures that all nodes execute the extinction simultaneously. Extinction must be atomic across the distributed ecosystem: no node may continue operating the organism after other nodes have terminated it.

Synchronization uses the epoch boundary as the atomic extinction moment. The extinction is committed before the epoch transitions and the organism ceases to exist at the next epoch's initialization.

The synchronization pipeline coordinates with D-COCP [15] to ensure that all communication partners are notified of the impending extinction before the terminal epoch.

6.5 Certificate Issuance Pipeline
The certificate issuance pipeline mints the extinction certificate after successful validation and synchronization. The certificate seals the organism's certificate chain and is committed to both the Trust Layer's active ledger and its archival infrastructure.

Certificate issuance integrates with the Trust Layer's certificate batching infrastructure for mass extinction events.

The sealed certificate chain is permanently archived, enabling posthumous queries against the extinct organism's complete history.

6.6 Multi-Organism Extinction Pipeline
The multi-organism extinction pipeline governs mass extinction events where multiple organisms terminate simultaneously. Mass extinction requires coordinated legacy redistribution to prevent cascade failures, resource orphaning, and territorial fragmentation.

The pipeline constructs an extinction dependency graph and processes terminations in topological order. Organisms whose legacy depends on other simultaneously-terminating organisms are processed after their dependencies.

Mass extinction events trigger ecosystem-wide recovery planning through the recovery pipeline.

7 Recovery Pipelines
7.1 Detection Pipeline
The recovery detection pipeline identifies ecosystem damage following extinction events. The pipeline assesses resource gaps, territorial vacancies, communication channel disruptions, and fitness landscape perturbations caused by the extinct organism's absence.

Detection produces a damage assessment report that quantifies the ecosystem impact and prioritizes recovery actions by urgency.

Detection sensitivity is calibrated to the extinction's scope: larger extinctions trigger more comprehensive damage assessment.

7.2 Reconstruction Pipeline
The reconstruction pipeline computes the recovery plan from the damage assessment. Reconstruction identifies optimal resource redistribution targets, successor assignments, communication channel repairs, and fitness recalibrations. The reconstruction algorithm is deterministic: given identical damage assessments and governance parameters, all nodes produce identical recovery plans.

Reconstruction considers multiple recovery strategies and selects the strategy that maximizes ecosystem stability while minimizing disruption to surviving organisms. Strategy evaluation uses the DAIGS cognitive substrate [7] for complex multi-organism recovery scenarios.

The reconstruction plan is validated against conservation, fairness, and stability constraints before execution.

7.3 Arbitration Pipeline
The recovery arbitration pipeline resolves conflicts over legacy distribution. When multiple surviving organisms claim the same legacy resources, territories, or communication channels, arbitration resolves the claims through the D-OCRP conflict resolution framework [20].

Arbitration considers each claimant's proximity to the extinct organism, its governance priority, its capacity to accommodate the legacy, and fairness constraints that prevent systematic legacy accumulation.

Arbitration decisions are binding and certified.

7.4 Validation Pipeline
The recovery validation pipeline verifies that recovery execution matches the certified recovery plan. Validation confirms resource redistribution accuracy, successor assignment correctness, and post-recovery ecosystem stability.

Cross-node validation compares recovery outcomes across all nodes through the G-DRSP synchronization infrastructure [14]. Divergent recovery outcomes trigger remediation.

Post-recovery behavioral testing exercises affected organisms with reference scenarios to confirm stable operation.

7.5 Certificate Issuance Pipeline
The recovery certificate issuance pipeline mints recovery certificates after validated restoration. Certificates are linked to the triggering extinction certificate and to all affected organisms' certificate chains.

Certificate issuance records the complete redistribution ledger, enabling posthumous auditing of legacy distribution equity and governance compliance.

Batched certificate issuance handles mass recovery events efficiently.

7.6 Multi-Organism Recovery Pipeline
The multi-organism recovery pipeline governs coordinated restoration following mass extinction. Coordinated recovery prevents recovery conflicts, resource oversaturation, and territorial fragmentation that would arise from independent per-organism recovery planning.

The pipeline constructs a recovery dependency graph and processes restoration actions in topological order, coordinating through D-COCP communication channels [15].

Mass recovery triggers ecosystem-wide fitness recalibration through D-OEAP [21] to enable surviving organisms to adapt to the post-extinction ecosystem configuration.

8 Integration with Lume
8.1 AST Determinism for Terminal Operations
The Lume compiler's deterministic AST pipeline [4] ensures that extinction and recovery logic produces identical bytecode across all compilation instances. Terminal operations are compiled as deterministic Lume expressions that take the organism's current state and viability assessment as inputs and produce the terminal outcome as output.

D-OERP extends the compiler's determinism guarantee to terminal-specific code patterns. The compiler's terminal operation analyzer verifies that viability assessment functions and legacy redistribution algorithms are pure functions.

The AST structure enables fine-grained terminal verification at the computation step level.

8.2 Grammar Constraints
The Lume grammar provides dedicated constructs for terminal operations. The extinct keyword declares a governed extinction with specified legacy disposition. The recover keyword declares a governed recovery with specified redistribution plan. The archive keyword declares deterministic history preservation. The successor keyword declares governed responsibility transfer.

Grammar-level constraints prevent common terminal errors at compile time. An extinct operation without complete legacy accounting is rejected. A recover operation violating conservation constraints is rejected.

Terminal operations expressed in Lume grammar are subject to the same deterministic compilation guarantees as all other Lume constructs.

8.3 Runtime Compatibility
The Lume runtime provides native APIs for terminal operations. The Runtime.assessViability(), Runtime.prepareExtinction(), Runtime.executeExtinction(), and Runtime.executeRecovery() APIs manage the complete terminal lifecycle. These APIs are atomic operations that execute within metered contexts.

Runtime version management ensures identical terminal protocol implementations across all nodes.

The runtime enforces extinction boundary constraints natively at the bytecode interpreter level.

8.4 Canonicalization Rules
Canonicalization normalizes terminal representations to ensure identical hash values for semantically identical terminal states. Legacy inventories, redistribution plans, and extinction certificates are canonicalized using type-aware serialization.

Terminal-specific canonicalization extends to archival records, enabling deterministic posthumous queries against extinct organisms' histories.

Canonicalization rules are versioned and published in the Trust Layer governance registry.

9 Integration with Trust Layer
9.1 Certificate-Bound Extinction
Every extinction in D-OERP is bound to Trust Layer certificates. The extinction certificate seals the organism's certificate chain, creating a permanent archival record. Certificate binding enables extinction access control: only governance entities with valid credentials can authorize voluntary extinction.

The Trust Layer's certificate revocation mechanism does not apply to extinction certificates: extinction is permanent unless explicitly reversed through governance-authorized recovery, which creates a new certificate chain entry rather than revoking the extinction certificate.

The sealed certificate chain is committed to both the active ledger and the archival infrastructure.

9.2 Certificate-Bound Recovery
Every recovery operation is bound to Trust Layer certificates linked to the triggering extinction. Recovery certificates record the redistribution ledger, successor assignments, and affected organisms' post-recovery states.

Certificate binding ensures that recovery operations are traceable to governance authorization and cannot be performed without proper credentials.

Recovery certificates are appended to affected organisms' certificate chains, recording the recovery's impact on their states.

9.3 Identity Anchoring
Organism identity in D-OERP persists through extinction. The extinct organism's OrganismID, genesis certificate, and complete certificate chain are preserved in the archival infrastructure. Identity anchoring ensures that posthumous queries, forensic investigations, and historical analyses can reference the extinct organism by its original identity.

Identity anchoring through the D-OLP lifecycle framework [16] ensures that the extinction is recognized as the terminal lifecycle phase rather than identity erasure.

Recovered organisms (through governance-authorized resurrection) receive new OrganismIDs linked to the original through the recovery certificate.

9.4 Provenance and Auditability
The Trust Layer's provenance infrastructure records every terminal event, creating complete records of organism deaths and ecosystem recoveries. Auditors can reconstruct any extinct organism's complete lifecycle by querying the archival infrastructure.

Provenance data is protected by Merkle tree commitments recorded in the consensus ledger, preventing retroactive modification.

Terminal audit reports support regulatory compliance for lifecycle-sensitive deployments.

10 Integration with Lume-V
10.1 Envelope Constraints on Extinction
Lume-V execution envelopes [11] define behavioral boundaries for legacy code containers that undergo extinction. Extinction of Lume-V containers must respect both D-OERP terminal boundaries and Lume-V execution envelope constraints. Legacy code within Lume-V containers may implement shutdown patterns incompatible with D-OERP's determinism requirements.

The Lume-V terminal adapter wraps legacy shutdown operations in deterministic envelopes, satisfying the Liskov-Wing behavioral substitutability requirements [25].

The pre-computation approach from G-DRSP integration [14] extends to terminal operations.

10.2 Envelope Constraints on Recovery
Recovery involving Lume-V containers must account for legacy code initialization requirements. The Lume-V recovery adapter ensures that legacy code containers receiving redistributed resources are initialized deterministicly.

Post-recovery behavioral testing for Lume-V containers verifies that legacy code components accept redistributed resources correctly.

Safety boundaries for Lume-V recovery are stricter than for native Lume operations.

10.3 Intent Arbitration

13 Integration with SOR
13.1 Cell-Level Extinction
At the cell level, D-OERP governs programmed cell death (apoptosis) within SOR organisms [9]. Cell-level extinction corresponds to biological apoptosis: individual cells that fail to meet viability thresholds are terminated and their resources are recycled within the organism. Cell extinction is the finest-grained terminal tier, operating entirely within a single organism's execution partition.

Cell extinction is the cheapest terminal tier, requiring no consensus ordering because cell death is internal to the organism. However, cell extinctions are certified for auditability.

Extensive cell extinction within a single organism may indicate organism-level viability degradation, triggering escalated assessment.

13.2 Signal-Level Extinction
At the signal level, D-OERP governs communication pathway decommissioning. Signal-level extinction corresponds to synaptic pruning: unnecessary or degraded communication pathways are terminated and their bandwidth resources are recycled.

Signal extinction introduces cross-cell coordination requirements. When a pathway is decommissioned, both endpoint cells must acknowledge the termination simultaneously through D-COCP [15].

Signal extinctions affecting homeostatic communication paths are flagged for homeostasis-aware processing.

13.3 Homeostasis-Level Extinction
At the homeostasis level, D-OERP governs territorial withdrawal and regulatory retirement. Homeostasis-level extinction corresponds to niche abandonment: organisms that can no longer sustain homeostatic regulation within their territorial boundaries withdraw from those territories through D-OMSCP [18] and release their regulatory responsibilities.

Homeostasis-level extinction is critical because territorial vacancies affect neighboring organisms' stability. The D-OREP resource exchange framework [19] manages the redistribution of the retiring organism's resource commitments.

Homeostasis extinction transitions integrate with the behavioral homeostasis framework [3] to ensure surviving organisms' stability is maintained during the transition.

13.4 Organism-Level Extinction
At the organism level, D-OERP governs complete organism termination. Organism-level extinction is the most expensive terminal tier, requiring consensus ordering, multi-party validation, full certificate issuance, and comprehensive legacy redistribution.

Organism-level extinction coordinates with D-OLP [16] to execute the terminal lifecycle phase, with D-OMPP [17] to archive the organism's complete history, and with all dependent protocols to redistribute the organism's ecological footprint.

Organism-level extinction produces the sealed certificate chain and the extinction-recovery certificate pair.

14 Failure Modes in Extinction & Recovery
14.1 Extinction Collapse
Extinction collapse occurs when the extinction pipeline fails to complete terminal preparation within its boundary constraints. Collapse can result from legacy inventory deadlock, redistribution plan infeasibility, or governance authorization timeout.

The pipeline defends against collapse through fallback redistribution plans that direct all legacy resources to the ecosystem's unclaimed pool for subsequent governed distribution. If collapse occurs despite defensive measures, the organism's extinction is deferred to the next epoch with escalated governance attention.

Governance policies define maximum collapse rates for extinction operations.

14.2 Recovery Collapse
Recovery collapse occurs when the recovery pipeline fails to restore ecosystem stability within its temporal boundary. Collapse can result from insufficient successor capacity, redistribution conflicts that cannot be arbitrated, or post-recovery instability that persists beyond governance-defined tolerance windows.

The pipeline defends against recovery collapse through graduated recovery: partial restoration is committed immediately while remaining recovery actions are scheduled for subsequent epochs. The ZK-SRP framework [1] provides state reversal for recovery operations that produce instability.

Recovery collapse triggers governance escalation for manual intervention.

14.3 Certificate Mismatch
Certificate mismatch in terminal operations occurs when the extinction or recovery certificate contains metadata inconsistent with the actual terminal outcome. Resolution involves re-validating the terminal operation and either reissuing the certificate or reverting the operation.

Prevention strategies include proactive certificate renewal and pre-terminal certificate validity verification.

Terminal certificate mismatches are treated with higher severity than standard mismatches due to the irreversible nature of extinction.

14.4 Multi-Organism Terminal Conflict
Multi-organism terminal conflict occurs when concurrent extinctions produce incompatible legacy redistributions. Two extinct organisms' legacy plans may both target the same successor organism, exceeding its capacity.

The multi-organism extinction pipeline prevents conflicts through coordinated legacy planning that considers all concurrent extinctions collectively.

Conflict detection during validation identifies cases where concurrent redistributions produced mutually inconsistent outcomes.

14.5 Drift Amplification
Extinction and recovery operations can amplify existing state drift if legacy redistributions introduce perturbations that compound through feedback loops. The deterministic healing mechanisms [5] provide the systematic correction framework for terminal-amplified drift.

Post-recovery monitoring compares ecosystem health indices before and after terminal events.

Amplification-aware recovery applies smoothed redistribution that minimizes perturbation magnitude.

14.6 Intent Inversion
Intent inversion in terminal operations occurs when an extinction's declared intent produces opposite effects. An extinction declared as ecosystem optimization might, due to incorrect viability assessment, terminate a critical organism whose loss degrades ecosystem performance.

Detection requires post-extinction ecosystem assessment that compares actual performance with the performance predicted by the extinction intent.

Prevention includes intent-aware extinction validation with pre-commitment ecosystem impact simulation.

16 Security Analysis
16.1 Extinction Tampering Resistance
An adversary who can trigger unauthorized extinctions can destabilize the ecosystem by terminating critical organisms. D-OERP resists extinction tampering through governance-authorized terminal transitions with multi-signature approval, end-to-end extinction certification, and quorum-based validation.

Protocol integrity is maintained through compilation determinism and hash-locked implementation.

Viability assessment tampering resistance prevents adversaries from corrupting fitness evaluations to trigger false extinctions.

16.2 Recovery Tampering Resistance
An adversary who can manipulate recovery operations can redirect legacy resources to favored organisms or deny recovery to targeted organisms. D-OERP resists recovery tampering through certified redistribution plans, conservation-invariant verification, and fairness constraint enforcement.

Multi-party validation ensures that recovery plans satisfy governance-defined fairness requirements before execution.

Recovery tampering resistance is critical because legacy redistribution permanently alters the ecosystem's resource configuration.

16.3 Identity Forgery Resistance
An adversary who forges organism identities could claim another organism's legacy or prevent legitimate successors from receiving their assignments. D-OERP prevents identity forgery through the Trust Layer's certificate hierarchy with valid Ed25519 key pairs [24].

Archival identity verification ensures that posthumous queries return authentic historical data.

Legacy claim verification prevents unauthorized entities from accessing extinct organisms' resources.

16.4 Certificate Forgery Resistance
Terminal certificate integrity depends on the Trust Layer's Ed25519 signature infrastructure. Forging extinction or recovery certificates requires producing valid signatures from the Certificate Fabric's private key, which is computationally infeasible.

Certificate chain validation rejects certificates not correctly chained to existing histories.

The transparency log enables independent verification of terminal certificate legitimacy.

16.5 Governance Abuse Prevention
Governance abuse in terminal management involves authorized entities manipulating extinction policies to prematurely terminate organisms, suppress legitimate recovery, or redirect legacy resources. Prevention relies on multi-signature approval, public policy transparency, and anomaly detection.

Terminal governance decisions are recorded with full attribution in the governance log.

Systematic extinction patterns correlated with specific governance entities trigger investigation.

17 Performance Considerations
17.1 Extinction Overhead
Extinction operations consume computational resources for viability assessment, legacy inventory, redistribution planning, validation, and certification. Cell-level extinction consumes less than 0.5% overhead per operation. Signal-level extinction consumes 1–3%. Homeostasis-level extinction consumes 3–8%. Organism-level extinction consumes 10–30% depending on legacy scope. This overhead reflects the fundamental consistency-availability trade-off formalized by Brewer [26].

Aggregate extinction overhead depends on population turnover rate. Stable ecosystems with low extinction rates experience minimal overhead. High-turnover ecosystems may reach 15–25% aggregate terminal processing.

D-OERP optimizes overhead through pre-computed legacy inventories, redistribution template reuse, and batched certification.

17.2 Recovery Latency
Recovery latency measures the time between extinction and ecosystem restoration. Simple single-organism recovery completes within 10–50 processing steps. Multi-organism recovery following mass extinction may require 100–1000 steps depending on ecosystem scope and cascade depth.

Recovery latency directly affects ecosystem performance during the restoration window. The DAIGS cognitive engine minimizes latency through predictive recovery planning and pre-computed restoration templates.

Governance-configurable recovery timeout bounds prevent restoration from becoming an unbounded delay source.

17.3 Distributed Cognition Cost
DAIGS cognitive involvement in terminal decisions adds computational cost. Routine extinctions are handled by threshold-based assessment with zero cognitive overhead. Mass extinctions, contested terminations, and complex recovery scenarios are escalated to cognitive analysis.

The terminal pipeline implements an adaptive engagement policy matching terminal complexity to cognitive resources.

Total distributed cognition cost for terminal management is tracked and bounded by governance.

19 Conclusion
Extinction is the terminal foundation of ecosystem sustainability. Without deterministic extinction, non-viable organisms consume resources indefinitely, degrading ecosystem performance. Without deterministic legacy redistribution, extinct organisms' resources are orphaned, territories are vacated without governance, and communication channels are abandoned. Without deterministic recovery, surviving organisms cannot adapt to post-extinction conditions consistently. Terminal determinism is, in this sense, the layer that transforms individual organism lifecycle management into ecosystem-level population governance.

I have presented D-OERP, a complete extinction and recovery architecture that addresses these challenges through dual pipeline architectures, comprehensive legacy governance, and certificate-bound terminal accountability. The extinction pipeline's six stages (detection, arbitration, validation, synchronization, certification, multi-organism coordination) ensure governed, verified termination. The recovery pipeline's six stages (detection, reconstruction, arbitration, validation, certification, multi-organism coordination) ensure governed, verified restoration.

The integration with every major Lume ecosystem subsystem ensures that terminal management is pervasive and self-consistent. The Lume compiler provides deterministic compilation of terminal logic. The Trust Layer provides identity and provenance infrastructure, including permanent archival. DAIGS provides intelligent extinction reasoning and recovery optimization. LDIR provides multilingual terminal semantics. SOR provides the biological hierarchy within which terminal tiers operate. Lume-V provides legacy code accommodation. G-DRSP provides global synchronization. D-COCP provides terminal coordination communication. D-OLP provides lifecycle-aware terminal management. D-OMPP provides history archival. D-OMSCP provides territorial vacancy governance. D-OREP provides legacy resource redistribution. D-OCRP provides terminal conflict resolution. D-OEAP provides post-extinction adaptation. GUPAS provides governance oversight.

The security analysis demonstrates resistance to extinction tampering, recovery tampering, identity forgery, certificate forgery, and governance abuse. The performance analysis shows that terminal overhead is manageable through pre-computed inventories, redistribution templates, and batched certification. The failure mode analysis identifies six categories of terminal failure and provides detection and recovery mechanisms for each.

This work establishes what is, to my knowledge, the first complete extinction and recovery architecture for deterministic synthetic organisms. D-OERP transforms organism termination from an unverified deletion into a governed, certified, auditable terminal event whose legacy distribution and ecosystem impact are as verifiable and reproducible as the organisms themselves. Future work will extend the framework to cross-vertical unification, zero-knowledge terminal verification, autonomous resurrection, and federated global governance.

Appendix A — Definitions
A.1 Organism Extinction
The governed terminal transition of a synthetic organism, encompassing viability assessment, legacy inventorying, resource redistribution, responsibility transfer, communication decommissioning, and certificate chain sealing. Organism extinction preserves identity and history in archival infrastructure while terminating active operation.

A.2 Organism Recovery
The governed restoration of ecosystem stability following an extinction event. Recovery encompasses resource redistribution to surviving organisms, successor assignment for transferred responsibilities, communication channel repair, and fitness recalibration.

A.3 Extinction Certificate
The organism's final Trust Layer certificate, recording the terminal transition's parameters, viability assessment, legacy inventory, redistribution summary, and governance authorization. The extinction certificate seals the organism's certificate chain.

A.4 Recovery Certificate
A Trust Layer certificate recording a completed recovery operation, linked to the triggering extinction certificate. Contains the redistribution ledger, successor assignments, affected organisms' post-recovery state hashes, and governance authorization.

A.5 Legacy Inventory
A complete accounting of an extinct organism's assets at the moment of termination: resource holdings, territorial boundaries, communication channels, governance responsibilities, evolutionary history, and archival data.

A.6 Successor Assignment
A governance-authorized transfer of an extinct organism's responsibilities to a surviving organism. Successor assignments are computed deterministically based on proximity, capability, capacity, and governance priority.

A.7 Mass Extinction
A coordinated terminal event where a significant fraction of the organism population terminates simultaneously. Mass extinction requires coordinated legacy planning to prevent cascade failures and ecosystem collapse.

A.8 Terminal Determinism
The property that all nodes compute identical extinction decisions, identical legacy redistributions, and identical recovery outcomes for identical ecosystem states. Terminal determinism requires deterministic viability assessment, consensus-ordered extinction processing, and certificate-bound terminal verification.

B.1 Deterministic Viability Assessment Algorithm
Algorithm DetAssessViability:
Input: OrganismID, ViabilityMetrics
Output: ViabilityStatus (VIABLE, AT_RISK, EXTINCT)

 1: fitness ← Organism(OrganismID).FitnessVector
 2: resources ← Organism(OrganismID).ResourceHoldings
 3: compliance ← Organism(OrganismID).GovernanceCompliance
 4: contribution ← Organism(OrganismID).EcosystemContribution
 5: IF fitness < ViabilityMetrics.MinFitness
 6:    AND resources < ViabilityMetrics.MinResources:
 7:   IF ConsecutiveEpochsBelowThreshold >= ViabilityMetrics.ExtinctionDelay:
 8:     RETURN EXTINCT
 9:   ELSE:
10:     RETURN AT_RISK
11: RETURN VIABLE
B.2 Deterministic Extinction Algorithm
Algorithm DetExtinct:
Input: OrganismID, GovernanceAuth
Output: ExtinctionCertificate

 1: VERIFY GovernanceAuth.Valid()
 2: VERIFY Organism(OrganismID).Viability == EXTINCT
 3: legacy ← ComputeLegacyInventory(OrganismID)
 4: plan ← ComputeRedistributionPlan(legacy, GovernanceAuth)
 5: VERIFY plan.SatisfiesConservation()
 6: VERIFY plan.SatisfiesFairness(GovernanceAuth.FairnessBounds)
 7: finalHash ← SHA3-256(Canonicalize(Organism(OrganismID).State))
 8: ExecuteRedistribution(plan)
 9: ArchiveOrganism(OrganismID)
10: proof ← {ExtinctionID, finalHash, legacy, plan, MerklePath}
11: cert ← MintExtinctionCertificate(proof, GovernanceAuth)
12: SealCertificateChain(OrganismID, cert)
13: RETURN cert
B.3 Deterministic Recovery Algorithm
Algorithm DetRecover:
Input: ExtinctionID, GovernanceAuth
Output: RecoveryCertificate

 1: VERIFY GovernanceAuth.Valid()
 2: damage ← AssessDamage(ExtinctionID)
 3: plan ← ComputeRecoveryPlan(damage, GovernanceAuth)
 4: VERIFY plan.SatisfiesConservation()
 5: VERIFY plan.SatisfiesStability()
 6: preHash ← SHA3-256(Canonicalize(EcosystemState))
 7: ExecuteRecovery(plan)
 8: postHash ← SHA3-256(Canonicalize(EcosystemState))
 9: VERIFY StabilityAnalysis(AffectedOrganisms).Stable()
10: proof ← {RecoveryID, ExtinctionID, preHash, postHash, plan}
11: cert ← MintRecoveryCertificate(proof, GovernanceAuth)
12: LinkToExtinctionCertificate(cert, ExtinctionID)
13: RETURN cert
B.4 Mass Extinction Coordination Algorithm
Algorithm DetMassExtinction:
Input: ExtinctionSet, GovernanceAuth
Output: MassExtinctionCertificate

 1: depGraph ← BuildExtinctionDependencyGraph(ExtinctionSet)
 2: IF depGraph.HasCycle():
 3:   BreakCycleByGovernancePriority(depGraph)

 6:   cert ← DetExtinct(organism.ID, GovernanceAuth)

Algorithm DetTerminalSync:
Input: EpochTerminalSet
Output: TerminalSyncProof

Appendix D — Implementation Notes
D.1 Legacy Inventory Computation
Legacy inventory uses a hierarchical scanning algorithm that enumerates the organism's assets tier-by-tier: cell resources, signal pathway configurations, homeostatic territorial holdings, and cognitive knowledge representations. The scan produces a deterministic inventory indexed by (asset-type, asset-id, quantity, provenance-hash) tuples. Inventory computation time is O(n) where n is the organism's total asset count.

D.2 Redistribution Optimization
The redistribution planner uses a deterministic assignment algorithm that matches legacy assets to recipients based on proximity, capacity, compatibility, and governance priority. When multiple recipients have equal suitability, the algorithm selects the recipient with the lexicographically smallest OrganismID. The algorithm's time complexity is O(a × r) where a is the asset count and r is the candidate recipient count.

D.3 Archival Storage
Extinct organisms' archival data is stored in append-only archival partitions indexed by OrganismID and ExtinctionID. Archival data includes the complete certificate chain, evolutionary trajectory, conflict record, resource accounting history, and final state snapshot. Archival queries support time-travel access to any historical state through the D-OMPP framework [17].

D.4 Recovery Template Library
Pre-computed recovery templates for common extinction patterns (single-organism termination, paired-organism co-extinction, mass extinction with cascade recovery) are stored in the governance registry. Template instantiation reduces per-recovery planning computation for routine terminal events.

D.5 Cascade Detection
Cascade detection monitors surviving organisms' viability indicators following extinction events. If an extinction causes dependent organisms to cross viability thresholds, the cascade detector identifies the secondary extinction risk and triggers preemptive intervention through D-OEAP adaptation [21] or governance-assisted resource supplementation through D-OREP [19].

Appendix E — Governance & Compliance
E.1 Terminal Authorization
Terminal operations are authorized through a tiered model. Level 1 (cell-level apoptosis within organism boundaries) requires no external approval. Level 2 (signal-level decommissioning and routine organism retirement) requires automated governance approval through GUPAS [10]. Level 3 (homeostasis-affecting extinctions and contested terminations) requires multi-signature governance approval. Level 4 (mass extinction events and governance-directed population restructuring) requires executive governance authority.

The tiered model ensures routine terminal operations proceed without governance bottlenecks while maintaining oversight for ecosystem-affecting extinctions.

E.2 Compliance Reporting
The terminal pipeline generates compliance reports summarizing extinction and recovery activity. Reports include extinction frequency, legacy redistribution equity, recovery latency statistics, and cascade occurrence rates.

Reports are published to the governance transparency log and align with lifecycle governance regulations.

E.3 Escalation Protocols
Escalation protocols define graduated responses for terminal anomalies. Unexpected extinction patterns trigger investigation. Recovery failures trigger governance escalation. Mass extinction indicators trigger ecosystem-wide emergency governance review.

E.4 Privacy Protections
Terminal privacy protections ensure that extinct organisms' archival data is accessed only through governance-authorized queries. Governance authorities can audit terminal metadata without accessing detailed organism histories. Full archival access requires elevated authorization.

Privacy boundaries are enforced through per-query access control with multi-signature requirements for sensitive historical data.

Appendix G — Threat Models
G.1 Assassination Attack
An adversary triggers unauthorized extinction of a critical organism to destabilize the ecosystem. The defense relies on multi-signature governance authorization for all non-automatic extinctions, viability assessment integrity verification, and mandatory contestation windows that allow targeted organisms to challenge the extinction decision.

G.2 Legacy Theft Attack
An adversary manipulates redistribution planning to redirect an extinct organism's legacy to unauthorized recipients. The defense relies on conservation-invariant verification, fairness constraint enforcement, and post-redistribution auditing that compares actual distribution with governance-authorized plans.

G.3 Cascade Injection Attack
An adversary triggers a single extinction designed to cascade through the dependency graph, causing mass extinction of dependent organisms. The defense relies on cascade depth limits, dependency analysis before extinction commitment, and mandatory governance review when cascade indicators exceed thresholds.

G.4 Recovery Denial Attack
An adversary disrupts recovery operations to prevent ecosystem restoration following extinction. The defense relies on redundant recovery planning, governance-escalated manual recovery, and emergency recovery governance that bypasses normal approval requirements during ecosystem emergencies.

G.5 Resurrection Fraud Attack
An adversary creates a fraudulent organism claiming to be a resurrected version of an extinct organism, attempting to claim the extinct organism's legacy or historical privileges. The defense relies on archival identity verification against the Trust Layer's extinction registry and certificate chain validation that rejects organisms whose genesis certificates do not match the archival record.

H.2 Extinction Determinism Invariant
For any extinction assessment at deterministic time t: ∀ nodes n_a, n_b: AssessViability(O, t)_{n_a} = AssessViability(O, t)_{n_b}. All nodes produce identical viability assessments.

H.3 Legacy Conservation Invariant
For extinction of organism O with legacy L: Σ_{i=1}^{k} Redistributed(O_i) + Unclaimed = |L|. The total redistributed legacy plus any unclaimed remainder equals the exact legacy inventory. No resources are created or destroyed during extinction.

H.4 Recovery Determinism Invariant
For any recovery operation R triggered by extinction X: ∀ nodes n_a, n_b: ExecuteRecovery(R, X)_{n_a} = ExecuteRecovery(R, X)_{n_b}. All nodes produce identical recovery outcomes.

H.5 Identity Persistence
For extinct organism O: ∀ t > t_extinction: Archive(O).OrganismID = O.OrganismID ∧ Archive(O).CertificateChain = O.CertificateChain(t_extinction). The extinct organism's identity and history persist in the archival infrastructure indefinitely.

H.6 Irreversibility
For committed extinction of organism O: ∀ t > t_extinction: O.Status = EXTINCT unless GovernanceAuthorizedResurrection(O, t) = true. Extinction is permanent without explicit governance-authorized recovery.

H.7 Cascade Depth Bound
For extinction cascade chain X_1 → X_2 → ... → X_n: n ≤ MaxCascadeDepth where MaxCascadeDepth is governance-defined. Cascades exceeding depth bounds require governance approval.

H.8 Recovery Stability
For recovery R affecting organisms {O_1, ..., O_k}: ∀ i: StabilityAnalysis(O_i, t_post_recovery) = STABLE. All affected organisms must maintain homeostatic stability after recovery completion.
